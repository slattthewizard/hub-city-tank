# Oil Tank Inspection for Insurance: What Gets Checked

An oil tank inspection for insurance is a written record, not just a look. When an insurer, a buyer's insurer, or a home inspector asks for one, they want specific facts on paper: the tank's age, its construction, its outlet position, its stand, and a list of any rust, wet spots, or weeping seams. That is a different exercise from the ten-minute walkaround you can do yourself with a flashlight, and it's worth knowing the difference before the letter or the closing date arrives.

This guide covers what a professional inspection records, how it differs from your own once-over, what happens after it's done, and why there is no single set price for one. ## What Triggers an Oil Tank Inspection

A handful of situations bring a professional oil tank inspection into the picture, and they usually arrive with a deadline attached.

The most common is an insurance renewal questionnaire. Insurers ask about tank age and condition on a regular schedule, and once a tank crosses a certain age, some send a letter asking for an inspection or a replacement before they will renew. We cover the age side of that in detail in our guide to [oil tank age and insurance limits](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/).

Buying or selling a home with oil heat is the other big trigger. A buyer usually can't get home insurance bound until the insurer knows the tank's age, material, and condition, and without insurance the mortgage doesn't close. A real estate deal can stall on exactly that question. Our post on [buying a house with an oil tank](/blog/buying-house-with-oil-tank/) walks through that side from the buyer's chair.

Less often, an inspection gets triggered by something more immediate: a delivery driver who hesitates at a leaning tank, a home inspector who flags a rusted seam during a general house inspection, or storm or ice damage that leaves visible dents. Any of those is reason enough to get a proper look before you're forced into one.

## Your Own Walkaround vs a Professional Oil Tank Inspection

There is a real difference between checking your own tank and having it professionally inspected, and it's not just about who holds the flashlight.

Our [10-minute DIY oil tank inspection checklist](/blog/oil-tank-inspection-checklist/) is built for exactly what it sounds like: a homeowner catching obvious red flags twice a year, things like a stuck gauge, a leaning leg, or a damp patch under the filter. It's genuinely useful, and it catches most of the problems that turn into expensive ones if ignored.

A professional oil tank inspection for insurance is a different document. It's a written record from an oil burner technician that follows a standard set of categories rather than a homeowner's impressions, and it's meant to be handed to a broker, an insurer, or a buyer's lawyer as proof of what was found. It doesn't replace your own walkaround. Think of your own check as the thing you do every spring and fall, and the professional inspection as the thing you get when someone official needs it in writing.

## The Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire: What Insurers Ask

The Centre for Study of Insurance Operations (CSIO), a Canadian insurance industry body, publishes a national [Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire) for personal property policies. Many brokers and insurers use it, and others use their own version with similar questions. Either way, the categories on that form are a good preview of what any professional inspection will cover. What follows describes the questions on the form. They are things an underwriter wants answered, not a list of legal requirements.

The form starts with installation basics: who installed the tank, whether the installer was a certified oil burner technician, the installation date, and whether the tank was new or used when it went in. From there it moves into the tank itself: its status and usage, construction and fuel type, whether it sits indoors or outdoors, its support and stand type, the certification label, capacity, year manufactured, manufacturer, and even the wall thickness of the shell, head, and bottom.

It also asks which safety features are present, things like an anti-siphon device, a concrete dam, a drip leg, a leak detection alarm, a level alarm, a vent alarm or whistle, and a release barrier such as a drip tray under the tank. Then it lists tank condition items to check off directly: leaks, drips, spills, fumes, odours, rust, corrosion, weeping, wet spots, and dents. Any one of those boxes checked is the kind of thing that gets a follow-up call from an underwriter.

The supply line gets its own set of questions too: whether the fuel line is looped or the tank has a swing joint, where the outlet and filter sit, what material the line and the fill and vent pipes are made of, whether the fill and vent pipes are capped, whether there is a shut-off valve on the supply line at the burner, and whether the tank, line and valve are protected from impact by vehicles or ice. The form also asks whether the tank is inspected or serviced every year by a certified oil burner technician, and whether the vent pipe sits at least 15 centimetres (6 inches) above the fill pipe. That last one is the kind of granular check a professional inspection catches and a casual look usually misses.

Finally, it records clearances. For an indoor tank, that means the shortest distance to the back wall, side wall, corner, floor, floor drain or sump, and the fuel-fired appliance itself, plus whether the tank is filled and vented outside. For an outdoor tank, it means distances to the nearest open water, drilled well, drainage ditch, storm drain, manhole cover, septic bed, property line, window, door, and air intake vent. Many of those distances matter because the installation code for oil-burning equipment, CSA B139, sets minimum clearances for how a tank gets installed in the first place. We cover the code itself in our guide to [CSA B139 oil tank rules](/blog/csa-b139-oil-tank-code/). The current edition, [CSA B139 Series:24](https://www.csagroup.org/store/product/2702055/), was published in 2024 and includes a part written for residential and small commercial buildings.

## What Gets Recorded, and Why Your Insurer Cares

| What's recorded | Why it matters to your insurer |
|---|---|
| Label, year made, manufacturer | Confirms real age instead of a guess |
| Construction and wall type | A single-wall steel tank has no second barrier if the shell rusts through |
| Location, support, and stand | A tank on soft ground can tip; one crowding the furnace fails a clearance check |
| Supply line, outlet, and filter | Water and sludge collect differently depending on outlet position |
| Vent, fill, and whistle | A blocked or silent vent is an overfill risk during delivery |
| Condition flags (rust, wet spots, weeping) | Any checked box can trigger a decline or a repair demand |
| Clearances, indoor or outdoor | Distances to windows, wells, doors, and property lines |

Nothing on that list is exotic. It's the same handful of things an oil burner technician looks at on any tank, written down in a standard order so an insurer who has never seen your basement can still make a decision from the paperwork.

## Tag, Age, and the Label: The Detail That Carries the Most Weight

Of everything on that form, the manufacturer's label does the most work. It's the one field that turns "the tank looks fine to me" into a documented fact an insurer will accept.

Most residential tanks carry a metal plate or sticker, usually near the top or end of the tank, showing a manufacture date and often a serial number. A legible label closes the age question in seconds. A rusted-off or missing label does the opposite: insurers generally treat an unprovable age as an old tank, which tends to trigger the same response as a genuinely old one. ## What "Passing" an Oil Tank Inspection Means

There is no pass or fail stamp on an oil tank inspection the way there is on, say, a vehicle safety check. What you get instead is a written report, and what happens next depends on what it says.

A clean report, meaning no condition flags, a tank within the age range your insurer accepts, and clearances and safety features that check out, usually means a smooth renewal or a straightforward closing. That report becomes the document your broker or the buyer's lawyer keeps on file.

A report with flagged items is where things branch. A minor issue, like a missing vent whistle or an unprotected line, can sometimes be corrected without replacing the whole tank. A tank with no readable certification label, heavy corrosion, or an age past that insurer's limit usually has only one real fix. Either way, the report tells you which situation you're in, and it's far better to see that in writing on your own schedule than in a decline letter with a deadline attached.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does a professional oil tank inspection for insurance check?

It records the same categories used on the industry fuel oil tank questionnaire: the tank's age, manufacturer, and construction, its indoor or outdoor location and stand type, safety features like a vent whistle or leak alarm, visible condition issues such as rust or wet spots, the supply line and filter setup, and clearances to walls, windows, wells, and property lines.

### Is the 10-minute checklist the same as an insurance inspection?

No. The 10-minute checklist is a homeowner's own walkaround meant to catch obvious red flags between professional visits. An insurance inspection is a written record, made by a technician and covering set categories, that a broker, insurer, or buyer's lawyer can rely on. It carries more weight than a homeowner's impression alone.

### How much does an oil tank inspection cost?

There is no single published price, since it depends on who performs it and what is required: a basic visual check versus a scan or soil test. We don't charge for a written estimate visit to look at your tank and discuss replacement, though that is a different service from a formal insurer-required inspection report.

### What happens if my tank fails the inspection?

There is no formal pass or fail grade, just a written report. Minor findings, like a missing vent whistle, can sometimes be fixed without replacing the tank. A tank with no readable label, heavy corrosion, or an age past your insurer's limit usually leaves replacement as the practical option, ideally scheduled on your own timeline rather than an underwriter's.

### Do I need a professional inspection to sell a house with an oil tank?

Most buyers' insurers will ask about the tank's age and condition before binding coverage, and the lender needs that coverage in place, which in practice means someone has to produce a documented answer. As a seller, having the tank's label photos, age and any recent inspection or replacement paperwork ready before listing keeps that question from stalling the deal.

### Does New Brunswick require oil tank inspections by law?

New Brunswick's [Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation](https://laws.gnb.ca/en/showdoc/cr/87-97) is aimed at larger storage: the province's [petroleum storage tanks](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg/environment/content/land_waste/content/reference_manual/petroleum_storagetanks.html) page ties registration and installation approval to sites holding 2,000 litres or more, well above a typical single 910 litre home tank. For most homeowners, the inspection requests they actually face come from an insurer or a buyer, so ask your broker exactly what they need.
