# Oil Tank Cleaning: What Gets Done About Water and Sludge, and When to Stop

"Oil tank cleaning" covers a few different jobs, and which one you need depends on what's really wrong. A filter change isn't the same as a pump-out, and pumping off water and sludge isn't the same as polishing the fuel. None of it opens up the tank shell and scrubs it, because that isn't how a residential heating oil tank gets serviced.

The short version: cleaning helps when the tank itself is sound and something got into the fuel: water from condensation, sludge stirred up by a delivery, or contamination from a bad fill. It stops helping once the tank is old enough that the sludge is a symptom of the steel failing, not the cause of your furnace trouble. At that point you're paying to clean a tank you're about to replace anyway.

## What People Mean by "Oil Tank Cleaning"

There's no single service called "tank cleaning" the way there's a car wash for a car. When someone around Moncton, Dieppe or Riverview asks for one, they usually mean one of these:

**Filter and line service.** The canister filter at the tank outlet gets swapped, and the fuel line and nozzle get checked. This deals with symptoms at the burner end, not with what's sitting in the tank.

**Water and sludge pump-out.** A technician draws water and loose sludge off the tank floor through the fill pipe or the drain, if the drain is in safe condition to use. The fuel and residue that come out are handled as petroleum waste, not poured down a drain or onto the ground.

**Fuel polishing.** A step further than a plain pump-out: the fuel gets pumped out, run through filtration to remove contamination, and returned to the tank. This is worthwhile only when the tank itself, not just the fuel, is in decent shape.

What none of these do is get inside the tank and clean the steel. A residential tank isn't designed to be opened, and cutting into one that has held fuel oil is a genuine hazard, not a maintenance step. ## When a Cleaning Actually Solves the Problem

Cleaning is the right call when the tank shell is sound and the problem is what's floating around inside it, not the steel itself.

That covers a specific set of situations:

- **A recent delivery stirred up sediment.** A fill churns whatever has settled on the tank floor, and if the furnace acts up in the day or two after, a new filter and nozzle often sort it out once things settle. If it keeps happening, pumping the bottom off is the next step.
- **A water test came back positive.** If a water-finding paste test shows more than a trace on the stick, pumping it off the floor now is far cheaper than waiting for it to reach the outlet and the filter.
- **You bought fuel from a source you don't normally use and it's caused trouble.** Contaminated fuel is rare with reputable suppliers, but it happens, and polishing can salvage what's already in the tank.
- **The tank is younger and otherwise healthy.** A tank under 15 years old with a first-time fouling issue is a good candidate for a cleanout rather than a swap.

In all of these cases, the tank isn't the problem, what's inside it is. Get it cleaned, fix whatever let water in to begin with (a loose or missing fill cap, or a vent cap that lets rain in), and a sound tank can keep going.

## When Cleaning Is Money Spent on a Tank That Should Be Replaced

Here's the harder case, and it's the one that costs homeowners the most when they get it wrong. If sludge and water keep coming back, the tank itself is usually the reason, not bad luck with fuel deliveries.

Steel tanks corrode from the inside, at the bottom, in the one spot nobody can see without opening it up. Water sitting on the floor is most often what starts that corrosion, and the same water that causes sludge is quietly pitting the steel underneath it. A pump-out removes the water and the sludge. It does nothing about the pitting that's already there, because there's no way to clean out damage that's happened to the metal itself.

A few signs point toward "replace" rather than "clean again":

- **The tank has fouled more than once.** One bad winter is bad luck. A repeat pattern, especially two or three filter clogs or lockouts within a year, usually means the tank is generating its own contamination faster than a cleanout can keep up.
- **The tank is past 15 to 20 years old.** Life expectancy varies with installation quality, maintenance, and whether the tank sits indoors or outdoors, according to [Granby Industries' own guidance on tank service life](https://www.granbyindustries.com/en-us/petroleum-tanks/questions-and-answers/), but by that age range, a lot of steel tanks in our climate are working on borrowed time regardless of how clean the fuel inside them is.
- **There's visible rust, pitting, or weeping at the seams**, not just contamination in the fuel. Our guide to [how long oil tanks last](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) breaks down typical lifespans by tank type and location.
- **The tank sits outdoors near the coast.** Salt air along the Northumberland Strait accelerates corrosion on outdoor tanks around Shediac faster than it does further inland, which shortens the window where cleaning still makes sense.

If any of that describes your tank, a pump-out treats the symptom and the pitting keeps going. ## Filter Service, Pump-Out, and Fuel Polishing: What Each One Targets

These three jobs get lumped together as "cleaning," but they solve different problems and none of them substitutes for the others.

| Job | What it targets | When it's the right fix | What it won't fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Filter and line service | Fuel flow to the burner | A clog after a delivery, or routine annual service | Water or sludge still sitting in the tank |
| Water and sludge pump-out | Contamination on the tank floor | A positive water test, or repeated fouling in a sound tank | Corrosion or pitting already in the steel |
| Fuel polishing | Contaminated fuel throughout the tank | Bad fuel in an otherwise healthy tank | A tank that's fouling because it's failing |
| Tank replacement | The tank itself | Repeat fouling, tank past 15 to 20 years, visible pitting | Nothing, this is the fix once the tank is the problem |

None of these has a fixed price, because the right one for your tank depends on how much oil is in it, how the tank is set up, and how bad the contamination is. Every one of them gets quoted after someone looks at the tank in person, not off a generic price list, which is a different situation from the flat ranges published for removal and replacement work.

## What Happens During a Professional Pump-Out or Polishing Job

A straightforward cleanout follows roughly the same steps whether it's a first-time fouling issue or part of a bigger job:

1. **The tank gets checked first.** A sounding stick or water-finding paste identifies how much water and sludge are sitting on the floor, and a visual check for rust or weeping tells the tech whether cleaning is even the right move.
2. **Fuel and contamination are pumped out.** A suction wand through the fill pipe, or the drain valve if it's usable, draws off the fuel along with the water and sludge sitting underneath it.
3. **The fuel gets filtered, if it's a polishing job.** Clean fuel goes back into the tank rather than being replaced entirely.
4. **The filter and any affected fittings get checked.** If sludge reached the outlet, the filter that caught it usually needs changing too.
5. **Everything removed is handled as petroleum waste.** It doesn't go down a drain, into a floor sump, or onto the ground; it's transported and disposed of the way regulated waste has to be.

A yearly look is more than a good habit. Granby's FAQ (linked above) says all tanks should be inspected and serviced annually, and [CSA Group's summary of the current CSA B139 edition](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/), the installation code for oil-burning equipment in Canada, lists an annual visual inspection for leaks in fuel oil tanks, tubing, piping and filters as a new requirement. [Natural Resources Canada suggests booking the heating system check-up in the fall](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/stories/spotlight-energy-efficiency/guide-maintaining-your-home-heating-cooling-system), and that's also when a technician is most likely to catch a water or sludge problem before a cold snap turns it into a no-heat call.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does cleaning an oil tank stop it from rusting?

No. A pump-out or polishing job removes water, sludge and contamination from inside the tank, but it can't reverse corrosion that has already started eating into the steel. If the tank floor is already pitted, cleaning buys time at best. It doesn't repair the metal.

### How much does oil tank cleaning cost in the Moncton area?

There's no published flat rate, because the job depends on how much water or sludge is down there, how much fuel is in the tank, and how the tank is set up. It's quoted after someone looks at the tank in person, case by case, rather than off a price list. Ask for a written estimate before any work starts.

### How often does a home oil tank need to be cleaned?

There's no fixed cleaning schedule. What should happen every year is an inspection and service: Granby recommends it for all its tanks, and the current CSA B139 edition requires an annual visual check for leaks. A pump-out or polishing job is triggered by a real problem: a positive water test, repeated filter clogs, or trouble after a delivery. A healthy tank may never need one.

### Can I clean my own oil tank?

Testing for water with paste on a stick is a safe DIY step. Pumping fuel, water or sludge out of the tank is not. The vapour inside a tank that has held oil is flammable, the residue is regulated petroleum waste that can't go in household garbage or down a drain, and a mishandled pump-out can turn into a spill on your own property. Leave the pumping to someone equipped for it.

### What happens to the sludge and water once it's pumped out?

It's treated as petroleum waste, not household waste. It gets transported and disposed of properly rather than dumped, which is also why a homeowner shouldn't try to drain a tank into a pail or onto the ground. Ask whoever does the work to confirm disposal is included before they start.

### Should I clean my oil tank or just replace it?

It depends on whether the tank itself is sound. A tank under 15 years old with a one-time fouling problem is usually worth cleaning. A tank that keeps fouling, shows rust or pitting, or is already past 15 to 20 years old is often past the point where cleaning makes financial sense, since you'd be paying to service a tank you'll likely replace within a year or two anyway.
