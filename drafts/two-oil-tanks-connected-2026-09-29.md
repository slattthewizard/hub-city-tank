# Two Oil Tanks Connected Together: What a Tandem Setup Means for You

Some older homes around Moncton, Riverview, and out toward Salisbury and Memramcook heat with two oil tanks piped together instead of one. It's often left over from a bigger farmhouse, an addition that added heating load, or a time when adding a second tank was easier than swapping to one larger tank. The two tanks are meant to work as one bigger tank, sharing a fill line, a vent, a fuel supply line, or all three.

The trouble is that a tandem setup rarely ages evenly. The levels drift apart, the gauge stops meaning much, and by the time an insurance letter arrives you're dealing with two old tanks instead of one. This guide covers how these setups are plumbed, why they cause trouble, and what the fix usually looks like when replacement time comes.

Roth's [installation manual](https://www.roth-america.com/app/uploads/2021/02/DWT_Installation_Manual_2023.pdf) gives the other common reason: several smaller tanks can go where one large tank won't fit, or where the path in is too tight for a bigger tank.

Either way, once two tanks feed the same burner, someone has to decide how they're connected. That decision is where most of the long-term headaches start.

## How a Tandem Oil Tank Setup Is Plumbed

There are three ways two tanks end up sharing a system, and the differences matter.

### The engineered manifold (Roth's approach)

Roth's manual gives instructions for its double-wall tanks that cover indoor multiple-tank installs. Its expansion kits let several tanks fill from one fill pipe and vent through one vent, with reducers in each fill tee so oil flows into every tank at the same time. The manual adds conditions: the delivery truck has to pump within a set flow range or the tank nearest the fill point fills first, each tank still needs its own vent alarm, and the kits are for indoor installs only. Outdoors, each Roth tank gets its own fill and vent.

On the supply side, Roth uses what it calls an "equal manifold": equal-length piping from each tank to a common tee, so the burner draws from every tank at the same rate. The manual says the connected piping also lets the levels even out after the burner shuts off, as oil siphons from one tank to the other. It's explicit about one rule: tanks sharing a common fill or supply line must be the same model, at the same height, level and plumb, because different models can't be manifolded without a risk of uneven filling or draining.

### Fully independent tanks (Granby's approach for its 2-in-1)

Granby takes a different line for its 2-in-1 double-wall tanks. Section 3.8 of its [installer instructions](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0018_Ga-Granby-2-in-1-Installer-Instructions-1.pdf) says that where several of these tanks are installed, each one must be installed and piped independently as a single tank, cascade filling and combined venting are prohibited, and the total capacity must stay within what the applicable codes allow.

So there's no single rule for every tank. What one manufacturer allows for its product, another rules out for its own, and the right setup depends on the tanks actually in your basement or yard.

### The field-built crossover

Then there's what many older farmhouses have: an old steel tank piped to a second steel tank by whoever did the work at the time, sometimes with a crossover line between the two and only one vent alarm. That kind of setup predates either manufacturer's current instructions, and it's the one most likely to drift out of level, collect sludge unevenly and confuse a gauge.

All of this sits inside [CSA B139](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/), the Canadian installation code for oil-burning equipment. Roth's manual cites a clause of the code that caps the combined capacity of tanks inside a building at 1,100 imperial gallons (about 5,000 litres), including tanks on the lowest storey, and warns that local rules may be stricter. Few houses come near that, but it shows that "just add another tank" was never a free pass.

## Why the Levels Drift and the Gauge Stops Making Sense

If your two tanks were set up as a matched, engineered manifold, the levels should track each other closely. Older field-built connections are another story, and a few things throw them out of step.

Uneven draw is the first. If the supply piping wasn't balanced when it went in, or one tank now sits lower than the other on a settled floor or a farmhouse foundation that's moved over the decades, oil comes out faster from whichever tank has the easier path to the burner. Roth's own troubleshooting list for uneven levels points at the same suspects: connections, supply piping, blocked fill or vent piping, and the delivery rate.

Sludge is the second. An old steel tank collects rust flakes, water and sediment at the bottom, and that buildup takes up room the oil used to have. Two tanks of the same rated size can hold noticeably different amounts of usable oil once one has years more sludge than the other. Our post on [oil tank sludge](/blog/oil-tank-sludge/) covers what that buildup looks like and what it does to a burner.

The gauge makes it all harder to read. A tandem setup often has only one working gauge, on one tank, and it can only report that tank's level. If the other tank is fuller or emptier, you're judging your oil supply on half the picture. Add a gauge that's already sticking, which is common at this age, and "half a tank" can turn into a cold furnace with little warning. Our guide to a [gauge that isn't working](/blog/oil-tank-gauge-not-working/) covers the stick test, which is the reliable way to check what's in each tank.

## What a Tandem Setup Looks Like to an Insurer or Inspector

An insurer or home inspector looking at a tandem system doesn't see one heating system. They see two tanks, each with its own age, its own wall condition and its own chance of being the first to fail. Two old tanks mean two dates to track, and it only takes the older one to trigger a renewal letter or a buyer's question.

The piping is part of that picture. A supply line that has fed two tanks for decades has more fittings and joints than a single-tank line of the same age. ## The Usual Fix at Replacement: One Tank Instead of Two

When a tandem setup comes up for replacement, the common answer is to take out both old tanks and put in a single tank sized for the house's real oil use. One tank is simpler to inspect and simpler to gauge, and it gives an insurer one age and one wall type to look at instead of two.

Sizing it is the same job as sizing any single tank: work from the household's actual yearly oil use rather than adding the two old tanks' rated sizes together. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it against code to have two oil tanks piped together?

Not automatically. Roth's installation manual describes multiple-tank installs that meet CSA B139, and it cites a code cap on the combined capacity of tanks in a building. The tank makers add their own rules: Roth allows a shared fill and vent indoors with its kits, while Granby requires each of its 2-in-1 tanks to be piped on its own. An old field-built crossover may meet neither.

### Why do my two oil tanks never read the same level?

Usually because the piping was never built to draw evenly, because one tank now sits lower than the other after years of settling, or because sludge has taken up more room in one tank than the other. A single gauge on one tank also can't tell you what's in the tank it isn't mounted on. The stick test is the way to check both.

### Can I remove one tank and just keep the other running?

Sometimes, if the tank staying behind is sound, re-plumbed as a stand-alone system to its maker's instructions, and large enough to carry the house through winter on its own. It won't help with insurance if the tank you keep is the old one. The tank that stays has to stand on its own age and condition.

### Does having two old oil tanks make home insurance harder?

It can, because an insurer is looking at two tanks instead of one, each with its own age and condition. In practice the older of the two tends to set the pace for the whole system, since it's the one most likely to trigger a renewal question first. Ask your broker what they need to see for each tank.

### What does it cost to replace two connected oil tanks with one?

Using our published ranges, a full swap (one old tank out, the new tank in) runs $3,500 to $6,400, plus a separate removal for the second old tank: $500 to $900 outdoors or $700 to $1,100 indoors. A new fibreglass tank installed on its own runs $4,500 to $5,500, against $3,000 to $4,500 for steel.

### Can two different tank brands or sizes be piped together as one system?

Roth's manual says tanks sharing a common fill or supply line must be the same model, at the same height, level and plumb, or they risk filling or draining unevenly. Granby's 2-in-1 instructions go further and don't allow a shared fill or vent at all. A tandem setup built from two mismatched tanks is worth having someone look at as a whole system.
