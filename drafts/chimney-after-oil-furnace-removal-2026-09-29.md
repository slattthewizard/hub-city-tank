# What Happens to the Chimney When the Oil Furnace and Tank Come Out

Pulling an oil furnace almost always comes with a tank decision, and the tank decision has a quieter partner: the chimney that vented the burner. Once the furnace is gone, that opening doesn't close itself.

The short answer depends on what's left. If nothing else in the house vents into that chimney, the flue opening should be properly closed and the top of the chimney protected from rain and animals. If something else still vents there, like an oil-fired water heater, the chimney may now be too big for it, and that needs a technician's look. And if you're keeping the oil furnace as backup for a heat pump, the chimney is still working equipment and still needs its yearly check.

## The furnace, the tank and the chimney are one decision

An oil furnace doesn't vent on its own. A flue pipe (sometimes called the smoke pipe) runs from the furnace to a chimney. Natural Resources Canada's [Heating with Oil guide](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/sites/nrcan/files/energy/pdf/energystar/Heating-with-Oil_EN.pdf) lists the chimney types most oil furnaces and boilers use:

- a Type A chimney, a double-walled, insulated, prefabricated metal chimney with a stainless steel lining
- a masonry chimney lined with clay flue tile
- a masonry chimney lined with a certified stainless steel liner

Some newer oil appliances vent out the side wall instead and have their own venting rules. What they have in common is that the furnace, the tank and the chimney were all installed to work together, so pulling one piece out changes what the others need.

In practice they usually fall to different trades. Whoever removes the furnace disconnects the flue pipe. What happens at the chimney itself depends on its condition and on whether anything else still uses it. The chimney side falls to the heating contractor removing the furnace, or to a chimney contractor if masonry or liner work is needed. ## Going fully electric: close it off, don't leave it open

If the furnace is coming out for good and nothing else vents into that chimney, don't leave an open hole behind.

An unused, open flue works against you in a few ordinary ways. Rain and melting snow can run down the chimney and soak into the masonry or the framing around it. Birds and small animals treat an open chimney as a way in. In winter, an unsealed opening is one more place for warm house air to escape, which shows up on your electricity bill once you're heating with a heat pump or baseboards.

What closing it off looks like depends on the chimney:

- **Where the flue pipe entered the chimney.** The opening in the chimney wall (often called the thimble) should get a proper non-combustible cover, not a rag, cardboard or tape.
- **At the top.** A masonry chimney that no longer vents anything still faces weather. A chimney contractor can advise on a cap that keeps rain and animals out, or on whether the chimney should come down below the roofline instead.
- **Metal chimneys.** A prefabricated metal chimney that's no longer in use still needs its cap and flashing kept in good shape, or it needs to be removed and the roof patched.

## If a water heater still uses the chimney, check the size

This is the case that catches people out. Say the oil furnace comes out but an oil-fired water heater stays behind, still venting into the same chimney. That chimney was sized for the furnace and water heater together. Now it's serving one small appliance.

NRCan's guide explains why that matters. When a chimney is larger than needed, the combustion gases rise slowly, cool in the oversized flue and can reach the point where the water vapour in them condenses. The guide notes that this is worse in a masonry chimney on an outside wall, where the cold outdoor air chills it. That condensation can soak into the brick and cause structural or water damage.

The signs NRCan lists are worth knowing:

- white, powdery deposits on the outside of the chimney
- bricks that are flaking or spalling, or crumbling mortar joints
- wet patches on inside walls behind the chimney
- pieces of flue tile at the bottom of the chimney
- water running out of the cleanout door or around the base

The usual fix, according to the same guide, is a properly sized stainless steel liner, often insulated, and in some cases a sealed double-walled stainless flue pipe from the appliance to the chimney. A smaller liner keeps the gases warm and moving so they don't condense. NRCan also says to check with your provincial fuel safety authority on which liner method it approves. Sizing and installing a liner is technician work, so have whoever services the water heater look at the chimney as soon as the furnace is gone, not after the first winter.

## Keeping oil as backup: the chimney doesn't get to retire

Many heat pump conversions keep the oil furnace for the coldest stretches. If the furnace still fires on cold nights, the chimney is still carrying combustion gases every time it runs.

A backup furnace that runs less isn't automatically easier on its chimney. It still has to be vented properly every time it lights, and the chimney still goes through New Brunswick's freeze-thaw cycles all winter. That's the argument for keeping the annual service even though you're burning far less oil. NRCan's guide recommends annual service by a heating contractor and says to check the chimney occasionally for deterioration, which you can do by holding a mirror in the cleanout opening on a bright day and looking for a broken or flaking liner.

Health Canada's page on [preventing carbon monoxide exposure](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/pollutants/carbon-monoxide/preventing-exposure.html) points the same way. It advises having fuel-burning appliances inspected by a professional at least once a year, or as the manufacturer says, and checking exhaust vents during and after snowstorms so they aren't blocked by ice, snow or debris. A backup furnace you rarely think about is exactly the one that gets missed.

The oil side needs its yearly look too. The 10th edition of CSA B139, the installation code for oil-burning equipment, added an annual visual inspection for leakage in fuel oil tanks, tubing, piping and filters, as [CSA Group's summary of the changes](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/) notes. If a technician is coming out anyway, ask them to look at the tank, the line, the flue pipe and the chimney on the same visit. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to cap the chimney if I remove my oil furnace?

If nothing else vents into that chimney, yes, close it off. The opening where the flue pipe entered should get a proper non-combustible cover, and the top of a masonry chimney should be protected from rain and animals or taken down below the roofline. Ask the heating contractor removing the furnace, or a chimney contractor, which suits your chimney.

### What happens if I just leave the old flue opening as is?

An open, unused flue lets in rain, snowmelt and animals, and it lets warm air escape in winter. Water running down the inside of a masonry chimney can damage the brick and the walls around it over time. It's better to close it off properly than to leave it open "for now."

### Does an oil water heater need a new chimney liner once the furnace is gone?

It might. A chimney sized for a furnace and a water heater together can be too big for the water heater alone, and NRCan's Heating with Oil guide explains that an oversized, cold chimney lets flue gases cool and condense, which damages masonry. A properly sized stainless steel liner is the usual fix. Have a technician check it.

### If I keep my oil furnace as backup with a heat pump, does the chimney still need yearly attention?

Yes. The chimney carries combustion gases every time the backup furnace fires. Health Canada advises having fuel-burning appliances inspected by a professional at least once a year or as the manufacturer recommends, and the 10th edition of CSA B139 added an annual visual inspection for leaks in the tank, lines and filters. Do both on one visit.

### Who caps or seals the chimney, the tank company or someone else?

Usually someone else. The flue pipe connection comes off with the furnace, so the heating contractor removing it deals with that end. Capping the top of a masonry chimney, taking it down or relining it is chimney contractor work. We remove and replace oil tanks across Greater Moncton, but we don't price or sell chimney work.

### Does removing the oil tank affect the chimney at all?

Not directly. The tank stores fuel and the chimney vents combustion gases, so removing the tank on its own doesn't change what the chimney needs. The two come up together because they're usually decided at the same time, when a household is moving off oil or keeping it only as backup.
