# Oil Tank for a Toyotomi or Monitor Heater: Setup, Rules and When to Replace It

Plenty of Atlantic Canadian homes and cottages have a small oil tank outside a wall, with a copper line running through to a direct-vent heater inside. The heater is usually a Toyotomi Laser, or an older Monitor unit. These heaters don't use a basement furnace or a central tank. They run from a separate tank, and that tank is the part we can speak to.

The short answer: the tank feeding one of these heaters is still an oil tank, and the same installation rules apply to it as to any other. It has to be sound, set on a stable base, vented, piped properly and checked. If yours is rusty, leaning or of unknown age, replace it. ## What these heaters are, and how the tank fits in

A direct-vent oil heater sits against an outside wall and breathes through a sealed pipe: combustion air comes in from outdoors and exhaust goes out the same way. That's the "vent" in the name. It has no ductwork and no basement furnace. Fuel arrives by a small copper line from an outdoor tank, set outside the same wall.

In heater terms this is called a gravity-fed external tank. The heater has no big pump pulling oil from a distance. It relies on the tank sitting at the right height so oil flows down to the burner under its own weight. That is why the tank's position matters more than with a typical furnace tank.

## What the manufacturer says: Toyotomi's Laser 73 manual

The most useful thing we can point to is the maker's own instructions. Toyotomi's [Laser 73 installation and operation manual (Type O)](https://toyotomiusa.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Laser-73-Type-O-English.pdf) has a fuelling section for the large external tank. This is one model's manual, printed by Toyotomi USA, so check the manual for your own model before you rely on any number below. The Laser 73 section says:

| Point | What the manual says |
|---|---|
| Who installs | The tank must be purchased separately and installed by a qualified fuel supply technician |
| Codes | Installation must comply with NFPA 31, CSA B139 (the Installation Code for Oil Burning Equipment), or locally applicable codes, and the owner should check with local building officials |
| Outlet height | The tank's fuel outlet should be at least 16 inches above the floor the heater stands on |
| Maximum height | The top of the tank should be no more than 8-1/2 feet above that floor, to avoid excess pressure at the heater |
| Heat sources | The tank should be at least 6 feet from all heat sources |
| Fuel line | 3/8 inch OD copper tubing, smooth, with no U-shaped or sharp bends that can trap air |
| Filter and valves | A UL listed filter at the tank outlet (the manual highly recommends a small-mesh in-line filter with a water block), plus shut-off valves on the line |
| Vent | The tank must have an operating vent |

Two other items in that section are worth knowing. If the height from the top of the tank to the bottom of the heater is over 8-1/2 feet, the manual says a pressure reducing valve is required. And an extra shut-off valve next to the exterior wall means less fuel drains if the heater has to be disconnected.

None of this is unusual. It's the same list of things that applies to any tank: vent, filter, valves, good copper and a stable stand. The only different details are the height window, which comes from the gravity feed.

## What fuel goes in it

Check your own manual here too. The Laser 73 manual lists ASTM D3699 1-K kerosene, ASTM D396 low sulphur No. 1 fuel oil, or ASTM D975 ultra low sulphur diesel, and it says never to use gasoline. It does not list ordinary No. 2 furnace oil, the heavier oil in most home basement tanks. Furnace oil, stove oil and diesel are cousins, not twins; our guide to [furnace oil vs diesel vs stove oil](/blog/furnace-oil-vs-diesel/) goes through the differences.

For you as a tank owner, the point is simple: put the fuel your heater's manual names into the tank, and don't change it without checking. If you're not sure what's in the tank now, find out before the next fill. A mix of fuel types in a small tank, with water and sludge on the bottom, is how a heater starts to lock out and a filter starts to clog.

## The tank still has to meet the same rules

People sometimes treat a small heater tank like a jerry can: it's small, it's for a cottage, it doesn't count. It counts. It's an oil tank holding fuel outside a building in a freeze-thaw climate, and the manufacturer's own manual says the installation must comply with CSA B139 or local codes.

In practice that means the same list we use for any outdoor tank:

- **A stable, level base.** A small tank on a couple of cinder blocks that have settled is a classic weak point. Our guide to [oil tank stands and pads](/blog/oil-tank-stand-and-pad/) explains why the base matters more than the tank.
- **Clearances.** Distance from the house, windows and property line follow the tank's installation rules; our guide to [outdoor oil tank clearances](/blog/outdoor-oil-tank-clearances/) covers them. The heater manual's 6 foot distance from heat sources is a separate rule.
- **Snow and ice.** A small tank beside a wall is exactly where roof snow lands. Our guide to [protecting your oil tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/) covers what to clear and why.
- **A sound fill and vent, and a filter you can reach.** The filter at the outlet is the first thing that tells you the tank is shedding rust or water.

The tank maker Granby says on its [questions and answers page](https://www.granbyindustries.com/en-us/petroleum-tanks/questions-and-answers/) that "all tanks should be inspected and serviced annually," and that a tank with no label that shows any signs of ageing should be replaced as soon as possible. A small tank isn't exempt from either.

## Reading an old small tank

Some of these tanks have been outside for decades, and nobody remembers when they went in. Spend ten minutes on it with our [10-minute oil tank inspection checklist](/blog/oil-tank-inspection-checklist/). The signs that matter on a small tank:

- **Rust at the bottom or along seams**, especially blistered or flaking steel, not just surface colour.
- **A wet spot or staining under the tank**, or a smell of oil at the wall where the line goes in.
- **A tank that leans**, or legs that have sunk into the ground.
- **A filter that clogs every few weeks.** That points to rust, sludge or water in the tank, not a bad filter.
- **A heater that keeps locking out** when the filter and fuel are fine, which can point to oil flow, not just the heater.
- **No label or date at all.**

If one of these is true, the tank is the problem, and repairing the heater won't fix it. A small steel tank corrodes from the inside the same way a big one does. Our guide to [how long oil tanks last](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) covers the lifespan side.

## The decision: replace the tank, or plan for the heater

You have two honest choices.

**Replace the small tank.** If the heater is working, the house or cottage still depends on it, and the tank is rusty, leaning or of unknown age, replace the tank before it fails. A new tank means new fittings, a clean filter setup and a stable base, set to the heater maker's height and clearance rules. The prices on our [oil tank replacement cost](/oil-tank-replacement-cost/) page are for standard home heating tanks, so a small heater tank is quoted by written estimate. Our [oil tank replacement in Moncton](/oil-tank-replacement-moncton/) page explains how the job goes.

**Plan for the heater's future.** If the heater itself is old and you're thinking about replacing it with a heat pump or another system, the tank may not be worth replacing. Removal alone is $500 to $900 for an outdoor tank. In that case, burn down what's in the tank, stop deliveries in writing and have the tank, fill and vent come out together. Don't let an unused tank sit and age beside the wall.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use the same tank for a Toyotomi heater and a furnace?

Sometimes, but that depends on the fuel each appliance is rated for and on how the tank is piped. The Laser 73 manual lists kerosene, No. 1 fuel oil or ULSD, not ordinary furnace oil. Check both manuals before you share a tank, and have the setup looked at.

### How high should the tank be above the heater?

For the Laser 73, the manual says the tank's fuel outlet should be at least 16 inches above the floor the heater stands on, and the top of the tank no more than 8-1/2 feet above it. Other models can differ, so read the manual for yours.

### Do I need a filter on the line?

The Laser 73 manual says to install a UL listed filter at the tank outlet and recommends a small-mesh in-line filter with a water block. A filter that clogs often is a sign of water, sludge or rust in the tank.

### Does a small heater tank need to meet code?

Yes. The heater manual says the installation must comply with CSA B139 or locally applicable codes, and that it should be installed by a qualified fuel supply technician. Ask your local authority what applies at your address.

### My tank is rusty but the heater works fine. Should I wait?

A working heater doesn't tell you anything about the tank. Rust, a lean or a wet spot are reasons to act now, before the tank fails in cold weather. Have it looked at and decide whether to replace it or remove it.
