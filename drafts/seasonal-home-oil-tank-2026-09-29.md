# Your Cottage Oil Tank Over an Empty New Brunswick Winter

You're closing up the cottage for the season, and the oil tank is the one piece of the property that doesn't get to hibernate quietly. It sits through six or seven months with nobody watching, and along the Northumberland Strait it does that sitting in salt air on top of everything a New Brunswick winter throws at it.

There's a real decision to make before you lock up: fill the tank, leave it low, or land somewhere in between. The short answer is to keep it topped up and pair that with a proper look at the tank. There's also a question that has nothing to do with the tank itself and everything to do with the insurance policy in your filing cabinet. This guide covers both, plus a checklist to run through before you turn the key for the last time this year.

## A Cottage Tank Ages on a Different Clock

An oil tank at a year-round home in Moncton or Riverview gets looked at, whether the homeowner realizes it or not. Someone notices the gauge on the way to the car, smells something off near the basement stairs, sees a stain on the pad while mowing. A cottage tank around Shediac, Pointe-du-Chêne or Cocagne gets none of that between Thanksgiving and the May long weekend.

That gap matters more on this stretch of coast than inland. Salt carried off the Strait speeds up rust on bare or chipped steel, and Roth's [tank installation manual](https://www.roth-america.com/app/uploads/2021/02/DWT_Installation_Manual_2023.pdf) notes that long exposure to water-borne salts can significantly shorten even a galvanized steel shell's life. Add the freeze-thaw cycle that works on every outdoor tank in the province, plus a stand and pad nobody's checking for a sinking corner, and a closed-up cottage tank can be aging faster than its owner assumes. ## Full, Low, or Somewhere In Between: The Real Trade-off

The question is simple to ask: do you fill the tank before you leave for the season, or let it run low?

The case for filling is the standard advice. The Capital Regional District's [homeowner's guide to heating oil tanks](https://www.crd.ca/media/file/wwrhome-heating-oil-tanks-oct3webpdf) tells owners to keep the tank full over the summer to reduce condensation inside metal tanks, which can cause corrosion. Less air space means less surface for moisture to condense on, and that water is what settles at the bottom and works on a steel tank from the inside. We go through the mechanism in our [guide to keeping an oil tank full](/blog/keep-oil-tank-full-summer/) and our [winter preparation checklist](/blog/oil-tank-winter-preparation/). The same physics applies whether the tank heats an occupied house or sits untouched at a closed cottage.

The counterpoint is worth saying out loud. A full tank is more oil sitting idle for months, and more oil at risk if something goes wrong while nobody's there: a cracked fitting, a bit of frost heave under the stand, a fill pipe seal that finally gives out. Nobody spots a small weep at a closed cottage in January the way they might at a home they walk past every day.

That gap in supervision is exactly what the installation code now addresses. The 10th edition of [CSA B139](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/), the national code for oil-burning equipment, added a requirement for an annual visual inspection of fuel oil tanks, tubing, piping and filters for leakage. A tank at a year-round home tends to get that kind of look just from daily life around it. A cottage tank often doesn't, unless someone builds it into the closing or opening routine.

Put those together and the practical answer isn't really full versus empty. Protect the tank from condensation by topping it up before you close, and pair that with a proper look at the tank itself, right before you leave or right when you're back in spring, so the annual check happens on a tank nobody watches in between. ## Salt Air Doesn't Wait for You to Come Back

Every general rule about coastal corrosion gets sharper at a cottage that sits empty. A spring rinse to wash salt off the shell, a quick scratch test on a suspicious patch of paint, a dab of touch-up on a chip: none of it happens on a schedule when the person who'd do it is back in Moncton or out of province.

That's a fair argument for choosing a tank that needs less babysitting if you're replacing one at a coastal cottage. A fibreglass tank can't rust. ## What Insurers of Seasonal Homes Tend to Ask

Seasonal properties are covered differently from a primary home, and it's worth knowing that before an oil tank question turns into a coverage surprise.

The [Insurance Bureau of Canada](https://www.ibc.ca/news-insights/in-focus/what-to-know-about-seasonal-property-insurance) notes that coverage may be more limited when a property sits unoccupied for long periods, often written on a named-perils basis rather than comprehensive, and that insurers may weigh occupancy, security, maintenance and winterization when setting a premium. It also says poor maintenance and wear and tear may not be covered, and that insurers often expect owners to inspect, maintain and winterize a property when it isn't in use. Keeping photos and records makes a claim easier. For the tank, that means dated photos of the tank, the pad and the ground around it when you close up, and again when you open.

If your cottage tank is old enough that a year-round homeowner would already be getting an insurance letter about it, expect the same question at a seasonal property. Don't assume part-year use buys an old tank any slack; ask your broker. ## A Closing Checklist for the Tank, Not Just the Cottage

Most closing checklists focus on pipes, the fridge and the mice. Give the tank ten minutes of its own before you leave.

**Decide on the fill level and do it on purpose.** If the tank is staying in service, top it up rather than leaving it to run low by accident. If you're not sure it'll be back in use, that's a different conversation: the 10th edition of CSA B139 also added requirements for removing a tank system that hasn't been used for more than three years. Talk it through before you decide for the winter.

**Walk the whole tank, not just the gauge.** Check the seaward side if you're on the coast, since that's often where corrosion shows first. Look low along the bottom seam and where the legs meet the shell. Photograph anything that looks off, and photograph the tank even if nothing does, since that record is what an insurer or a buyer might ask for later.

**Check the legs and stand.** A tank that's leaning even slightly in September can be worse by May after a winter of frost heave. This isn't a fix-it-yourself job if it's already moving.

**Clear the vent and fill caps, and make sure they'll stay clear.** Snow and ice sliding off a cottage roof onto an outdoor tank is a real risk in exposed spots along the Strait. Our guide on [protecting a tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/) covers what to check before you go.

**Ask someone local to glance at it, if you can.** A neighbour, a caretaker, anyone who's around over the winter and willing to notice a stain on the ground or a tank that's shifted. It isn't a substitute for the annual inspection the code calls for, but it closes some of the gap between visits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I leave my cottage oil tank full or empty over the winter?

Keeping it topped up is the standard advice, because less air space in the tank means less condensation, and that water is what corrodes steel from the inside. The trade-off is that a fuller tank means more oil at risk if something fails unseen, which is why a visual check of the tank at closing or reopening matters as much as the fill level.

### How often should someone check on a closed-up cottage with an oil tank?

There's no single rule for every policy or property. CSA B139, the national installation code for oil-burning equipment, now calls for an annual visual inspection of the tank, tubing, piping and filters for leakage. At a cottage that sits closed for months, build that check into the closing or opening routine, and arrange more frequent looks where you can. Your insurer may have its own expectations.

### Does homeowner's insurance cover an oil tank leak at a vacant cottage?

It depends on the policy, and seasonal property coverage is often more limited than a standard home policy. The Insurance Bureau of Canada notes that insurers weigh maintenance, winterization and occupancy for seasonal properties, and that poor maintenance may not be covered. Read your policy wording, ask your broker about the tank, and keep dated photos of it each time you close up or open.

### Will salt air ruin my cottage's oil tank faster than one inland?

It can shorten a steel tank's life. Salt speeds up rust wherever paint is chipped or thin, and a cottage that sits unattended loses the small habits, like touching up a chip or rinsing salt off the shell, that buy a tank extra years. A coastal steel tank deserves a closer look at every opening and closing, especially along the bottom seam and the legs.

### What should I do if I find a leak when I reopen the cottage in spring?

Don't try to patch or drain anything yourself.
