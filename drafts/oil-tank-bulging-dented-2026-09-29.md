# Oil Tank Bulging, Dented, or Cracked: What It Means

A dent in an oil tank isn't automatically an emergency, and a bulge isn't always either. What matters is where the damage sits, what the tank is made of, and whether the shape change is a scuff on the paint or a sign that the wall underneath has given way.

This guide walks through the kinds of damage a Southeast New Brunswick winter tends to leave behind: a dent from a chunk of roof ice, a bulge you don't remember seeing before, a crack in a fibreglass or double-wall tank, and a bent leg. We'll cover which of those you can leave alone, which need a professional look, and what replacement costs when the damage means the tank has to go.

## Dents From Falling Ice, Snowblowers, and Everyday Impact

A steel oil tank takes a hit reasonably well. Falling ice, a snowblower discharge chute, a ladder leaned against it, or a bumper backing too close can all leave a dent without doing real damage, because steel dents by absorbing the energy of the impact rather than cracking outright.

Location decides whether that matters. A shallow dent high on the tank's shoulder or top, away from any seam, is usually cosmetic. Something you'd note, photograph, and keep an eye on. A dent on a seam, over a fitting, or sharp enough that you can feel a crease rather than a smooth dish, is a different story. The lower part of a standard 910 litre tank is holding the full weight of the oil, and a sharp crease can become a weak spot, especially once the paint cracks and rust gets into the dented area. We cover that failure pattern in our guide to [rust on oil tanks](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/).

Manufacturers draw the same line. Granby's [installer instructions for its 2-in-1 double-wall tank](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0018_Ga-Granby-2-in-1-Installer-Instructions-1.pdf) list the damage an annual inspection should look for, including any dents on the seam of the shell, "major" dents or scratches in the shell, cuts or holes, loosened fittings, and any bending or leaning of the tank support. If any of those turn up, the instructions say to contact your installer immediately. That list is written for one tank model, but it's a sensible yardstick for any outdoor tank.

Southeast New Brunswick gives tanks more of this than most places see. A slab of ice off a steel roof lands hard, and a plow or snowblower working close to the tank all winter can leave the lower shell scuffed and dented by spring. ## A Bulging Tank: What It's Telling You

True bulging, where a flat panel pushes outward into a dome, isn't something a single-wall steel heating oil tank should do. These tanks are built for oil at atmospheric pressure, vented to the outside, not to hold pressure. If a steel tank's end or side looks pushed out, the likely causes are pressure it was never meant to see, such as a blocked vent during a fill, or steel that's been weakened by corrosion. Neither is something to watch and wait on. Have it looked at before the next delivery.

Double-wall tanks are a different case, and worth understanding if that's what you have. [Granby's product page for its 2-in-1 tank](https://www.granbyindustries.com/en-us/petroleum-tanks/products/2-in-1-double-wall-oil-tanks/) describes a high-density polyethylene (HDPE) inner tank inside a galvanized steel containment tank, with a visual leak indicator for monitoring the inner tank. That indicator, not the look of the outer shell, is the designed warning. Granby's installer instructions say to check the leak detector quarterly, and if oil is confirmed in the space between the two walls, the installer is to be contacted immediately to have the tank taken out of service.

Some movement in a double-wall tank is normal. Roth's [installation manual for its double-wall tank](https://www.roth-america.com/app/uploads/2021/02/DWT_Installation_Manual_2023.pdf) says the sides may expand up to 2 inches (about 5 cm) per side when the tank is filled, and calls that normal. It lists bulging beyond that as a sign of a fill pumped in too fast, a restricted vent or vent whistle, or water that got into the space between the walls and froze. So on a double-wall tank, a slight swell when full is expected, while a shell that's creased, swollen more than usual, or changing shape from one season to the next needs a professional look, along with a check of the leak indicator.

On either kind of tank, a bulge that's new, that's getting worse, or that comes with any dampness or smell nearby means the tank has moved from "keep an eye on it" to "get it assessed soon."

## Cracks: Steel, Fibreglass, and the Poly Inner Tank Aren't the Same Problem

Cracking shows up differently depending on what the tank is made of, and the honest answer for each is different too.

**Steel tanks** don't typically crack from a single impact the way brittle materials do. What looks like a crack on an old steel tank is almost always corrosion that has eaten through the metal from the inside, usually along the bottom seam, and finally let go. If that's what you're seeing, our guide to [how long oil tanks last](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) explains why the inside of the tank ages faster than the outside looks.

**Fibreglass tanks** handle corrosion far better than steel, which is a big part of why homeowners choose them, but they respond to a hard, sharp impact differently. Fibreglass doesn't dent the way steel does, so a hard point impact (the corner of a falling slab of ice, a dropped tool, a post backed into it) can crack or craze the material instead. A crack you can see, or a spot where the surface has gone white and starred, needs a professional look. Our honest view is that a cracked fibreglass tank is a replacement job. It isn't something to patch at home and trust with several hundred litres of oil.

**The poly inner tank** in a double-wall setup is out of sight, which is why the leak indicator matters. You can't inspect the inner tank directly, so a leak indicator showing oil, or oil in the space between the walls, is your sign that the inner tank has failed. That's a replacement, not a repair.

In all three cases, the material doesn't heal on its own. A crack or a leak stays at least as bad as it is, and usually gets worse.

## Damaged Legs and Stands

A dented or bent leg gets less attention than a dent in the tank shell, but a full 910 litre tank is carrying roughly 770 kg of oil before you count the steel, and on a standard tank that weight rides on four small legs. A leg that's been clipped by a plow, bent by a sliding snow load, or rusted thin at the base doesn't have to fail all at once. It can slowly let the tank tilt, which puts strain on the oil line and fittings long before the tank itself is the problem. ## Cosmetic or Replace Now: A Quick Read on Tank Damage

| What you're seeing | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Small shallow dent or scuff high on the shell, paint intact | Usually cosmetic | Photograph it, recheck each season |
| Dent on a seam, a sharp crease, or damage near a fitting | Possible weak spot, especially once rust sets in | Get a professional look before your next delivery |
| Pushed-out end or side on a single-wall steel tank | Pressure or corrosion the tank wasn't built for | Get it assessed before the next delivery |
| Double-wall tank swollen more than usual, creased, or leak indicator showing oil | Fill, vent, or frozen-water problem, or a failed inner tank | Call for an assessment, don't wait on this one |
| Crack in a fibreglass shell | The material has failed at that point | Replacement, not a repair |
| Bent, cracked, or badly rusted leg | Less support under several hundred kilograms of oil | Have the stand and tank checked together |
| Any of the above plus an oil smell, staining, or a wet spot underneath | Possible active leak | Treat it as urgent and call right away |

## What Your Insurer Asks About Dents and Damage

Insurers ask about this kind of damage by name. The national [CSIO Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire), a standard form many brokers use, has a tank condition checklist that includes leaks, drips, spills, fumes, odours, rust, corrosion, weeping, wet spots and dents, and asks for details on anything checked. A separate question asks whether the tank, fuel supply line and valve are protected from impact, with vehicles and ice given as examples. That's the same damage this article covers.

How a particular insurer treats a dent depends on the company and the policy, so ask your broker rather than guessing. Having photos and a professional's written assessment on hand makes that conversation easier. If you're wondering what your policy would do if the tank did leak, our guide on [whether insurance covers oil tank leaks](/blog/does-insurance-cover-oil-tank-leaks/) walks through it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a small dent in my oil tank dangerous?

Usually not, if it's high on the shell, the paint isn't cracked, and the metal underneath still feels smooth and solid. Dents become a concern when they sit near a seam, a fitting, or the bottom third of the tank, or when a dent starts trapping moisture that leads to rust. Photograph it and have it checked at your next inspection if you're unsure.

### Why would a double-wall oil tank's outer shell bulge?

A little is normal. Roth's manual says its double-wall tank's sides may expand up to 2 inches per side when full. It lists bulging beyond that as a sign of a fill pumped too fast, a restricted vent, or water frozen between the walls. The designed warning for a leaking inner tank is the leak indicator, so check it, and get the tank assessed if the shell is creased or swelling more than usual.

### Can a cracked fibreglass oil tank be repaired instead of replaced?

We wouldn't trust one. Fibreglass resists corrosion well, but a crack from an impact goes through the material that holds the oil in, and it isn't something to patch and put back in service. Treat a cracked fibreglass tank as a replacement, and have it looked at soon, because the longer it stays in use, the more chance the crack turns into an active leak.

### Will my insurance company care about an old dent?

It may. The standard CSIO tank questionnaire many brokers use lists dents in its tank condition checklist and asks for details on anything checked, so expect questions if one shows up in a photo. How much it matters depends on your insurer. A professional's written assessment saying the dent is cosmetic, or a record of the repair or replacement, gives your broker something concrete to work with.

### What if the dent or crack is right where the oil line connects?

Treat that as more urgent than damage elsewhere on the shell. The fitting and the line are the most delicate parts of the whole setup, and damage there is far more likely to turn into a leak than a dent on an open stretch of steel. Call and describe exactly what you're seeing so we can tell you whether it's safe to leave until a scheduled visit or needs a closer look right away.
