## Why a Driver Refuses to Fill Your Tank

Delivery companies aren't guessing here. Filling a tank by hose means pumping oil fast into something the driver can't see inside, so the driver relies on the tank's vent, fill pipe and whistle doing their jobs. [CSA B139](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/), Canada's installation code for oil-burning equipment, sets the rules for how tanks, vents and fill pipes are installed. The current edition, for example, says a vent pipe that simply opens downward without a vent cap isn't enough to keep out debris and ice. Each supplier sets its own delivery policy, but a driver who sees a problem will usually walk away rather than gamble. Our full breakdown of the code is in [CSA B139 oil tank rules](/blog/csa-b139-oil-tank-code/), but at the tank, these are the things that commonly trigger a no:

- **A visible leak or a wet spot.** Staining on the pad, a damp patch under the tank, or a puddle near a fitting. No driver will add more oil to a tank that's already losing it.
- **A leaning tank or a sinking leg.** If the tank isn't sitting level, or a leg has lost contact with the ground, the shell and the oil line are under stress the driver can see just by looking. Our post on [oil tank leaning or sinking](/blog/oil-tank-leaning-sinking/) covers why this gets worse on its own, not better.
- **A vent that stays silent.** The vent whistle sounds as air is pushed out during a fill and goes quiet when the oil nears the top. It's the driver's main warning that the tank is close to full, so a whistle that doesn't sound when the fill starts is a common reason to stop. We cover how that works in [the oil tank vent whistle](/blog/oil-tank-vent-whistle/).
- **A buried, iced-over, or missing vent or fill cap.** If the driver can't find or reach the fittings safely, or snow and ice have sealed them, the delivery waits.
- **Heavy rust, a dent, or a bulge in the shell.** Corrosion and physical damage both point to a tank that may not hold up to a full fill.
- **No readable certification tag on a tank that already looks its age.** A missing or unreadable label on its own rarely stops a fill, but paired with rust or a lean, it can tip the driver toward a no.

Most of this is about condition, not the date on the label. New Brunswick's [Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation](https://laws.gnb.ca/en/showdoc/cr/87-97) is the provincial rulebook for petroleum storage, and its licensing requirement is written for systems of 2,000 litres or more, well above a typical 910 litre home tank. The tank-age limits most homeowners run into come from insurance companies; our post on [oil tank age and insurance limits](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) covers those. Supplier policies vary, and some may have their own rules about very old tanks, so ask. But in most cases what stops a delivery is what the driver can see standing in front of the tank. An older tank that's solid, level, and whistling on the fill may well get filled. A ten-year-old tank with a bad lean might not.

## What a Refusal or a Red Tag Is Telling You

Some companies leave a tag on the fill pipe. Others note it on the invoice or call the office to flag your account before the next scheduled stop. However it reaches you, it means the same thing: something at the tank needs attention before the next fill, not that you did anything wrong.

Treat it as useful information, not a penalty. The driver who turns around today is the same one who'd otherwise be explaining an overfill on your lawn tomorrow. A refusal costs you an inconvenient afternoon. A fill that goes wrong costs a great deal more, in oil on the ground and in the cleanup that follows. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why would an oil company refuse to deliver to my tank?

Usually because of something the driver could see at the tank itself: a leak or wet spot, a lean or a sinking leg, a vent that didn't whistle during the attempt, ice or snow blocking the fill or vent, or visible rust and damage to the shell. Account issues and a supplier's own tank policies can play a part too, so ask the office for the exact reason before you book any work.

### Is a refused delivery the same thing as a "red tag"?

They usually mean the same thing, described differently. Some companies leave a physical tag on the fill pipe, others note the refusal on your invoice or flag it internally and call the office. Either way, it's a signal that something needs attention before the next fill attempt. If the tag or note names a specific defect, keep it and show it to whoever looks at the tank.

### What if I'm almost out of oil and the driver won't fill?

If the refusal wasn't for a leak, a road diesel fill from a gas station, 20 to 40 litres poured into the fill pipe, will usually run a furnace for a day or two while you get the real problem fixed. If the refusal was for a leak or a wet spot, skip the diesel and call a technician instead, since adding fuel to a leaking tank makes the problem worse, not better.

### Does a refused delivery mean I need a whole new tank?

Not necessarily. A blocked vent, a dead whistle, or a tank that needs re-levelling on a proper base can often be fixed without replacing the tank. Heavy rust, a bulge, corroded-through legs, or a shell that's already weeping usually mean the tank has reached the end of its useful life, and a repair there just delays the same problem.

### Is it the tank's age that makes companies refuse older tanks?

Usually not by itself. The tank-age limits homeowners hear about mostly come from insurance companies, and most delivery refusals come from what a driver can see at the tank on the day. Some suppliers may have their own policies about very old tanks, so ask yours. A well-kept older tank can pass fine; a newer tank with a bad lean or a silent vent might not.

### What should I do if I see oil on the ground when the driver leaves?

Stop and don't touch the tank or fittings. Report it: 1-800-565-1633 is the 24-hour environmental emergency number the federal government lists for New Brunswick, and during business hours you can also reach the provincial environment department's regional office. Then call a tank crew to look at the tank and the ground around it. The sooner both calls happen, the smaller the cleanup usually turns out to be.
