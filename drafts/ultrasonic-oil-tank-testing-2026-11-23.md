# Ultrasonic Oil Tank Testing: What It Can Tell You About an Old Steel Tank

Ultrasonic testing measures how much steel is left in the wall of an oil tank. A technician presses a probe against the outside of the tank, the probe sends a pulse of sound through the steel, and the time the echo takes to come back gives the thickness at that spot. It's a way to look at the inside of a steel tank without opening it, which matters because steel tanks fail from the inside out.

That sounds like a clean answer to "is my old tank still good?" It's a useful one, with limits. This guide covers what the test measures, what it can and can't find, whether your insurer will accept it, and how a result leads to one of three decisions: keep the tank, test it again, or replace it. ## What ultrasonic testing measures

A steel tank is lost one thin layer at a time. Water and sludge settle on the bottom, and the steel there corrodes where nobody can see it. From the outside, a tank can look sound while its floor has thinned. Our guide to [rust on an oil tank](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) explains why outside rust is only part of the picture.

Ultrasonic thickness testing gets around that by measuring the wall directly. Maine's Department of Environmental Protection, which runs a program on home heating oil tanks, describes it on its page about [ultrasonic thickness testing of your home heating oil tank](https://www1.maine.gov/dep/waste/abovegroundtanks/tanksure.html). The page says the test is used to find out how thick the steel is, that the thickness is typically measured at several spots along the bottom centreline (that's where water and sediment collect), and that the test is non-invasive and takes about fifteen minutes. It adds that it's usually done annually with the heating system's tune-up and cleaning.

Two notes on that source. It's a US state agency, not a New Brunswick one, and the page doesn't say what thickness counts as a failure. We haven't found a New Brunswick rule that sets one, and we won't invent a number. How a result gets judged is up to the tester, who compares readings with what the tank should measure, and to your insurer.

## What the test can tell you

**The thickness at the spots that were measured.** That's what you get, and it's a real fact about the tank. If several readings along the bottom are close to what a tank of that type should have, the steel at those points hasn't lost much. If they're well down, the tank has been losing steel.

**Whether the bottom is thinning faster than the rest.** Because water and sludge collect there, bottom readings are the ones that matter most on an old tank. Readings on the sides and ends give a comparison.

**A trend, if you test more than once.** One reading is a snapshot. Two readings a year or two apart tell you how fast the steel is going. That's the most useful thing the test can do for a tank you're thinking of keeping, and it's why Maine's page describes it as an annual check.

**Something concrete to take to your insurer.** A written set of readings is a different kind of evidence from "it looks fine to me." Our guide to [oil tank inspection for insurance](/blog/oil-tank-inspection-for-insurance/) explains how a written record differs from your own walkaround.

## What it can't tell you

These limits matter as much as the reading.

**It can miss a pit.** Corrosion in a steel tank often shows up as pits, small deep spots, rather than an even loss. A technical paper by J. C. Drury, an ultrasonic specialist, on [ultrasonic corrosion monitoring](https://www.eddyfi.com/doc/Downloadables/ultrasonics_corrosion_pitting-01.pdf) says that cone-type pitting is very common and the most difficult to detect, because the surfaces are rough and often ridged and the target is small in relation to the sound beam. It says that type of corrosion has the lowest probability of detection and the greatest inherent inaccuracy in its measurement. The paper is from 2005 and written for industrial corrosion work, not home tanks, but the point carries over: a handful of readings can pass a tank that has a pit a few centimetres away.

**It's only as good as where it was measured.** The test reads the steel under the probe. A tank has a lot of steel, and a technician takes a limited number of readings. A tank with a good bottom centreline could still have a weak seam, a thin spot near a leg or a problem at the weld.

**It doesn't test the fittings.** A leaking filter, valve, gauge or oil line isn't a wall-thickness problem. Our guide to [a wet spot under an oil tank](/blog/wet-spot-under-oil-tank/) covers how to tell those apart.

**It isn't the right test for a fibreglass or plastic tank.** The method is described for steel, and a fibreglass tank has no steel wall to corrode. We haven't found a source that recommends it for fibreglass home tanks. A fibreglass tank is checked by looking for cracks, damage, a weeping fitting and a moving base.

**It doesn't predict the future.** A good reading says the tank was in good shape on the day. It doesn't say it will be next winter, and it doesn't change the tank's age, which is what many insurers use.

## Will your insurer accept an ultrasonic test?

It depends on the insurer. Whether a test result changes an insurer's decision is theirs to say, and different companies take different positions.

What's documented is that the pressure from insurers is real and often turns on age. A Canadian Underwriter feature on [total system containment](https://www.canadianunderwriter.ca/features/total-system-containment/), first published in 2010 and last updated in 2024, says that some insurers have required replacement based on a tank's appearance, age or a visual check, and that some have limited or excluded coverage by endorsement. Its authors argue that age alone is a poor yardstick and that ultrasonic testing is the better one. That's an argument for testing, made in an industry article, and it doesn't mean your insurer agrees. Treat it as background, not a promise.

So the practical order is:

1. **Ask your insurer first.** Ask whether they accept an ultrasonic thickness report, who has to perform it, and what result they'd want. Get the answer in writing.
2. **Check the age rule.** Many insurers have an age limit regardless of condition. Our guide to [oil tank age and insurance limits](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) explains how that works. If your tank is past the line, a good test may not change the outcome, and you'd be paying to learn something the insurer won't use.
3. **Then decide whether to test.** A test makes sense when the insurer will accept it, the tank is otherwise in decent shape, and you'd like to keep it.

## Does it make sense for your tank?

A test is worth thinking about in some situations and a waste of money in others.

**It can make sense when** the tank is middle-aged, the outside looks clean, the label is readable, your insurer has said in writing that it would take a thickness report, and you'd rather keep the tank than replace it.

**It usually doesn't make sense when** the tank has visible rust, weeping seams, a wet spot or a bulge. Those are signs on their own, and a test that passes a tank with a wet spot under it would be a bad reason to wait. Our guide to [how long oil tanks last](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) covers why age and location change the odds.

**Outdoor tanks near the coast** are a special case. Salt air attacks the outside of a steel tank as well as the inside. Our guide to [salt air oil tank corrosion](/blog/salt-air-oil-tank-corrosion/) explains why tanks around Shediac age faster. A thickness reading only shows the steel at that point, so outside corrosion is something to check by eye too.

## The decision: keep, retest or replace

The result should lead somewhere. Here's how we'd read it.

**Keep it.** The readings are healthy, the tank has no visible rust, wet spots or bulges, the insurer has accepted the report in writing, and the age is within what they'll cover. Keep the report with the tank's other paperwork and plan to look again.

**Retest.** The readings are acceptable but lower than you'd like, or the tank is old but the insurer accepts it. Ask the tester how soon to repeat the test. A second set of readings shows how fast the steel is going, which is more useful than either set alone. Put the next date on the calendar, and watch the bottom of the tank for signs of seepage.

**Replace it.** The readings are thin, the tank has a wet spot, the insurer won't accept a test and has a hard age limit, or you simply don't want to depend on a few readings from a tank near the end of its life. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is ultrasonic testing on an oil tank?

It's a way to measure the thickness of a steel tank wall from the outside. A probe sends sound into the steel and times the echo. Maine's environmental agency says the test is typically done at several spots along the bottom centreline and takes about fifteen minutes. It tells you the steel thickness at those spots and nothing more.

### Can ultrasonic testing find a pinhole leak?

Not reliably. The probe reads only the steel under it, and technical literature says pitting is among the hardest corrosion to detect because the pits are small and rough. A pit between readings can be missed. A damp spot, a smell or a stain is a stronger sign than a clean test, and should be treated as a leak.

### Will my insurance company accept an ultrasonic test?

It depends on the insurer, and we can't say for every one. Some may take a written thickness report, and some go by age and a visual check. Ask yours before you pay for one: whether they accept it, who must do it, and what result they need. Get the answer in writing.

### Can you ultrasonically test a fibreglass oil tank?

The method we found described is for steel walls, and a fibreglass tank has no steel to thin out. We haven't found a source recommending it for home fibreglass tanks. Fibreglass is checked by eye for cracks, impact damage, fittings and a settling base, so ask the maker or an inspector what they'd accept.

### How often should an old steel tank be tested?

Maine's agency describes it as an annual check, usually done with the system tune-up. There's no New Brunswick schedule we can point to for homes, so ask your insurer and the tester. Because a single reading is a snapshot, a repeat is what shows how fast the steel is being lost.
