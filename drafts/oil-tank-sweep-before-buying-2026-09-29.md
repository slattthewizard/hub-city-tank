# Oil Tank Scan Before You Buy: What a Sweep Can Tell You, and What It Can't

An oil tank scan, sometimes called a sweep, is a walk of the yard with a magnetometer or ground-penetrating radar to check whether an old buried heating oil tank is sitting under the lawn. It's the most direct way to answer a question a standard home inspection usually can't: does this property have a tank nobody's mentioned, and where is it?

## Magnetometer vs GPR: The Two Ways a Sweep Works

Two technologies handle most residential scans, and they don't do the job the same way.

A **magnetometer** reads distortions in the earth's magnetic field caused by ferrous metal, which is exactly what an old steel tank is made of. It's quick to set up and covers a yard fast. The catch is that it can't tell a buried tank from any other buried steel: rebar in a driveway slab, a fence footing, a steel water or gas line, an old post. Any of those can produce a hit that needs checking.

**Ground-penetrating radar (GPR)** sends radar pulses into the ground and reads what bounces back. Instead of reacting only to metal, it picks up changes in density and material, so it can show a steel tank, disturbed backfill where a tank may once have been, and some non-metallic objects a magnetometer would miss. It can also read through a concrete slab, which matters where a driveway or patio was poured over an old tank.

Neither method is perfect on its own. Dense clay, a high water table and a yard crossed by old utility lines can all make a signal harder to read. That's why many scanners use both: the magnetometer flags anything metallic quickly, and GPR is used on ambiguous spots to estimate depth and rule out a false alarm from a fence line or an old water pipe before anyone reaches for a shovel.

| Method | Detects | Strong point | Blind spot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnetometer | Ferrous metal (steel tanks, rebar, pipes, fence posts) | Quick first pass over an open yard | Can't tell a tank from other buried steel; doesn't see non-metallic material |
| Ground-penetrating radar (GPR) | Changes in density and material: metal, disturbed soil, some non-metallic objects | Reads through concrete and asphalt; helps estimate depth and rule out false hits | Slower; readings need a trained operator to interpret; dense clay and high groundwater reduce clarity |

## What a Written Sweep Report Should Say

The scan is only useful if the report holds up when an insurer or a lawyer reads it. A report worth handing to either one should spell out:

- **The method used.** Magnetometer only, GPR only, or both, and which parts of the property each one covered.
- **What was and wasn't swept.** Nobody can scan under a shed, a deck or a big tree as thoroughly as open lawn, and the report should say so rather than imply full coverage.
- **Any anomaly found**, with its approximate location, depth and size, plus an image of the reading if the equipment produces one.
- **A clear finding**: no indication of a buried tank in the areas swept, or a probable tank that needs to be confirmed by digging.
- **The date of the sweep and the equipment used**, so a reader knows the report is current and not inherited from a sale years earlier.

Insurers often ask a homeowner to fill in the industry's standard [Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire), published by the Centre for Study of Insurance Operations (CSIO). The form asks about the tank you use, its location, age, construction and condition, and it also asks directly whether there are any empty, abandoned or unused tanks at the location. If you're answering "no" to that line on an older property, a clean, dated sweep report is good paperwork to have behind the answer.

## What a Scan Cannot Rule Out

A sweep is one of the cheapest ways to cut your risk before closing. It still isn't a guarantee, and a technician worth hiring will say so upfront instead of overselling the result.

**It can't promise zero tanks.** A signal can be masked by a nearby utility line, a fence or dense clay, and a tank deeper than the equipment reads well can be missed. That's the case for a sweep that uses both methods and covers the whole accessible yard, not a quick pass along the foundation.

**It can't tell you the condition of a tank it finds.** A scan says something is there, roughly how big and roughly how deep. It says nothing about whether the steel is sound or paper-thin, or whether oil has already reached the soil around it. That only gets answered by exposing the tank and, where there's reason to, testing the soil.

**It doesn't replace digging when the stakes are high.** If a lawyer, lender or insurer needs certainty rather than probability, the scan is the first step, not the last. Say your offer on a 1960s bungalow in Riverview is conditional on "no evidence of a buried tank," and the sweep comes back clean. That's a strong result, but it describes the areas that were swept. It doesn't cover ground that a fence, a deck or an addition kept the equipment from reading.

## You Got a Hit. Here's What Happens Before You Close

A positive reading isn't a reason to walk away from a deal. It's also not something to sit on. Here's the order that keeps you covered.

**Confirm it.** A probable tank gets confirmed by exposing it, at least enough to see it and check the soil. This isn't a DIY job: an old tank can still hold oil, sludge and vapour decades after it was last used.

**Decide removal or abandonment.** Many buyers, lenders and insurers prefer a tank removed rather than left in place, because removal comes with soil samples showing whether the ground underneath is clean. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does an oil tank scan cost?

### Can a scan guarantee there's no buried oil tank on a property?

No. A scan can only speak to the ground it reads, and signals can be masked by nearby utility lines, dense clay or a high water table. It's a strong tool for reducing risk before closing, not an absolute guarantee, which is why the written report should state exactly what was and wasn't swept.

### Is GPR better than a magnetometer for finding a buried oil tank?

Neither one wins outright. A magnetometer is quick and suits a first pass over an open yard, but it can't tell a tank from other buried steel and doesn't see non-metallic material. GPR reads through concrete and helps estimate depth and rule out false alarms, but it's slower and needs a trained operator. Many sweeps use both.

### What happens if the scan finds a tank?

The next step is confirming it by exposing the tank enough to inspect it and check the soil. From there the usual choice is removal, which our price table puts at $2,000 to $4,000 for a straightforward buried tank, or abandonment in place where digging isn't practical. If the soil is contaminated, remediation adds $10,000 and up.

### Do I need a scan if the home inspector didn't mention a tank?

A standard home inspection isn't a tank sweep and doesn't use the same equipment. Inspectors look for visible signs, like a capped pipe or a patched hole in the basement wall, but a buried tank with no surface trace can go unnoticed. If the house has any history of oil heat, a separate scan is worth considering before you remove conditions.
