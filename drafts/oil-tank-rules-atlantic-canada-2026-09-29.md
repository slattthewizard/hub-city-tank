# Oil Tank Rules in New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, PEI and Newfoundland: How They Differ

Every Atlantic province regulates home heating oil tanks in some way, but no two do it the same way. New Brunswick's provincial tank licensing starts well above the size of a household tank, so for most homes the installation code and the insurance company set the practical rules. Nova Scotia puts its weight on the licensed technician who installs the tank. Prince Edward Island inspects and tags household tanks, and a steel tank's tag carries an expiry year. Newfoundland and Labrador goes furthest: it's illegal to put oil in a tank that hasn't been inspected and registered.

If you're comparing notes with a relative in another province, you've moved here from one, or you're reading an "Atlantic Canada" guide online, that patchwork matters. This guide walks through what each province requires for a typical residential tank, with New Brunswick covered in the most depth, since Southeast New Brunswick is the only area we work in.

## Why the Atlantic provinces don't share one rulebook

All four provinces point to the same national installation code, CSA B139, for how an oil-burning system goes together: the tank standard, the base it sits on, clearances, and the vent and fill piping. Where they part ways is in who checks that a tank meets it, and what happens as the tank ages.

New Brunswick and Nova Scotia don't run a tag-and-expiry system for an ordinary household tank. In practice, the insurer decides when a tank is too old to keep covering. Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland and Labrador built mandatory inspection and tagging systems, so the tag on the tank matters as much as what the insurer thinks.

Spills are the one area where the provinces sound alike. A heating oil release is reportable, and there's a 24-hour number to call. The federal [environmental emergency contacts page](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program/report-emergency.html) lists 1-800-565-1633 for New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island, and a different number, 1-800-563-9089, for Newfoundland and Labrador.

## New Brunswick: CSA B139 and your insurer, not a provincial tag

New Brunswick regulates petroleum storage under the Clean Environment Act's [Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation](https://laws.gnb.ca/en/showdoc/cr/87-97). Its licensing rules, and its requirement for approvals before a system is installed, altered or dismantled, apply to systems with a total capacity of 2,000 litres or more. That's well above the roughly 900 litre tank beside a typical Moncton, Dieppe or Riverview home, so a normal household tank isn't licensed or tagged by the province the way a fuel depot's system is.

What does apply is the installation code, CSA B139, and your insurance company's checklist. Insurers write their own rules on tank age, material and paperwork, and they vary from company to company, so your broker is the one to ask. The other half of New Brunswick's rules has nothing to do with age. The same regulation says anyone who suspects or finds a petroleum leak from a system has to report it right away, and that section doesn't set a minimum amount. The 24-hour line is 1-800-565-1633. ## Nova Scotia: a licensed technician, not a licensed tank

Nova Scotia splits tanks between two sets of rules, and it trips people up. The province's Fuel Safety section covers ordinary [domestic oil tanks](https://novascotia.ca/lae/fuelsafety/petroleum-domestic/): the page says the tank should be installed by a trained and licensed Oil Burner Technician, working to the latest recognized edition of CSA B139 and the latest National Fire Code of Canada. That page doesn't set an age limit for a household tank, so, as in New Brunswick, expect your insurer to draw that line.

Separately, Nova Scotia Environment's Petroleum Management Regulations cover all underground petroleum tanks and aboveground tanks of 4,000 litres or more. A standard 900 litre heating tank on legs sits well under that, so it isn't part of that registry. A buried tank is a different story, since underground tanks are covered regardless of size.

For spills, Nova Scotia uses the same 24-hour number as New Brunswick and PEI, 1-800-565-1633, per the federal contacts page above.

## Prince Edward Island: inspection, a tag, and an expiry year

PEI runs the strictest system of the three Maritime provinces. Under its Home Heat Tanks Regulations, tanks of 2,200 litres or less used to heat a home or other building are inspected and tagged by a licensed contractor. The province's [Home Heat Tanks FAQ](https://www.princeedwardisland.ca/sites/default/files/publications/home_heat_tanks_-_frequently_asked_questions.pdf) sets out how it works:

- **Tags expire on June 30 of the expiry year.** Non-metallic and double-bottom tanks don't have an expiry year, so in practice this bites older single wall steel tanks.
- **The label decides a lot.** A tank without a ULC or other approved standards label can't be inspected at all, and if the label is unreadable, the tank is treated as past its expiry date and must be replaced.
- **Steel has been phased out.** Since April 1, 2012, new or replacement outdoor tanks have had to be non-metallic, while indoor tanks could be non-metallic or double-bottom steel with a bottom outlet. Since July 1, 2020, every new tank installation has to be non-metallic.
- **Wells.** For new home construction, the FAQ calls for at least 5 metres between the well and a tank of 1,200 litres or less, and 15 metres for anything bigger.

Those are PEI rules, made under PEI's own regulations. They aren't New Brunswick standards, so don't expect a Southeast NB installer to apply PEI's steel ban or well distances by default. They do show the kind of question worth asking locally rather than assuming from another province's rulebook.

## Newfoundland and Labrador: registration is mandatory

Newfoundland and Labrador has regulated heating oil tanks since April 1, 2002, and its Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations, 2003 go a step further than PEI. According to the province's [owner information brochure](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/files/publications-env-protection-host-regs-brochure-20160909.pdf), a licensed inspector has to inspect the tank and rivet a brass registration tag, stamped with an expiry date, to the vent pipe. It's illegal to operate an unregistered tank or to add heating oil to one. Anyone can install the tank itself, but it can't be used until it passes inspection, and the brochure tells owners to check with their insurer before doing the work themselves.

Since July 29, 2015, a single wall steel tank sitting outdoors can't be registered at all, new or existing, and the province adopted the 2015 edition of CSA B139 under its rules on that date. That's a real service-life cutoff, but it's a Newfoundland and Labrador rule. It has no legal force in New Brunswick, and an NB insurer or installer isn't bound by a Newfoundland tag date. Newfoundland also has its own spill number, listed above.

## How the four provinces compare

| Province | Who regulates a typical household tank | Age or expiry rule | Tag or registration | Installation code |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New Brunswick | Provincial licensing only at 2,000 L and up; leak reporting applies to all | None set by the province; insurers set their own | No | CSA B139 |
| Nova Scotia | Fuel Safety (licensed Oil Burner Technician); NS Environment only for underground tanks and aboveground tanks of 4,000 L or more | None published for a household tank; insurers set their own | No, for a typical aboveground household tank | CSA B139, latest edition, plus the National Fire Code |
| Prince Edward Island | Home Heat Tanks Regulations, inspection and tagging by licensed contractors | Steel single wall tags expire June 30 of the expiry year; all new installs non-metallic since July 2020 | Yes, for tanks of 2,200 L or less | CSA B139 plus PEI's own regulations |
| Newfoundland and Labrador | Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations, licensed inspectors | Outdoor single wall steel tanks can't be registered since July 2015 | Yes; illegal to fill an unregistered tank | CSA B139, 2015 edition adopted |

The pattern to notice: Nova Scotia and PEI, the two provinces next door, sit at opposite ends of how much paperwork a homeowner deals with. If you're used to one province's system, don't assume it carries over when you cross a border.

## What this means for a Southeast New Brunswick homeowner

None of this changes the practical questions a homeowner here has. If you've moved from PEI or Newfoundland and you're waiting for someone to tag your tank, nobody will: New Brunswick doesn't tag household tanks. What matters here is whether your tank meets CSA B139 and what your insurer wants to see. A PEI expiry tag or a Newfoundland registration tag carries no legal weight in New Brunswick. Your insurer will ask the same things it asks about any tank in Moncton, Dieppe or Salisbury: how old it is (the ULC label answers that), what it's made of, whether it sits on a stable base with proper clearances, and who installed it.

The same goes for advice you read online. An "Atlantic Canada" guide that tells you tanks expire after a set number of years, or that steel tanks are banned outdoors, is usually describing PEI or Newfoundland. Check which province a rule comes from before you act on it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does New Brunswick set an age limit for oil tanks the way some other provinces do?

No. New Brunswick doesn't tag household tanks or stamp an expiry date on them the way PEI and Newfoundland and Labrador do. The province's petroleum storage licensing applies to systems of 2,000 litres or more, and its leak-reporting rule applies to everyone. Age limits for a household tank come from your insurance company, and they carry real weight, since most mortgage lenders require home insurance.

### Is Nova Scotia's oil tank licence the same thing as PEI's tank tag?

No, and mixing them up causes confusion. Nova Scotia's rule is about who installs the tank: a trained and licensed Oil Burner Technician working to CSA B139. Its environmental tank registry covers underground tanks and aboveground tanks of 4,000 litres or more. PEI instead inspects and tags each household tank of 2,200 litres or less, and steel single wall tags carry an expiry year.

### Does Newfoundland's outdoor steel tank rule apply in New Brunswick?

No. Newfoundland and Labrador's registration system, including the rule that outdoor single wall steel tanks can't be registered since July 2015, was made under that province's own regulations and has no legal force here. A tank in Southeast New Brunswick has to meet CSA B139 and satisfy your insurer. An insurer can still set its own rules on outdoor steel tanks, so ask your broker.

### Who do I call to report a leaking oil tank?

In New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island, the 24-hour environmental emergency line is 1-800-565-1633. In Newfoundland and Labrador, the federal contacts page lists 1-800-563-9089. Stop the leak if you safely can, keep people and pets away, and have the tank looked at next.
