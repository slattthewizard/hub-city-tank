# Buying a House As Is With an Oil Tank: What You Can Still Check

An as-is listing, an estate sale or a power of sale usually means the seller can tell you little or nothing about the oil tank. Nobody may know how old it is, whether it's ever been inspected, or whether there's a second tank in the yard. That doesn't mean you can't buy the house. It means the checking moves to you, and it has to happen before you waive your conditions, because afterward the tank is yours with whatever it comes with.

This guide covers the checks you can still make on a house with no seller knowledge: the tank's age and label, an insurance answer in writing, a look for a buried tank, and a number for the tank decision that goes into your offer. ## What "As Is" Does and Doesn't Mean for the Tank

"As is" says the seller isn't promising anything about the condition of the house. It doesn't change what the tank is, and it doesn't change what an insurer will ask. If the tank leaks after you own the house, the cleanup lands on the property owner, which is you.

Estate and power of sale listings are as-is for a particular reason: the person selling often hasn't lived in the house. An executor may never have been in the basement. A lender selling after a default has never seen it. They can't describe the tank because they don't know it, and they usually won't pay to find out.

Even where a seller fills in a property disclosure statement, an estate that never lived in the house can only answer "unknown" to most of it. What a seller must disclose, and what happens if they don't, is a question for your real estate lawyer, not for us. The practical point holds either way: where the seller can't tell you about the tank, you have to find out for yourself.

## Check 1: The Label and the Tank's Age

Start with the tank. You don't need to be an expert, and you don't need the seller. Ask your agent to get you into the basement or the yard for a few minutes with a flashlight and a phone.

Look for the manufacturer's label or a stamped plate, usually near the top or on one end. It normally lists a build year or a date code and a serial number. Photograph it, the whole tank, the legs and the floor or ground beneath it. Our guide on [how to tell the age of your oil tank](/blog/how-old-is-my-oil-tank/) explains how to read the codes.

What you find sorts into three cases:

- **A readable label with a date.** Good. Send it to your insurer.
- **A label painted over, rusted off or missing.** The insurer may treat the age as unknown, and for many that means old.
- **No tank visible, but signs of oil heat.** A chimney, an old furnace, or patches in the wall. That points to a buried tank, which is a separate check below.

Also look at the condition: rust blisters, weeping seams, staining on the floor, legs sitting on soil, a gauge that's cracked or missing. If the tank looks rough and nobody can say how old it is, assume you'll be replacing it and plan the offer around that.

## Check 2: Get the Insurance Answer in Writing Before You Waive

For an as-is purchase this is the most useful check. Insurers set their own age and condition limits for oil tanks, and there's no way to know whether yours will accept this one except to ask. Our article on [oil tank age and insurance limits](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) explains how those limits tend to work.

Call your broker with the facts you have: the tank's age or lack of a date, the type, whether it's indoors or outdoors, and the photos. Ask directly:

1. Will you insure this house with this tank as it is?
2. If not, what exactly do you need (a new tank, an inspection report, a data plate, an installation record)?
3. How long does the insurer need before closing to process that?

Get the answer by email. If the broker says the tank has to be replaced, you now have the information you need for your offer, which matters more than anything else in this section. If the broker says it's fine, you've removed the single biggest risk on an as-is house.

The insurance industry's standards body, CSIO, publishes a national [Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://www.csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire) for personal lines property insurance. If your insurer sends you something like that form, it's asking for age, location and condition, which is what the label and photos give you.

The Insurance Bureau of Canada notes that [your lender will likely ask for proof of home insurance](https://www.ibc.ca/ns/home/buying-home-insurance) before a home loan goes through, so an insurance answer that comes late can put a closing date at risk. Ask your lender or mortgage broker how they handle it.

## Check 3: Look for a Tank Nobody Mentioned

An as-is seller with no history can't tell you there isn't a buried tank. In some older houses, a buried tank was replaced by an indoor or above-ground one years ago and the old one was never dug up. Our guide on [finding a buried oil tank](/blog/find-buried-oil-tank/) lists the signs: a capped pipe near the foundation, a depression in the lawn, a patched hole low in the basement wall.

Walk the property with those in mind. If you see two or three, ask whether a scan is possible before your conditions expire. Our article on [oil tank scans before buying](/blog/oil-tank-sweep-before-buying/) explains what a sweep can and can't tell you. A scan needs the seller's permission for access, and on an estate or power-of-sale listing that can take time, so ask early.

If you can't get a scan done in the time you have, one option is to negotiate a longer condition period. Another is to buy knowing the question is open and to price that risk into your offer. Which one fits depends on how many signs you saw and what your lawyer advises.

## When to Walk Away, and When Not To

The tank alone is rarely a reason to walk. An old tank is a known cost with a known range, and plenty of houses are worth buying at the price after a tank swap. A few situations deserve a harder look:

- **An unknown-age tank and an insurer who won't confirm in writing**, with no way to extend your conditions.
- **Several signs of a buried tank** and no chance of a scan before you have to decide.
- **Signs of a past leak**, like staining on the floor around the tank or a persistent oil smell, with no paperwork.

In those cases it's reasonable to ask for more time or to price in the worst case. If the answer to both is no and the price doesn't leave room, walking away is a legitimate decision, not a failure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I get insurance on an as-is house with an old oil tank?

Often yes, but it depends on the insurer's own age and condition rules, and it's better to find out before you waive conditions. Call your broker with the tank's age (or the fact it's unknown) and photos, and ask what they require. Get the answer in writing.

### Is the seller required to tell me about the oil tank in New Brunswick?

That's a legal question, and your real estate lawyer is the one to answer it for your purchase. On an estate or power-of-sale listing, expect the seller to know little about the tank either way, and plan your own checks.

### What if there's no label on the tank?

Treat the age as unknown. Many insurers read an unknown age as an old tank, so ask your broker what they'd accept, and plan for a replacement in your offer. A new tank installed with paperwork resolves the question for the insurer.

### Should I ask for an oil tank scan on an estate or power-of-sale house?

If the house is older and you see signs of oil heat in its past with no tank visible, or a capped pipe in the yard, yes. Ask early, because access can take time to arrange. If you can't get one done, price in the uncertainty.

### Can a tank decision be built into my offer?

Yes, and it's common to do so through the price or a condition tied to the insurance answer. Get a written estimate for the work so the figure is real, and talk through the approach with your agent and lawyer.
