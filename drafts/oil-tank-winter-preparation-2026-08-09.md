## Why Oil Tank Winter Preparation Matters More in New Brunswick

Southeast New Brunswick is hard on oil tanks in a way that milder places just aren't. We get deep cold, then a thaw, then deep cold again. That freeze-thaw cycle heaves the ground under tank legs, pops ice into vent caps, and works moisture into every seam and fitting on the system.

Then there's the condensation problem. A tank that sits half empty through a mild fall breathes humid air every time the temperature swings. That moisture condenses on the cold steel walls, drips into the fuel, and settles on the bottom, right where the steel is weakest. Over the years that's what rots outdoor tanks from the inside out.

Cold does one more sneaky thing. Furnace oil starts to thicken, or gel, when an exposed line or filter sits in deep cold long enough. The furnace is fine and the tank is full, but no fuel moves and the house goes cold.

And if you're near the coast, in Shediac or out toward Sackville, salt air speeds up outside corrosion on top of everything else. A tank that would last 20 years in Salisbury might not get there by the shore.

## Your Oil Tank Winter Prep Checklist

### Start with a fall walk-around

Before you buy anything or schedule anything, spend ten minutes with your tank on a dry day in September or October. Bring a rag and your phone for photos.

Look at the tank body first. Surface rust that looks like light freckling is normal on an older steel tank. What you're hunting for is blistering paint, flaking scale, a damp or oily sheen anywhere on the steel, or a dark stain on the ground underneath. A wet spot under the tank is never something to watch and wait on.

Check the legs and the pad next. All four legs should sit flat and solid on a proper base, not on bare soil, patio stones that have shifted, or blocks of wood. A tank that's visibly leaning or has one leg sinking is a winter failure waiting for a frost heave to finish the job.

Now follow the oil line with your eyes from the tank to where it enters the house. It should be protected, ideally sleeved, and not lying where ice will form or where a snowblower can reach it. A copper line pinched under a deck board or draped across the ground is the single most common thing we flag on fall visits.

Finally, check the top of the tank. The fill pipe cap should seat tight. The vent cap should be clear, undamaged, and whistling when the tank gets filled. And test your gauge by giving the outer casing a gentle quarter turn to make sure the float isn't stuck. A stuck gauge in winter is how people run out of oil with a "quarter tank" showing. If yours looks cloudy or doesn't move, our post on [oil tank gauges that stop working](/blog/oil-tank-gauge-not-working/) covers what to do.

### Then work through the full list

Here's the rest, in the order we'd do it. Most of it is free.

1. **Fill the tank before hard cold arrives.** A full tank has almost no air space, which means almost no condensation. It also means you're not begging for a delivery during a February cold snap when every truck in Westmorland County is running behind.

2. **Ask your fuel supplier about cold-weather treatment.** Most deliver winter-blended furnace oil in our region, but if your tank or line is exposed to wind, an anti-gel additive is cheap insurance. Ask before you dump anything in yourself.

3. **Change or check the fuel filter.** A filter half plugged with sludge in October will finish plugging on the first minus 20 night. If your furnace tech does an annual service, make sure the filter is part of it.

4. **Clear and test the vent.** Confirm the vent cap is intact and the whistle sounds during fills. A blocked vent can cause a pressurized fill, and that can split a weak seam.

5. **Mark your tank and fill pipe with tall stakes.** After a couple of nor'easters, your yard is a white blank. Delivery drivers need to find the fill pipe, and your snowblower needs to know where the oil line runs.

6. **Plan your snow shedding.** Look up. If your roofline dumps snow and ice anywhere near the tank, deal with it now, either with snow guards on the roof or a properly built shelter over the tank.

7. **Photograph everything.** Date-stamped photos of a clean, dry, well-supported tank are gold if you ever have an insurance conversation later.

If any single item on that list turns up a problem you can't fix with your hands and a rag, that's the point where the checklist stops being a DIY job. Fuel system work, leg repairs, and line replacement are licensed-trade territory in New Brunswick, and honestly, that's for your protection.

## Watch the Weak Points: Legs, Lines, and Water

Three failures cause most of the winter calls we get. Each one gives you a warning in the fall if you know where to look.

**Legs and support.** Frost heave moves ground, and ground moves tanks. A 910 litre tank weighs close to a tonne when full. If one leg drops even a few centimetres, the whole load shifts, fittings strain, and the oil line can kink or crack. If your tank is on soil or shifting stones, get a proper pad and stand under it before freeze-up, not after.

**The oil line.** Judy in Riverview learned this one the hard way two winters ago. Her tank was fine, but a short stretch of exposed copper line ran through an uninsulated corner of her crawl space entry. On a minus 26 night in January the oil in that line gelled solid, the furnace locked out, and she woke to a house at 9 degrees with pipes on the edge of freezing. The emergency service call, thawing, and a treatment dose came to $340. Sleeving and rerouting that line in the fall would have been a fraction of that, and she'd have slept through the night.

**Water in the tank.** This is the quiet one. Water from condensation sits on the tank bottom feeding corrosion and breeding sludge all winter. You won't see it from outside until it's a pinhole. If your furnace has been sputtering after deliveries or your filter keeps fouling, read our guide on [water in your oil tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/) and have the tank checked before the cold locks in.

## Keep Snow and Ice From Wrecking Your Tank

Snow load and falling ice do real structural damage every winter in Greater Moncton, and it's the most preventable damage there is.

Marcel in Memramcook found that out last February. A metre of accumulated roof snow let go all at once and came down square on his tank's fill pipe and vent. The fill pipe bent, the vent whistle cracked, and his delivery driver, quite rightly, refused to fill the tank until it was repaired. Marcel was under a quarter tank in the coldest week of the year. The repair ran him $480, plus an emergency delivery fee, plus two very stressful days. Snow guards on that section of roof would have cost less than the repair alone.

The rules for snow around your tank are simple. Shovel it away by hand with a plastic shovel, and keep a clear path for the delivery driver. Never chip or pry at ice that's frozen to the tank, the lines, or the fittings, because you can crack a fitting without knowing it until oil is in the snowbank. Never aim a snowblower discharge at the tank or run the machine over the area where your line is buried or sleeved.

Keep the vent and the gauge visible all winter, and after every big storm, do a 30 second check: legs still level, no new dents, vent clear, no smell of oil. We go deeper on this in our post about [protecting your oil tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/).

## When to Put the Shovel Down and Call a Pro

Most of this article is DIY-friendly on purpose. Walking around your tank, shovelling gently, staking the fill pipe, and taking photos are all safely yours. But there's a clear line.

Stop and call a professional if you find any of these:

- Any wet spot, oily sheen, or oil smell around the tank, even a small one
- A tank that's leaning, or a leg that's sinking or lifting
- Weeping, blistering, or a damp line along a seam
- A kinked, corroded, or damaged oil line
- A bent fill pipe or damaged vent after ice or snow impact
- A gauge you can't trust and no way to verify your oil level

Don't tighten fittings on a weeping tank, don't patch anything with epoxy from the hardware store, and don't drain a tank yourself. Oil work in New Brunswick is regulated for good reason, and a well-meaning fix can turn a $200 problem into a contaminated-soil problem.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I keep my oil tank full over the winter?

Yes, as full as practical. A full tank leaves less air space for humid air, which means less condensation and less water settling on the tank bottom where corrosion starts. Topping up before deep cold also means you're not chasing an emergency delivery during a cold snap when suppliers are swamped.

### Can I chip ice off my oil tank or its pipes?

No. Ice frozen to fittings, lines, or the gauge can transfer force straight into the metal, and one crack means oil in your yard. Brush off loose snow with a plastic shovel or broom, keep the vent and gauge visible, and let stubborn ice melt on its own. If ice has already bent or damaged anything, stop and call a professional before the next delivery.

### At what temperature does furnace oil gel?

Untreated furnace oil starts forming wax crystals around minus 9, and exposed lines or filters can plug well before the oil in the tank itself has trouble. Winter blends and additives push that threshold much lower, which is why deliveries in our region are usually blended for the season. Even so, a thin exposed line in the wind is the weak point, so sleeve and protect it.

### How do I know if my tank can handle one more winter?

Age, location, and condition tell the story together. A steel tank past 15 to 20 years, especially outdoors or near salt air, is in its risk years even if it looks fine, because corrosion works from the inside. Blistering, seam stains, a wet spot, or an insurance letter about tank age are all signs to get it assessed now rather than in February.
