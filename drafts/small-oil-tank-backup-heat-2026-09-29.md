# Small Oil Tank for Backup Heat: Does Downsizing Make Sense in Southeast NB?

If a heat pump is doing most of the work now and the oil furnace only kicks in on the coldest nights, the tank sitting in your basement or backyard was sized for a job it no longer has. A 910 litre or 1,150 litre tank made sense when oil carried the whole heating load. It's a fair question whether it still makes sense once oil is backup only.

The short answer: sometimes yes, but it's rarely urgent, and size is only one part of the decision. A smaller tank can mean fresher oil and less wasted floor space. It can also mean more frequent deliveries and a job you didn't need to pay for yet. This is how to think it through before you call [(506) 806-0900](tel:+15068060900) for a free written estimate.

## What "backup only" does to your oil use

A typical home heated only by oil in Southeast New Brunswick burns somewhere between 1,800 and 2,800 litres a year, as we've laid out in our guide to [residential oil tank sizes](/blog/residential-oil-tank-sizes/). Once a heat pump handles most of the season and the furnace only covers the coldest stretches, that number can drop well under 1,000 litres, and in a tight house with a cold-climate heat pump, to a few hundred litres a year. The only way to know your own number is to add up your delivery slips for the past winter or two.

Say you're burning 300 litres a year. Run that against a 910 litre tank and you get a tank that's mostly air for most of the year. It gets filled once, maybe topped up again the following winter, and otherwise just sits there. That's not a problem the tank was built to solve, and it changes the case for its size.

## The smaller tank sizes that exist

If oil use has genuinely dropped, there's real room to go smaller than the standard NB tank. Roth's double wall lineup runs from a 1,500 litre unit through two 1,000 litre models down to a 620 litre model and a compact 400 litre model, per the [manufacturer's product page](https://www.roth-america.com/product/oil-storage-tanks/double-wall-heating-oil-tank/). Granby lists its residential tanks in US gallons: the smallest standard steel model is 120 US gallons (about 455 litres), and its smallest fibreglass models are 180 US gallons (about 680 litres), per [Granby's residential tank page](https://www.granbyindustries.com/petroleum-tanks/categories/residential-oil-tanks/). Watch the units when you compare: a "200 gallon" tank in NB usually means imperial gallons (about 910 litres), while US-gallon figures run about 17% smaller per gallon. We don't sell either brand, but those published specs give you a real sense of what's out there. Our [Granby vs Roth comparison](/blog/granby-vs-roth-oil-tanks/) covers the two head to head if you want the fuller picture.

The 620 litre size is the familiar small tank around here, already common on cottages, mini-homes and small bungalows. It's a comfortable step down from 910 litres without going so small that a single delivery barely fills it. The 400 to 455 litre models suit very light, predictable use, and they make the delivery-minimum question below more pressing.

## What you gain by sizing down

The case for a smaller tank isn't really about the tank itself. It's about what sits inside it.

We've written before about why [keeping an oil tank full over summer](/blog/keep-oil-tank-full-summer/) matters: empty tank space is where condensation forms, and condensation is where water and sludge start. A 910 litre tank holding a few hundred litres of backup fuel spends most of the year mostly empty, which is exactly the setup that breeds the problem. A 620 litre tank holding that same few hundred litres is proportionally fuller, with less exposed steel and less humid air sitting above the oil line. Match the tank to the new, lower burn rate and you're working with the tank's condensation problem instead of against it.

There's a practical upside too. A smaller upright tank has a smaller footprint, which can matter if the old tank has been sitting in a spot you'd rather use for something else, or if a tight basement stairwell made the original tank awkward to get in.

## What you give up: fewer litres, more frequent fills

The trade off is straightforward. Fewer litres in the tank means the same annual usage gets split across more deliveries.

That matters more than it sounds like at first. Many heating oil suppliers set a minimum order size for a delivery truck to make the trip worthwhile, and a tank that's small relative to your usage can bump against that minimum more often than you'd like, or force a fill sooner than the gauge really needed. Ask your supplier directly what their minimum delivery is before you commit to a smaller tank, since it varies by company and isn't something we can quote on their behalf.

There's also a cold weather angle worth being honest about. Backup heat still needs to be there when you need it most: a stretch of extreme cold, or a heat pump that's down for a repair and waiting on parts. (A power outage is a different story. An oil furnace needs electricity for its burner, controls and blower, so it stops too, although it draws far less power than a heat pump and a properly connected generator can often run it.) If the heat pump quits in January, the oil furnace goes back to carrying the whole house, and a 620 litre tank at deep-winter burn rates of 25 to 40 litres a day lasts roughly two to three weeks from full. A smaller tank carries less buffer for those moments. If your backup use is genuinely light and predictable, that's a small risk. If you're not sure how often the furnace still fires, it's worth tracking a winter before you commit to less capacity.

## When keeping your current tank size makes more sense

Downsizing isn't automatically the smart move, and we'd rather tell a homeowner to hold off than sell a job that doesn't fit.

If your current tank isn't due for replacement, has years of service life left, and isn't causing any insurance headaches, there's no rush to swap it just to save on future condensation risk. A summer fill and a periodic visual check, the same routine we recommend for any oil tank, handles a lot of that concern without spending a dollar on a new tank.

Downsizing tends to make the most sense when the two decisions line up anyway: the current tank is old, showing rust, or already flagged by an insurer, and you also know your oil use has settled into backup only territory. In that case, sizing the replacement to match your new, lower usage is a reasonable call to make at the same time you're already paying for a new tank. Swapping a perfectly good tank early, purely to go smaller, rarely pencils out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a smaller oil tank cost less to install than a standard one?

Not automatically. Installed pricing tracks the material (steel or fibreglass) and the complexity of the job, such as access and old tank removal, more than the tank's capacity. Our published ranges are $3,000 to $4,500 for a new steel tank installed and $4,500 to $5,500 for fibreglass. We don't publish a separate rate for smaller tanks.

### What's the smallest residential oil tank made?

Manufacturer specs show tanks as small as 400 litres from Roth's double wall line and 120 US gallons (about 455 litres) in Granby's standard steel line. The 620 litre compact is the more familiar small size in this region, common on cottages and small bungalows. Whether a smaller model makes sense depends on your yearly oil use and your supplier's minimum delivery.

### Will a smaller tank mean I have to order oil more often?

Yes, in most cases, since fewer litres in the tank means the same yearly usage is split across more fills. Check with your supplier about their minimum delivery size before downsizing, because a tank that's too small relative to your usage can end up bumping against that minimum sooner than expected.

### Should I downsize now, or wait until my current tank needs replacing anyway?

If your current tank still has years of service life and no insurance concerns, there's usually no reason to replace it early just to go smaller. Downsizing makes the most sense when it lines up with a replacement you'd be doing anyway, so you're not paying for two tank jobs instead of one.

### Doesn't a smaller tank mean the oil sits and goes bad faster?

It's usually the opposite. A tank sized closer to your actual backup usage stays proportionally fuller through the year than an oversized tank holding the same low volume of oil. Less empty air space above the oil line means less condensation, which is the main driver of water and sludge building up over time.

### Can I just keep my current tank and fill it less instead?

You can, and it's a reasonable choice if the tank is in good shape and not due for replacement. The trade off is that a large tank holding only a small amount of backup oil sits mostly empty most of the year, which is the exact condition that encourages condensation inside the tank. A periodic visual check and a proper summer fill go a long way toward managing that risk in the meantime.
