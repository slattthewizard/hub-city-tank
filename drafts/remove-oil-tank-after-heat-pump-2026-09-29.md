# Remove the Oil Tank After a Heat Pump, or Keep It as Backup?

The heat pump is mounted, the installer is gone, and the oil tank is still sitting exactly where it was. Nobody covered this part in the sales pitch: do you keep the furnace and tank as backup heat, or is this the moment to get rid of oil for good?

Both are reasonable choices, and the right one depends less on the heat pump and more on what you want the tank to do for the next ten years. If you keep it, the tank has to stay in a condition your insurer will accept. If you remove it, there is a short list of things to close out properly so the job does not come back to bite you at renewal time or at closing.

This is the decision itself, not the conversion. ## Why This Decision Gets Skipped

Most of the planning around a heat pump goes into sizing the unit, the electrical panel, and the rebate paperwork. The tank rarely gets a line item, so it just sits there once the work is done, still full or half full, still connected to a furnace that may or may not run again this winter.

That gap matters because an oil tank is not a neutral object once it stops being your main heat source. A tank that is barely used still ages, still holds oil that can go stale, and still shows up on your insurance file. A tank you decide to remove still has oil in it, a fill pipe running to the outside wall, and a fuel supplier who does not automatically know to stop delivering. Both paths need a plan, not just a shrug.

## Option One: Keep the Oil Furnace as Backup

A lot of Southeast New Brunswick homes end up here, especially with a ductless heat pump that heats the main living space but was never sized to carry the whole house on the coldest night. Our [oil heat vs heat pump cost](/blog/oil-heat-vs-heat-pump-cost/) breakdown covers why so many homes run both.

Natural Resources Canada's [heat pump guidance for insurers, agents and brokers](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/sites/admin/files/documents/2025-12/NRCan-IBC-Guidance-FINAL-EN-GoC-Accesibility.pdf) notes that ductless heat pumps do not have integrated backup heating, so they are often installed in homes that keep an existing heating system or add baseboards, while ducted systems typically have backup heat built into the same unit. If your setup is a ductless mini split and your only backup is the oil furnace, that furnace and tank are doing real work on the coldest nights, not just sitting in reserve.

The same document makes a point worth sitting with: in regions like ours, a properly sized cold-climate heat pump can be the only heat source. Its table puts Moncton's historical average at 0.2 days a year below minus 30 degrees Celsius, projected to fall to zero. So before you assume you need to keep oil in the picture, ask whether your heat pump was sized to carry the whole house. If it was, the backup furnace may be more habit than necessity.

If you do keep the furnace and tank, three things follow:

**The tank still needs annual attention.** CSA B139, the national installation code for oil-burning equipment, has maintenance clauses of its own. A [Nova Scotia fuel safety bulletin](https://novascotia.ca/lae/fuelsafety/docs/FSB0906.pdf) summarizing the 2009 edition puts them this way: equipment maintained at least once a year, tanks visually inspected, and tanks in unconditioned spaces or outdoors tested for water and pumped if necessary. That is Nova Scotia's reading of the national code, not a New Brunswick rule, and your service tech can tell you what applies to your setup. The point holds anyway: a tank you only fill once a year does not get a pass on its yearly check just because it burns less oil.

**Water has more time to build up.** A backup tank gets drawn down slowly and can sit part empty for months, and the air space above the oil is where condensation forms. Oil also sits longer before it is burned, so whatever water gets in has time to settle and feed rust and sludge at the bottom. We cover the mechanics in our guide to [keeping an oil tank full over summer](/blog/keep-oil-tank-full-summer/), and the same logic applies to any tank that is now on light duty year round.

**Your insurer needs to know the setup, not guess at it.** If your policy was written around a full-time oil furnace and you are now running mostly on a heat pump, or the reverse, tell your broker what changed. An insurer that thinks your tank is idle when it is really your backup heat source, or vice versa, is a bad surprise waiting for renewal. If your tank is getting old regardless of how much it is used, our post on [how long oil tanks last](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) will help you judge whether to replace it before winter rather than bet on one more season.

Keeping the hybrid setup is not a consolation prize. Plenty of homes run this way permanently and it works fine, as long as the tank gets treated like active equipment instead of a spare part.

## Option Two: Go Fully Electric and Remove the Tank

If the heat pump is carrying the whole house, or you are adding backup electric heat instead of keeping oil, there is no upside to leaving an unused tank in the basement or the yard. It keeps rusting whether or not it burns oil, and an idle tank tends to raise more questions than it answers.

A few pieces need to be handled in order.

**Deal with the leftover oil first.** There is almost always fuel left in the tank. If you know removal is coming, the simplest plan is to stop ordering oil and let the furnace burn the level down over a few cold weeks before the crew arrives. Whatever is left gets pumped out at removal, and what happens to it depends on how clean it is; our guide to [oil tank disposal](/blog/oil-tank-disposal/) covers that part. What you should not do is leave oil sitting in an idle tank with no date to deal with it.

**Tell your insurer, and check what your province expects, before the crew shows up.** Natural Resources Canada's [eligibility page for the Oil to Heat Pump Affordability program](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/home-energy-efficiency/canada-greener-homes-initiative/eligibility-criteria-oil-heat-pump-affordability-program) (the program is now closed to new applications) strongly recommends checking plans to decommission or remove a tank with your home insurance provider and your local authority before work starts, and notes that a province or territory may require removal of a tank that is no longer in use. Our guide to [New Brunswick oil tank regulations](/blog/nb-oil-tank-regulations/) is a good place to start on what applies here. Doing this after the tank is already gone is the wrong order.

**Plan the furnace at the same time.** Once the burner is not going to run again, it needs to be disconnected by whoever services your heating, and it makes sense to have it hauled out around the same time as the tank rather than left standing next to an empty spot on the floor. There is little reason to keep a decommissioned oil furnace around once the tank that fed it is gone.

**The chimney or flue needs a plan of its own.** An oil furnace usually vents through a masonry chimney or a metal flue. Once it is disconnected, that opening should be capped so rain, snow melt, and animals cannot get into the chimney, and so you are not venting heated air out through an open flue all winter. If you keep a wood stove or a gas appliance on a shared chimney, make sure whoever caps the oil side knows what else uses that same flue.

## Keep vs Remove, Side by Side

| | Keep as backup | Remove the tank |
|---|---|---|
| What stays connected | Furnace, tank, and fuel line all stay active | Tank, fill pipe, and vent come out; furnace disconnected |
| Tank attention needed | Yearly check, water check, keep it topped up in the off-season to limit condensation | None once removal is complete |
| Insurer conversation | Confirm how the tank is being used now | Confirm the tank is gone and get it off the policy |
| Chimney or flue | Stays in active use for the oil furnace | Capped once the furnace is disconnected |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to remove my oil tank once I get a heat pump?

Not automatically. A heat pump install does not, on its own, force the tank out. What matters is whether the tank is still doing a job. If it is your backup heat, it needs the same yearly attention any active tank gets. If it is not being used at all, check with your insurer and your local authority before you decide to leave it sitting, because some expect an unused tank to be removed.

### What happens to the oil still in the tank if I remove it?

The easiest approach is to burn it down before removal day. Whatever is left gets pumped out at removal and handled according to its condition, and oil that has picked up water or sludge is not something to reuse or store in jugs. Never dump it or pour it down a drain.

### If I keep my oil furnace as backup, does the tank still need to be inspected?

Yes. The tank does not get a lighter standard just because it is used less. Plan on a check at least once a year, and if it sits in an unheated space or outdoors, have it tested for water and pumped if any has collected. A backup tank sits longer between fills, so water has more time to do damage.

### Will removing my oil tank change my home insurance?

It can. If your policy was priced or written around having an oil tank, letting your insurer know it is gone, with the removal documented, is worth doing. Insurers vary on how they handle this, so ask your broker directly rather than assuming.

### What happens to the chimney once the oil furnace is disconnected?

It gets capped so rain, melting snow, and animals cannot get into the flue and so warm air is not escaping through an open vent. If the same chimney also serves a wood stove or another appliance, only the unused flue gets capped, not the whole chimney.

### How much does it cost to remove a tank after switching to a heat pump?

An outdoor steel tank typically runs $500 to $900 to remove. A tank in a basement or other indoor space is $700 to $1,100 because of the extra handling.
