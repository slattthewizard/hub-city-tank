# Oil Tank Leak Detector: What Each Type Catches, What It Misses, and When the Tank Is the Real Fix

An oil tank leak detector tells you oil has already left the tank. That is useful, because a slow weep can run for weeks before anyone smells it. But a detector does nothing to stop the leak, and it says nothing about a tank that is thinning from the inside and hasn't failed yet. The best detector setups are the ones that catch a leak early and also keep the oil from spreading.

This guide covers the devices you will actually see for home heating oil tanks: the vent whistle and gauge you already have, a drip alarm in a containment pan, a floor sensor, and the sensing space between the walls of a double-wall tank. It also covers what none of them can do, and how to decide between adding a sensor, adding containment, or replacing the tank. ## The Tools You Already Have

Most tanks in Southeast New Brunswick already carry two basic indicators, and neither is a leak detector.

**The gauge** is a float inside the tank that moves an indicator on top. It shows how much oil is left, so a dial that drops faster than your burn rate should explain is a clue. It reads the level at one spot, though, and a stuck float gives a confident wrong number.

**The vent whistle** sounds while a delivery pushes air out of the vent pipe and goes quiet as the tank nears full. It is there to prevent an overfill, not to detect a leak. If you want the full story, see [the oil tank vent whistle](/blog/oil-tank-vent-whistle/).

Beyond those two, your own nose is the original leak detector. The Co-operators' [safety tips on heating your home with oil](https://www.cooperators.ca/en/support/resource-centre/stay-safe/oil-heating) advises checking for an oil smell after each delivery, since that is when spills most often happen. A smell, a sheen on the floor or a damp patch under the tank is the cue to stop and look, and our page on [a wet spot under an oil tank](/blog/wet-spot-under-oil-tank/) explains how to tell a fitting weep from a failing tank.

## The Four Kinds of Leak Detector

### 1. Drip pan or containment pan with an alarm

This is the one most homeowners can use. A shallow pan or tub sits under an indoor tank to catch drips from the filter, the valve and the fittings, and a small sensor in the pan sounds an alarm when oil collects. Nova Scotia's Labour and Advanced Education page on [domestic oil tanks](https://novascotia.ca/lae/fuelsafety/petroleum-domestic/) describes exactly this: release barriers, or drip pans, that catch indoor leaks and "can be installed with an audible alarm or monitored through a home security system." The same page says larger secondary containment units, which it calls tank tubs, are designed to hold 110 percent of the tank's capacity and can go inside or outside a building.

What it catches: drips and slow weeps at fittings and at the bottom of the tank, in the spot where the pan sits. What it misses: oil that runs off the edge of the pan, a rupture bigger than the pan, and anything coming from a part of the system outside the pan, like the oil line running to the furnace.

### 2. A floor sensor or puck

A small sensor sits on the floor beside or under the tank and alarms when it gets wet. It's cheap and easy to place, and also easy to buy wrong. Many household leak sensors work by conductivity, which suits water but not fuel oil, because heating oil doesn't conduct electricity. A sensor sold for a water heater may sit in a puddle of furnace oil and say nothing. If you buy one, check the packaging or the maker's spec sheet for oil or hydrocarbon detection, and don't assume.

What it catches: oil that has already reached the floor at that exact spot. What it misses: a leak that runs the other way across a sloped floor, or into a drain, or out the back of a tank against a wall.

### 3. The space between the walls of a double-wall tank

A double-wall tank has an inner tank for the oil and an outer shell around it. If the inner wall fails, the oil collects between the two instead of on your floor. Many double-wall models have a way to see or sense what's happening in that gap, such as a port, an indicator or a place to fit an alarm. What each model offers differs, so ask for the manufacturer's installation manual for the exact tank before you count on one. Our guide to [double wall oil tanks](/blog/double-wall-oil-tanks/) covers who tends to want one.

What it catches: a failure of the inner wall, early, with the oil still contained. What it misses: a failure of the outer wall on its own, and anything outside the tank like the oil line and fittings.

### 4. A level monitor

A level monitor sends your oil level to a phone. It can flag a sudden unexplained drop, but a slow weep disappears into normal burn. It is a separate device with a separate job, and we cover it in [oil tank monitors and what a level sensor can and can't tell you](/blog/oil-tank-level-monitor/). Treat it as a way to avoid running out of oil, with leak hints as a bonus.

## Where Each Detector Sits in a Real Failure

Say your tank is a 20-year-old steel tank in a Riverview basement, and a seam starts to weep a little at the bottom. Here is what each device would do, as a hypothetical.

| Device | Would it notice? |
|---|---|
| Gauge and vent whistle | No. Neither measures leaks. |
| Your nose | Maybe, once enough oil is on the floor to smell. |
| Level monitor | Probably not. A litre or two a day vanishes into normal burn. |
| Drip pan with alarm under the tank | Yes, if the weep drips into the pan. |
| Oil-rated floor sensor next to the tank | Only once oil spreads far enough to touch it. |
| Double-wall tank with a gap sensor | Yes, if the inner wall is the one that failed. |

The pattern is that the further the sensor sits from the leak, the later it speaks. A pan under the tank speaks sooner than a sensor across the room. A double-wall gap speaks sooner than either. And all of them speak only after oil has left the inner tank.

## What No Detector Can Tell You

A detector can't tell you a steel tank is rusting from the inside. Steel tanks mostly fail from the inside out, where water and sludge sit on the bottom, and by the time the wall lets go the damage is old. Nothing in a pan or on a floor reports that. That kind of condition only shows up when someone looks at the tank, which is why the yearly service matters more than any sensor.

A detector also can't clean up a spill. If it trips and you find oil on the floor or the ground, act on it. Call 911 first if there is any fire risk. Otherwise, New Brunswick asks you to report when you know or have reason to believe oil has been released: the regional Department of Environment and Local Government office during business hours, and the 24-hour environmental emergency line at 1-800-565-1633 after hours. That number is listed for New Brunswick on the federal [report an environmental emergency](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program/report-emergency.html) page. ## Adding a Detector vs Replacing the Tank

Here's how that decision usually breaks down.

**Add a sensor and a pan** if the tank is sound, fairly young and indoors, and the worry is a drip at the filter or a fitting. A pan with an alarm is a small addition to a tank that is otherwise in good shape. It is also a reasonable step if a basement tank sits near a sump or floor drain and you want to hear about a drip early.

**Replace the tank with a double-wall tank** if the tank is old, rusted at the seams or the legs, or if your reason for wanting a detector is that you don't trust the tank. A sensor on a failing tank only tells you when the failure arrives. A new tank with a second wall stops the problem from reaching the floor in the first place.

**Keep the tank and watch it** if it is young and dry and your insurer hasn't asked about it. A detector isn't a requirement for everyone, and spending money on one when the tank is in its first decade is rarely the best use of it.

One caution about the second option. A new tank installed badly is no better than an old one. The base, the legs or stand, the fill and vent pipes and the oil line all need to be right. That is why we look at the whole setup in a free written estimate, not just the tank itself.

## Questions to Ask Before You Buy a Detector

A few checks keep you from paying for a device that doesn't do what you think.

- Does it detect oil, or only water? Look for hydrocarbon or fuel oil on the spec sheet.
- Where does it sit relative to the tank, the filter and the oil line?
- Does it sound locally, send a message, or both? An alarm in a basement does nothing if you're away for a week.
- Who tests it, and how often? A sensor nobody tests can fail silently.
- If your insurer is asking about your tank, does adding a detector change the conversation? Ask them, and don't assume.

That last one matters. Insurers ask about the tank's age and condition, and a sensor may or may not change their answer, so call yours before spending anything.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a leak detector stop an oil tank from leaking?

No. A detector tells you oil has already escaped. It can shorten the time between a leak starting and you finding out, which keeps a cleanup smaller, but it doesn't repair the tank or hold the oil back. Containment, such as a pan or a second wall, is what limits where the oil goes.

### Will a water leak sensor work on heating oil?

Often not. Many household sensors detect water by conductivity, and heating oil doesn't conduct electricity, so the sensor may stay quiet in a pool of oil. Look for a sensor that lists fuel oil or hydrocarbons on its spec sheet, and test it the way the maker describes.

### Can I install an oil tank leak alarm myself?

A pan or floor sensor that just sits near the tank is simple to place. Anything that means opening a fitting on the tank, or changing the oil line, is tank work and should be left to a tradesperson. A badly sealed fitting is its own leak, which defeats the purpose.

### Does a double wall tank need a leak detector?

A double-wall tank already holds oil in the outer shell if the inner tank fails, so the sensor adds early warning rather than basic protection. Many models have a port or indicator for the gap between the walls. Check the manufacturer's manual for your model, and ask what the alarm option is before you count on one.

### Will my insurance company ask for a leak detector?

Some insurers ask about tank age, condition and containment on renewal forms, and the answer varies by company. A detector may help your case, but it won't replace a tank that is past an insurer's age limit. Ask your insurer in writing what they accept, and keep the reply.
