# Oil in a Sump Pump: What It Means and What to Do First

If you've looked into your sump pit and seen a rainbow sheen on the water, or the pump area suddenly smells like diesel, that is not a plumbing problem. It usually means heating oil has left the tank or its line and found its way to the lowest point in your basement, which is exactly where a sump pit sits.

The first move is to stop that pit from sending oily water anywhere else, not to fix the drainage. Then report it, and figure out roughly where the oil is coming from. If you want someone to look at the tank side of the problem, call [(506) 806-0900](tel:+15068060900) and describe what you're seeing.

This guide covers what oil in a sump pump tells you, what to do in the first few minutes, and how New Brunswick's spill reporting applies to a sump pit.

## Why a Sump Pit Is the Wrong Place to Find Oil

A sump pit exists to collect groundwater from the weeping tile and route it away from your foundation before it floods the basement. Under normal conditions, what shows up in there is plain water, maybe with some rust-coloured sediment from the soil or the pipe itself. It shouldn't smell of fuel.

Oil showing up in that pit means it travelled. There are two usual routes. Oil spilled on the floor can run across the slab and straight into an open pit or a floor drain that ties into it. Or oil that leaked below the floor, or outside beside the foundation, follows gravity through cracks, gravel, and the same permeable soil that groundwater uses to reach your weeping tile. The second route is the worse one, because it means oil is already in the soil around or under your house.

That is a different situation from the drip under a tank covered in our guide to [what to do when your oil tank is leaking](/blog/oil-tank-leaking-what-to-do/), and different again from the more common causes covered in [oil smell in the basement](/blog/oil-smell-in-basement/). Both of those usually start on top of the slab. Oil in a sump pit may mean it's moving beneath it.

Here is a quick way to sort out what you're looking at before you assume the worst:

| What's in the pit | Likely cause | How urgent |
|---|---|---|
| Clear water, maybe rust-coloured grit | Normal groundwater and sediment | Not urgent |
| Faint musty or earthy smell, no sheen | Standing water, algae, or debris | Check when convenient |
| A rainbow or oily film floating on the water | Possible heating oil reaching the pit through the floor or soil | Urgent: act now |
| Sharp diesel-like smell with or without visible sheen | Oil in or near the pit | Urgent: act now |

A thin sheen alone isn't proof of fuel. Iron bacteria in groundwater can leave a film that looks oily. The quick check is to poke the film with a stick: an oil sheen swirls and flows back together, a bacterial film breaks into flat plates. If there's a fuel smell with it, treat it as oil.

## The First Thing to Do: Stop Spreading It

Look at where your sump pump discharges. Most homes push that water out through a pipe to the lawn, a ditch, or a connection toward the street. If there's oil in the pit, every cycle of that pump can send oily water outside your house and off your lot, where it becomes harder and more expensive to clean up.

What follows is common-sense safety, not an official procedure, so use judgment for your own basement:

- **Electrical safety first.** Water and electricity don't mix. Don't reach into the pit, and don't touch the pump or its plug while you're standing in water. If the plug is dry and within easy reach from a dry spot, unplug it. If it isn't, switch off the pump's circuit at the electrical panel, and if you're not sure which breaker it is, leave it and get help.
- **If the basement isn't flooding, turn the pump off.** A pit that fills slowly buys you time while the spill is reported and the source is found. Keep an eye on the water level.
- **If turning it off means water will flood the basement,** you have a real trade-off. Tell the people you report to that the pump is running and where it discharges, and ask them how to handle it. Don't try to rig a bucket or a hose to catch the discharge: a running pump moves far more water than you can contain.
- **Don't add anything to the pit.** No detergent, no bleach, no degreaser. Soap breaks oil up into the water so it spreads further and is harder to recover.
- **No flames or sparks nearby.** Heating oil doesn't catch the way gasoline does, but there's no reason to smoke, run a torch, or use a space heater next to it. If there is ever a fire or explosion risk, the province says 911 is the first call.
- **Ventilate and limit time in the room.** Fumes in a closed basement can give you a headache or make you feel unwell. Open a window if you can, keep kids and pets out, and don't stand over the pit longer than you need to.

## Where the Oil Is Probably Coming From

Working backward from a sump pit to the source usually points to one of a few places. A tank that is corroding from the inside, at the seams or the bottom, can weep for a while before anyone notices a puddle. An oil line buried in or under the slab can leak below the floor with little sign at the surface. An outdoor tank beside the foundation can leak into the soil next to the wall, where the weeping tile picks it up. A loose or failed fitting at the filter or valve can drip steadily onto the floor.

Take a careful look (without touching or tightening anything) at the tank itself: the legs, the bottom seam, the filter and valve fittings, and the line running to the burner. New Brunswick's [guidelines on oil tank maintenance and spill prevention](https://www.gnb.ca/en/topic/environment-resources/waste-management/oil-tank-maintenance.html) tell homeowners to watch for dents, cracks, rust blisters, a darkened or stained surface under the tank, and a stronger than normal oil smell. The same page notes that nearly 40% of the oil spills reported to the province each year come from home heating oil tanks, so this is not a rare event.

If you find an obvious source, the [leaking oil tank guide](/blog/oil-tank-leaking-what-to-do/) covers the tank side of the problem. If nothing on the tank looks wrong but the pit still shows oil, the leak may be underground or under the slab, and that needs a professional to trace rather than more guessing.

## Do You Have to Report Oil in a Sump Pump in New Brunswick?

Yes, treat it as reportable. The province's page on [reporting a contaminant release](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/corporate/promo/contaminated-sites-program/reporting.html) says a person who knows or has reason to believe a contaminant has been released into the environment must notify the Department of Environment and Local Government, and that anyone who suspects or detects a leak from a petroleum storage system must notify the department immediately. Oil in a sump pit is a pretty clear reason to believe.

Who you call depends on the time:

- **Fire or explosion risk:** 911 first.
- **Business hours (8:15 a.m. to 4:30 p.m., Monday to Friday):** the department's regional office closest to you. For Greater Moncton that's the Moncton regional office, 506-856-2374.
- **After hours:** the National Environmental Emergencies Centre at 1-800-565-1633. The federal [environmental emergency contacts page](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program/report-emergency.html) lists that number for New Brunswick under the Canadian Coast Guard's Maritimes Regional Office. The province says it is for after-hours reports only, not general questions.

Mention where the sump discharges, especially if it runs to a ditch or toward the street, since oil leaving your property is the part responders will want to deal with first. Reporting is not an admission of fault. It creates a record that you acted promptly, which your insurer and any future buyer will care about far more than a quiet cleanup. Our guide on [reporting an oil spill in New Brunswick](/blog/report-oil-spill-nb/) walks through what to have ready when you call and what happens after.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is oil in my sump pump dangerous?

It's a real problem but usually not a fire emergency. Heating oil is much harder to ignite than gasoline, though you should still keep flames and sparks away, and call 911 if there's ever a fire or explosion risk. Fumes in a closed basement can cause headaches or nausea, so ventilate, keep kids and pets out, and don't linger over the pit. The bigger danger is to the soil, the groundwater, and anything the pump discharges onto.

### Can I keep running my sump pump until someone comes to look?

Not if there's a visible sheen or a strong oil smell and the basement isn't flooding. Running the pump sends whatever is in the pit out through the discharge line, often to a lawn or a ditch. If it's safe to reach the plug from a dry spot, or you know the breaker, turn it off. If stopping it would flood the basement, say so when you report the spill and ask how to handle it.

### Does oil in a sump pit always mean my tank is leaking?

The tank and its oil line are the usual suspects, but not the only ones. A corroded line under the slab, a failed fitting, or an old spill from years ago can all show up the same way, and a bacterial film can look like oil without being fuel. A careful look at the tank is the fastest way to rule it in or out. If nothing there looks wrong, the leak may be underground.

### Do I have to report it if it's only a small amount?

The province's rule is about knowing or having reason to believe a contaminant was released, and anyone who suspects a leak from a petroleum storage system is to notify the department immediately. Oil in a sump pit meets that test. Call the regional office during business hours, or 1-800-565-1633 after hours, and describe what you found.

### What if the sump pump already discharged outside before I noticed?

Report it the same way, and mention that the pump had been running. Note roughly how long it may have been cycling and where the discharge line ends up (lawn, ditch, or toward the street), since that helps whoever follows up know where to look. Past discharge doesn't change what you do now: stop the pump if it's safe, report it, and find the source.
