# Do You Have to Report an Oil Spill in New Brunswick?

Yes, you do. Even if it's a few litres on the basement floor. Even if you already wiped it up. If furnace oil has escaped your tank, you need to report an oil spill in New Brunswick to the province's 24 hour spill line, and making that call quickly is one of the smartest moves you can make.

That answer surprises a lot of homeowners around Moncton. A slow drip under the filter doesn't feel like an "environmental emergency." But heating oil doesn't stay put. It soaks into concrete, finds floor drains, creeps toward sump pits and wells, and the farther it travels, the more the cleanup costs.

So let's walk through it properly. This guide covers who to call, what actually happens after you report, what spills have cost real families around Greater Moncton, and how to make sure you never need the spill line again.

## Yes, You Have to Report an Oil Spill in New Brunswick

New Brunswick's Clean Environment Act treats furnace oil as a contaminant. When a contaminant gets loose, whether it dripped, spilled, or overflowed during a fill, the release is supposed to be reported to the province. There's no handy "under 10 litres doesn't count" exemption for homeowners. If oil got somewhere it shouldn't be, the safe assumption is that it's reportable.

Here's the part that matters just as much: reporting is not confessing to a crime. Homeowners don't get punished for having a tank fail on them. The trouble starts when a spill is hidden or ignored, because an unreported spill that spreads can bring cleanup orders, much bigger bills, and serious problems with your insurer and any future sale of the house.

Think of the report as your paper trail. It proves you acted responsibly the day you found the problem. Insurance adjusters, environmental consultants, and future buyers all respond a lot better to "we reported it right away" than to "we kept it quiet."

## The Number to Call to Report an Oil Spill in New Brunswick

For any spill in New Brunswick, the reporting number is 1-800-565-1633. It's answered 24 hours a day by the [Canadian Coast Guard](https://www.ccg-gcc.gc.ca/) regional operations centre, which takes spill reports for the Maritimes and notifies the New Brunswick [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg.html). When you dial the spill line, have a few basics ready:

- Your name, address, and a callback number
- What spilled (home heating oil, also called furnace oil)
- Roughly how much, even a guess like "less than 20 litres"
- Where it went: concrete floor, dirt crawl space, lawn, driveway, near a well or ditch
- Whether it's still leaking

Don't wait until you have perfect information. "My tank is dripping and I don't know how long it's been going" is a perfectly good report. The operator will ask questions to fill in the rest.

One more wrinkle worth knowing: if the spill happened during a fuel delivery, the delivery company has its own reporting duty. Don't assume they've handled it, though. Make the call yourself anyway, because it costs nothing, and if there's ever a dispute about what happened, your report is on the record with your version of events.

## What Happens After You Make the Call

For a small, contained spill, usually less drama than people fear. The operator logs the details and passes them to the province. If you've stopped the leak and cleaned up a minor spill on bare concrete properly, the follow-up may be a phone call from an inspector confirming what happened and how you handled it.

If oil reached soil, a drain, or water, expect more structure. The province will want the contamination assessed by a qualified environmental consultant, tested, cleaned up to the standards used across Atlantic Canada, and documented in a closure report. That paperwork protects you as much as the environment. It's exactly what future buyers, lenders, and lawyers will ask to see.

Your second call should be to your insurance company, the same day. Policies treat escaped fuel oil very differently from one insurer to the next, and late notice is one of the most common reasons claims get complicated. While everything is fresh, take photos of the tank, the spill, and the room. Dig out fuel delivery receipts and any old tank paperwork. Adjusters and consultants will want all of it, and a thick file of evidence tends to speed things up rather than slow them down.

## What to Do in the First 30 Minutes

Before the professionals arrive, a few careful steps can shrink the problem. A few careless ones can grow it.

1. **Stop the flow if it's safe.** Close the valve at the bottom of the tank if you can reach it without standing in oil.
2. **Kill ignition sources.** No smoking, no candles, no sparking tools near the spill. Furnace oil is hard to ignite, but don't test that.
3. **Contain what you can.** Kitty litter, absorbent pads, even old towels around the edge of the puddle stop it spreading toward drains.
4. **Never rinse it.** Hosing oil toward a floor drain or into the yard turns a small spill into a big one.
5. **Ventilate.** Crack windows and get fresh air moving. Oil fumes cause headaches and nausea in closed spaces.
6. **Make your calls.** The spill line at 1-800-565-1633, your insurer, and a tank professional.

Know where DIY ends, too. If oil has reached soil, a drain, or a sump pit, if the fumes are strong enough to give you a headache, or if the puddle is bigger than a cookie sheet, stop and let professionals take over. Cleanup at that scale needs equipment and disposal methods no homeowner has in the garage. Keep kids and pets out of the area until it's cleaned and aired out. Soaked rags and absorbent go into sealed containers, not the regular garbage, and your cleanup contractor can dispose of them properly.

## How to Stay Off the Spill Line for Good

Almost every spill call we take traces back to the same root cause: a steel tank that was quietly rusting past the end of its service life. Single-wall steel tanks in our climate typically last 15 to 25 years, and the ones near the coast, in Shediac or anywhere the salt air reaches, often sit at the short end of that range.

A five-minute look each season goes a long way. Check the legs for rust and settling, run a hand under the bottom seam feeling for dampness, and look for blistered paint, weeping around fittings, and any oil smell that wasn't there before. The bottom of the tank rusts from the inside out, so by the time you see a wet spot outside, the steel is already thin.

Outdoor tanks deserve extra attention in our climate. Snow sliding off a roof can shear a fill pipe, ice can heave a tank off its stand, and freeze-thaw cycles work on rusted legs all winter long. If your tank sits under a roofline, a simple snow deflector or a relocated tank can prevent the exact failure that fills the spill line every March.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to report a spill of just a few litres?

Yes. New Brunswick doesn't set a household minimum, and small spills are often bigger than they look because oil that reached a crack or drain is out of sight. The call takes five minutes and creates a record that protects you. If the spill truly was minor and cleaned up properly, that's usually where it ends.

### Will I be fined for reporting an oil spill?

Reporting itself doesn't trigger a fine. The province's goal is making sure spills get contained and cleaned up, not punishing homeowners whose tanks failed. Penalties and cleanup orders come into play when someone hides a spill or refuses to deal with it. Prompt reporting keeps you firmly on the right side of that line.

### Who pays for cleaning up a heating oil spill?

The property owner is responsible for the cleanup, which is why your insurance policy matters so much. Many home policies include some coverage for escaped fuel oil, but limits and conditions vary widely between insurers. Late reporting is one of the most common reasons claims go sideways, so call your insurer the same day you call the spill line.

### What if I discover an old spill from years ago?

It still needs to be reported and assessed, even if the tank that caused it is long gone. Old contamination usually surfaces during renovations or a house sale, and it never goes away on its own. A professional assessment tells you how far the oil travelled and what closure will take. The sooner you know, the more options you have.
