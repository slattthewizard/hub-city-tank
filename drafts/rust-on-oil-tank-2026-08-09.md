# Rust on Your Oil Tank: Harmless Patina or Warning Sign?

Here's the uncomfortable truth about rust on an oil tank: the rust that kills a tank is almost never the rust you can see. Steel heating oil tanks usually corrode from the inside out, so that orange patch you noticed beside the driveway could be nothing at all, or it could be the last visible warning before 900 litres of furnace oil ends up in your basement or your soil.

So which is it? That's what this guide is for. ## Harmless Patina or Warning Sign? How to Read Rust on an Oil Tank

Not all rust is equal. Here's how it typically progresses on a residential tank, from harmless to urgent.

**Light surface bloom.** A thin orange dusting sitting on top of the paint, usually where the coating got scratched or chipped. It wipes off, and the metal underneath is smooth and solid. This is the "patina" end of the scale. Take a photo, keep an eye on it, but it's usually cosmetic.

**Flaking scale.** Rust that has built up in crusty layers and lifts off in flakes. Underneath, you'll often find pitting, little craters in the steel. Pitting means the tank wall is actually getting thinner. This is no longer cosmetic. It's a countdown.

**Blistering.** Bubbles forming under the paint, especially along the bottom of the tank or the bottom seam. Blistering often means corrosion is pushing through from the inside, and the paint film is doing more structural work than the steel behind it.

**Weeping.** A damp, oily sheen at a rust spot, a slow drip, or a wet ring on the floor or pad below the tank. This is a failing tank, full stop. Stop reading and call someone today.

Location matters as much as texture. Rust on the top or upper sides is usually the least urgent kind. Rust along the bottom third of the tank, the bottom seam, the legs, or around the fittings and oil line is where tanks actually fail.

## The Rust You Can't See: Why Tanks Fail From the Inside Out

The most dangerous corrosion happens where you'll never spot it: inside the tank, right at the bottom.

Every time warm, humid air enters a partly empty tank, moisture condenses on the cold steel walls. Water is heavier than heating oil, so it sinks and pools at the lowest point of the tank. That thin layer of water then sits against the same patch of steel for years, quietly eating through it. Bacteria that live at the boundary between the water and the oil make things worse by producing acidic sludge that speeds up the attack.

This is why tanks so often fail at the bottom, and why a tank can look presentable on the outside while the floor of it is lace. It's also why exterior rust along the bottom seam is such a serious signal. By the time inside-out corrosion shows on the outside, there isn't much steel left in between.

A few habits slow the process down. Keeping the tank topped up through summer leaves less room for humid air to condense. Checking for water in the tank once a year helps too, and we cover how in our guide to [water in your oil tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/). Age, though, is the biggest factor of all. A single-wall steel tank typically lasts 15 to 20 years, and the odds of internal corrosion climb steeply after that. ## A 10-Minute Rusty Tank Check You Can Do This Weekend

Grab your phone and a flashlight. Here's the check we'd do at your place, in the order we'd do it.

1. **Start at the bottom third.** That's where tanks fail. You're looking for scale, blistering, or staining, not the light bloom up top.
2. **Trace the bottom seam.** Run the flashlight along the weld at the base of the tank. Blisters or crusty buildup here are the single biggest red flag on any oil tank.
3. **Feel underneath.** With a gloved hand, reach under the tank and feel for dampness, soft blisters, or an oily film you can't see from above.
4. **Check the legs and pad.** Rust where the legs meet the tank, a cracked or heaving pad, or any noticeable lean puts extra stress on that bottom seam.
5. **Inspect the fittings.** Look at the fill pipe, vent, gauge, filter, and the spot where the oil line leaves the tank. Rust plus a wet look at any fitting deserves a phone call.
6. **Look at the ground.** Stains on the concrete, a patch of dead grass, or an oil smell means you may already have a slow leak.
7. **Photograph everything with the date.** You'll want the comparison next season, and your insurer may want the photos too.

Just as important, know where DIY ends. Don't sand, grind, chip, or pressure-wash heavy rust to "see how bad it is", and never crank on a weeping fitting with a wrench. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is rust on an oil tank always a bad sign?

No. A light orange bloom on the paint with smooth, solid metal underneath is common and usually cosmetic, especially on older outdoor tanks. What matters is texture and location. Flaking, pitting, or blistering along the bottom third of the tank or its seams is the pattern that actually leads to leaks.

### Can I repair a rusty oil tank instead of replacing it?

Not safely, once rust has caused pitting or blistering. Patching or welding a corroded residential oil tank isn't accepted practice, and insurers won't recognize it. Sealants and rust paints only hide the problem while the inside keeps thinning. If the wall is compromised, replacement is the only fix that protects your home and your coverage.

### How can I tell if rust has gone through the tank wall?

You usually can't tell by looking, which is exactly the problem. The signs that failure is close include blistered paint at the bottom seam, a damp or oily film at rust spots, an oil smell near the tank, or staining on the floor or ground underneath. Any one of those means the wall is at or near the end, so treat it as urgent and get a professional assessment right away.

### Will my insurance company cancel me over rust on my oil tank?

They can decline to renew if the tank looks corroded or is past their age limit, and photo requests at renewal are now common across New Brunswick. Most insurers give you 30 to 60 days to replace the tank before anything happens to your policy. Handled promptly, it's almost always resolved with a new tank and installation paperwork, not a cancellation.
