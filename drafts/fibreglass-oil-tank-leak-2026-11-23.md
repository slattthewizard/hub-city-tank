# Can a Fibreglass Oil Tank Leak? What Fails, What to Check, and Repair or Replace

Yes, a fibreglass oil tank can leak. It can't rust, and that removes the most common way steel tanks fail, but it isn't leak-proof. Fibreglass fails differently: a crack from a hard knock, a fitting that weeps, a base that settles and puts a load on one spot, or a tank that was installed outside its maker's instructions. Most of those leave warning signs you can see, which is the useful part.

This guide covers where fibreglass tanks leak, how to check yours in a few minutes, and how to decide between fixing a fitting and replacing the tank. It doesn't compare cost or lifespan, because our [steel vs fibreglass oil tank comparison](/blog/steel-vs-fiberglass-oil-tank/) already does that. ## Why fibreglass tanks don't rust but can still fail

Steel tanks usually fail from the inside out, as condensation and sludge corrode the bottom. Fibreglass has no steel to corrode, so that failure is gone. What's left are the things that happen to any tank:

- Something hits it, or presses on it.
- Something it's connected to fails.
- Whatever it's sitting on moves.
- It was installed in a way the maker doesn't allow.

Granby's own warranty paper reflects this. Granby's [limited warranty certificate for its non-metallic tanks](https://res.cloudinary.com/wholesale-plumbing/image/upload/Documents3/GRANBY_961226_Warranty_Information.pdf), which covers its fibreglass and 2-in-1 lines for tanks made, sold and installed in Canada after 31 March 2023, covers leaks due to defects in materials or workmanship in manufacturing. It excludes damage from accidents, floods, neglect, unauthorized alteration or repair, faulty installation, and overfill spills. (This is Granby's document, hosted as a copy by a distributor, and your own tank's certificate is the one that governs.) So a leak from a knock or a bad install isn't a warranty matter. It's an owner's matter, and often an insurance one.

## Where a fibreglass tank leaks

### Impact damage

A rigid shell is more likely to crack than to dent. A tank beside a driveway, a shed door, or a snow-clearing route can get hit by a shovel, a plough blade or a vehicle. Under a roof edge, falling snow and ice can do it too. Granby's installation guidelines for its steel tanks say that if possible tanks shouldn't sit directly under house eaves where they may be subject to falling snow and ice or dripping water, and that protection should be provided if there's no other place for them. The same logic applies to a fibreglass shell, and arguably more so. Our guide to [protecting an oil tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/) covers what to clear and why.

What an impact looks like: a visible crack or split, a star-shaped or web-like pattern of fine lines, whitening around a spot, or a patch where the surface has gone dull or flaky. Fine lines on the outer surface aren't always a leak, but they're not something to leave alone either. Treat any new mark that appeared after a knock as a reason to look closer.

### Fittings, bungs and the outlet

On many tanks, the weak points are where things connect: the fill and vent openings, the gauge, the oil line outlet, the filter and the valve. If a fitting isn't seated or sealed properly, or has been bumped, oil can weep from there while the shell is perfectly sound. A leak at a fitting is often the least serious kind, because it's a part that can be replaced.

One detail from Granby's certificate shows why outlets matter. It says the warranty doesn't cover seepage, dripping or leakage from the supply outlet at the bottom of the tank if that outlet is capped or plugged when the tank feeds the burner from a connection on top. In other words, how the outlet is used is part of the install, and a bottom outlet that's been plugged isn't something to ignore.

### A base that settles or heaves

A tank is meant to sit on a base that's level and rigid. Granby's installation guidelines for steel tanks say the tank should be supported to keep it from shifting, settling or falling over, and that all tanks are at risk from base movement, especially new outside installations placed on recently disturbed ground. They recommend a well-drained base with compacted crushed stone for an outdoor tank. The same principle holds for any tank material.

Freeze-thaw cycles matter here in Southeast New Brunswick. Ground that heaves in a hard winter or softens in a wet spring can tilt a pad. If the support is uneven, the weight sits on a small area of the shell instead of spreading across it. A rigid material doesn't flex to take that load. A tilted or sinking tank is a clear sign something's wrong with the base. Our guide to [oil tank stands and pads](/blog/oil-tank-stand-and-pad/) explains why the base matters as much as the tank.

### Installation outside the maker's instructions

Granby's installation instructions for its 2-in-1 tank (a polyethylene inner tank in a steel jacket, not fibreglass, but from the same maker) say not to load or stand on top of the tank or its cover, and not to install the tank if there's physical damage that could affect its integrity. Fibreglass tanks come with their own manuals, and those are the ones to follow. If something has been stacked on, leaned against or strapped to your tank, take it off and look at what's underneath.

## How to check a fibreglass oil tank

A look every few months, and after any storm, plough pass or knock, takes about ten minutes. For fibreglass, look at these:

1. **Walk all the way around it.** Look at the whole shell, including the sides facing the wall or the fence. Granby's guidelines say a tank should be placed where it can be visually inspected from all sides, so if you can't see one side, that's a placement problem as well.
2. **Look for cracks, splits, whitening and bulges.** Natural Resources Wales has a [household oil tank leak check](https://naturalresources.wales/guidance-and-advice/environmental-topics/waste-management/check-your-household-oil-tank-for-leaks/?lang=en) that tells owners of plastic tanks to look for whitening, cracking and splits and for bulging or deformation of the tank profile. That page is about plastic tanks in the UK, not fibreglass in New Brunswick, but the same signs of stress are worth looking for on any non-metal shell.
3. **Check the base.** The same page says to check the tank base or supports for cracking or subsidence. Look for a tilt, a gap under a leg, a cracked pad, or a pad that's gone soft or uneven.
4. **Check every fitting.** Look at the fill, vent, gauge, outlet, valve and filter for staining, a damp film or a drip.
5. **Wipe and wait.** Dry a suspect spot with a paper towel and check it again later. Oil is easy to tell from condensation by the smell and the sheen. Our guide to [a wet spot under an oil tank](/blog/wet-spot-under-oil-tank/) walks through that test.
6. **Look at the surroundings.** Staining on the pad, oil on the floor, stressed grass or an oil smell near an outdoor tank all point to a leak that hasn't shown on the tank yet.

If you can't see any damage and everything is dry, you're fine. A fibreglass tank in good condition on a good base doesn't need more than regular checks.

## Repair or replace?

This is where the leak's source decides everything.

**A fitting or a part is leaking.** If the oil is coming from a filter, valve, gauge, gasket or line connection and the shell is dry and sound, this is a repair. The part gets replaced, the leak stops, and the tank carries on. Our guide on [whether a leaking oil tank can be repaired](/blog/oil-tank-repair-or-replace/) has more on how to tell a part leak from a shell leak.

**The shell is cracked or weeping.** We don't know of an approved field repair for a leaking fibreglass home heating tank, and we wouldn't put oil back in a tank with a cracked shell. Granby's certificate says that if a covered leak happens, the maker will replace, repair or credit the tank through an authorized distributor, with written prior authorization. That's the maker's route for a covered defect, not a patch job on a tank at your house. The Natural Resources Wales guidance for plastic tanks goes further and says plastic tanks can't be repaired. For fibreglass in New Brunswick, treat a cracked shell as a replacement decision unless the maker tells you otherwise in writing.

**The base has moved.** If the tank is sound but the base has settled, the fix is to re-set it on a proper base. That means emptying it and moving it, which a tank owner shouldn't do alone. Be aware that Granby's certificate says the warranty doesn't apply to a tank that has been taken out of service and reinstalled at a different location. Moving it a short way on the same site is a question to ask the maker before it's done.

**You can't tell.** If there's damage you can't classify, a tank that's lost oil, or a mark you're not sure about, get someone to look at it before you fill it again.

## If a fibreglass tank is leaking now

Stop the flow if you can do it safely: close the valve at the tank. Contain what's escaping with a pan or absorbent material, keep ignition sources away, and open windows if the tank is inside. Don't try to patch a shell with tape, epoxy or anything from a hardware store. ## The decision

For most owners it comes down to this:

- **Dry shell, dry fittings, solid base:** keep the tank, and check it a few times a year and after any knock or storm.
- **A weeping fitting or a worn part:** repair the part, then watch the spot.
- **A cracked shell, a bulge or a base that's moved:** replace the tank or re-set it, rather than patching or topping it up.
- **Damage you can't read:** get a second opinion before the next delivery.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a fibreglass oil tank rust?

No, there's no steel in the shell to rust. That doesn't make it maintenance-free. The fittings, the oil line and the base can still fail, and a hard knock can crack the shell. A fibreglass tank on a double-wall or steel-jacketed design may also have steel parts that can corrode.

### Can a cracked fibreglass oil tank be repaired?

We don't know of an approved field repair for a cracked heating oil tank shell, and we wouldn't recommend one. Granby's warranty describes its own remedy as replacement, repair or credit through a distributor for a covered defect. If your shell is cracked, plan on replacement and ask the maker before anyone attempts a fix.

### Where do fibreglass oil tanks usually leak?

Fittings and connections are a common place: the filter, valve, gauge, outlet and line connection. Impact damage and an uneven base are the other main causes. Dry the area and watch where the first bead of oil forms, since oil runs downhill and the puddle isn't always under the source.

### Is a fibreglass tank leak covered by the manufacturer's warranty?

Only if the leak comes from a defect in materials or workmanship in making the tank, and only if the tank was installed and maintained as the maker requires. Granby's certificate excludes accidents, faulty installation, neglect, unauthorized repairs and overfill spills. Read the certificate for your own tank.

### Does my insurance cover a leak from a fibreglass tank?

It depends on the policy, and many exclude some kinds of oil damage or gradual leaks. Don't assume. Ask your insurer in writing what's covered for an oil tank leak, and what they need to see about the tank's type, age and installation.
