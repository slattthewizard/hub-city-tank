# What to Do With Leftover Heating Oil When Your Tank Is Coming Out

If there's still oil in your tank and you're replacing it, the clean oil can usually be pumped out and put back into the new tank, so you're not paying for fuel twice. If you're dropping oil heat for a heat pump or electric heat, the best move, if your timeline allows it, is to burn the oil down before the tank comes out. Whatever's left at removal day, plus the water and sludge at the bottom, gets pumped out by whoever removes the tank. Either way, don't try to drain it yourself.

What you shouldn't do is let a part-full tank sit disconnected for months while you decide, or start moving the oil into pails and jerry cans on your own. This guide walks through what happens to leftover oil in each situation, and the few things worth avoiding.

## How Much Oil Is Really Left When You Think the Tank Is Empty

A gauge that reads near empty is not the same as a tank with nothing in it. The burner never pulls a tank completely dry. On many tanks the supply line draws from a little above the floor, and even where it doesn't, water and sludge settle on the bottom and stay there. How much is left behind depends on the tank, where its outlet is, and how level it sits.

This matters for two reasons. First, the clean oil above that bottom layer is fuel you paid for, and you can reasonably expect to use it or move it. Second, the bottom layer is not something to scoop out with a shop vac or a bucket. It's a mix of oil, water from condensation, and rust and sediment, and it gets pumped out and sent for proper disposal along with the rest of the tank's contents once a crew starts the job.

## If You Are Replacing the Tank: The Oil Usually Moves With You

This is the straightforward case. When a tank is being swapped for a new one, on the same spot or nearby, the leftover oil is your existing fuel supply, not waste.

A crew pumps the oil out first, holds it in clean drums, and pumps it back into the new tank through a filter once it's set and levelled, pulling from above the bottom of the old tank so the sludge and water stay out of your fuel. Our [oil tank replacement process](/blog/oil-tank-replacement-process/) walks through that step in the order it happens on a job.

A few things affect how much of your oil comes back:

- **Clean oil transfers.** If it looks and smells like normal furnace oil, it goes back in.
- **Water and sediment stay out.** Anything pulled from near the tank bottom is treated as contaminated and goes for disposal instead.
- **A nearly full tank takes longer to pump**, twice over, since it has to come out and then go back in.

## If You Are Dropping Oil Heat Entirely: Your Options Are Narrower

Converting to a heat pump or switching to electric heat is a different situation, because there's no new oil tank waiting to take the fuel back. In practice you have two paths, and most people use both.

**Burn it down before the tank comes out.** This is the sensible default. If the heat pump goes in while the furnace is still working, keep burning oil until the tank is close to empty, then book the removal. The oil you already paid for gets used for what you bought it for, and there's less to pump out on the day. A few practical points:

- **Stop your deliveries.** If you're on automatic delivery, cancel it as soon as you've decided to convert, so a truck doesn't top up a tank you're about to remove.
- **Plan around the season.** Oil burns down fastest in the heating season, and more slowly once a new heat pump is carrying most of the load. If the conversion happens in late spring, burning down may mean the tank sits part-full until fall. Weigh that against booking the removal sooner and having more oil pumped out, and keep checking the tank for weeps and smells while it waits.
- **Don't run it bone dry on purpose.** Running a tank all the way out can pull sludge into the filter and leave you with no heat. Aim for low, not empty, and let the crew pump the rest. Our post on [running out of heating oil](/blog/ran-out-of-heating-oil/) covers what happens if you overshoot.

**Have the rest pumped out at removal.** Whatever's left when the tank comes out gets pumped and handled by the crew doing the removal, the same way it would be for a replacement, and the bottom layer goes for disposal. Ask what the estimate includes for the oil and how it's handled, so there's no question on the day.

## What Not to Do With Leftover Heating Oil

A few habits come up often enough that they are worth calling out directly.

**Don't dump it.** Not on the ground, not down a floor drain, not into a septic system or a storm drain. Heating oil in soil or water is a contaminant release, and in New Brunswick a release has to be reported once you know or have reason to believe it happened. A few litres in the wrong place can mean soil testing and a cleanup bill far bigger than the value of the oil.

**Don't store it loosely and wait.** Moving oil into open pails, jerry cans or a drum in the corner of the basement "until you figure out what to do with it" turns oil that was sitting safely in a tank into a spill risk every time a container gets bumped, knocked over or forgotten. It also puts fuel vapour into your living space. Leave it in the tank until it's burned or pumped out.

**Don't burn it in a barrel or use it as a solvent.** Burning it anywhere but a properly installed oil appliance is a fire hazard, and using it to clean parts or kill weeds is just a slower way of putting it on the ground.

**Don't leave a half-full tank sitting indefinitely.** A tank that's disconnected from a working furnace, or slated for removal but keeps getting pushed back, can develop a slow leak nobody notices for months. The code agrees: [CSA Group's summary of the 10th edition of CSA B139](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/), the installation code for oil-burning equipment, lists new requirements for removing a tank system that hasn't been used for more than three years. If your tank already shows a wet spot or a smell, treat it as urgent; our post on [what to do about a leaking oil tank](/blog/oil-tank-leaking-what-to-do/) has the first steps.

If oil ever does end up on the ground or in water, whatever the source, report it. Call 911 first if there's any fire or explosion risk. During business hours, New Brunswick's rules point you to the regional Department of Environment and Local Government office. After hours, use the 24-hour environmental emergency number for New Brunswick, 1-800-565-1633, answered by the Canadian Coast Guard's Maritimes regional office according to the federal [report an environmental emergency](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program/report-emergency.html) page. ## When the Oil Itself Is the Problem, Not Just the Tank

Sometimes the leftover oil is not worth saving no matter how the tank comes out. Oil that has sat for years, especially in an outdoor tank through several New Brunswick winters, can pick up water through condensation and settle sediment at the bottom. Oil in that state doesn't transfer cleanly and can clog a filter, and whoever pumps it out should tell you if it isn't worth keeping.

That is one more reason not to treat "there's still oil in there" as a reason to put off dealing with the tank. Old, degraded oil sitting in an old tank is a worse combination than either one on its own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I pour leftover heating oil down a drain or onto the ground?

No. Heating oil in soil, a drain or a waterway is a contaminant release, and New Brunswick expects a release to be reported once you know or have reason to believe it happened. If oil ever ends up somewhere it shouldn't be, report it rather than quietly cleaning it up or covering it over. After hours, the 24-hour line for New Brunswick is 1-800-565-1633.

### What happens to the oil in my tank when it gets replaced?

In most cases it gets pumped into sealed drums, held during the swap, and returned to the new tank through a filter once it is installed. Clean oil goes back in. Anything pulled from the sludge and water layer at the old tank's bottom is kept out and handled as waste separately.

### Can I keep leftover oil in a container until I decide what to do with it?

It's not a good idea. Oil in an open pail, a jerry can or a drum sitting in the basement is far easier to spill than oil in a tank, and it puts fuel vapour into the house. Leave it in the tank until it's burned down or pumped out. If a tank is coming out, plan for the oil to be handled as part of that job rather than moved somewhere "temporary."

### I'm switching to a heat pump. What happens to the oil that's left?

If the furnace still works, burn the oil down first: keep heating with oil until the tank is low, cancel any automatic delivery, then book the removal. That uses the fuel you already paid for. Whatever is left on removal day, including the water and sludge at the bottom, gets pumped out and handled by the crew removing the tank. Aim for low, not empty, so you don't pull sludge into the filter.

### Is old heating oil still safe to use?

Sometimes, but not always. Oil that has sat for a long time, particularly outdoors through winter, can pick up water and settle sediment, which burns poorly and can clog a filter or line. A technician looking at the tank during a replacement or removal visit can tell you whether what is left is worth transferring or should just go for disposal.

### Do I need to do anything special before a crew comes to deal with a tank that still has oil in it?

Not much. Mention roughly how much oil you think is left when you book, clear a path to the tank, and leave the actual pumping, disconnecting, and handling to the crew. Moving or draining the oil yourself beforehand does not save time and adds risk for no reason.
