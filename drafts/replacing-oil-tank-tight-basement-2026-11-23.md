# Replacing a Basement Oil Tank When the Stairs and Doors Are Tight

If your basement stairs are narrow, the new tank is the hard part, not the old one. An old steel tank that won't come out whole can be opened up and taken out in sections. A new tank can't. [Roth's installation manual](https://www.roth-america.com/app/uploads/2021/02/DWT_Installation_Manual_2023.pdf) for its double-wall line says the tank must fit through every door, stair, hall and turn on the way to its spot, and that it can't be dismantled or altered to squeeze through. So the job starts with a tape measure, not a catalogue.

Your realistic choices are a narrower or lower tank, two smaller tanks that each fit, or putting the new tank outside. ## Why the new tank is the tight part

In a lot of older houses, the tank went in before the basement was finished, sometimes before the stairs were even enclosed. Years later, a finished stairwell, a new door frame or a duct run can leave less room than the original tank needed on its way in.

Removal has its own tricks, which we cover in our guide to [basement oil tank removal](/blog/basement-oil-tank-removal/): the tank is pumped out and then either carried out whole or opened and taken out in pieces. None of that helps the replacement. The new tank arrives sealed and finished, and its warranty and its leak protection depend on it staying that way.

## Measure the path before you pick a tank

Walk the route from the outside door to the exact spot where the tank will sit. Write down:

1. **The narrowest clear opening.** Measure between the door stops or the frame, with the door swung open as far as it goes or lifted off its hinges if that is practical. The frame measurement isn't the clear width.
2. **Stair width and height.** Measure wall to wall at the narrowest point, including handrails, and the headroom at the top and bottom, where beams, ducts and stair headers like to hide.
3. **Every turn.** At a landing, the tank's length swings around a corner. A tank that fits a straight stairwell can still jam at a turn.
4. **The final spot.** Check ceiling height at the spot, not only along the route, and what sits beside it: the furnace, a wall, a floor drain.
5. **The floor.** Look at the slab where the tank will stand. An old, thin or uneven slab is a bigger issue with a full tank than with an empty one.

Keep the numbers on paper. A quote that doesn't know your narrowest point is a guess about whether the tank will fit.

## What tank sizes actually look like

Published sizes vary a lot between makers and shapes. These are the figures from two manufacturers' own pages, for steel and double-wall polyethylene-in-steel tanks.

| Granby EcoGard steel, 910 L | 23 in | 60 in | 47 in | 290 lb |
| Granby EcoGard steel, 1,135 L | 23 in | 72 in | 47 in | 335 lb |
| Roth DWT 400 L | 28 in | 29 in | 44 in | 106 lb |

| Roth DWT 1000 LH | 30 in | 51 in | 54 in | 208 lb |

Sources: [Granby's EcoGard double-bottom tank page](https://www.granbyindustries.com/products/ecogard-double-bottom-oil-tank/) and [Roth's double-wall tank page](https://www.roth-america.com/product/oil-storage-tanks/double-wall-heating-oil-tank/). Roth also lists a "minimum height needed" above the tank height, for example 49 inches for the 400 L model and 66 inches for the 620 L and 1000 L models, so check headroom with that figure.

Two things stand out. The Granby steel tank is the narrowest at 23 inches, but it is long, and a long tank is the one that struggles at a turn. The Roth tanks are squarer and much lighter, which makes them easier to carry, but their height means ducts and low beams matter. Neither is better in general. The right one is whichever fits your route and your spot. ## Option 1: a different shape of the same capacity

Often the fix is a swap in shape. An upright, squarer tank has a smaller footprint than an oblong one, which can clear a narrow stairwell that a 72 inch tank can't turn in. A lower tank can clear a duct that a tall one can't. Before you settle, check capacity too: most homes want a tank close to what they have now, and our [residential oil tank sizes](/blog/residential-oil-tank-sizes/) guide covers how to match one to your use.

## Option 2: two smaller tanks

Roth's manual says multiple smaller tanks can be used where a single larger tank won't fit or the pathway is too small. It also gives the limits:

- It cites CSA B139 as capping aggregate capacity at 1,100 imperial gallons, and says local codes may restrict that.
- Two Roth 400 L tanks side by side take up about 29 by 60 inches of floor, according to its footprint table.
- Tanks that share a fill or supply line must be the same model, at the same height, level and plumb.
- Roth's common fill and vent kits are for indoor installs only. Outdoors, each tank needs its own fill, vent and cover.

[Granby's installer instructions](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0018_Ga-Granby-2-in-1-Installer-Instructions-1.pdf) for its 2 in 1 fibreglass tanks are stricter. Each tank must be installed and piped independently, with no cascade filling and no combined venting. The two makers don't agree, so the maker of the tank you choose decides how it gets piped.

Two tanks also hold less per tank. Two 400 L tanks give you 800 L, below a 910 L tank, so you may fill more often. They add fittings, vent alarms and floor space, and a lot of weight in one place on an old slab. Our guide to [two connected oil tanks](/blog/two-oil-tanks-connected/) covers how tandem setups behave once they are running.

## Option 3: put the new tank outside

If the stairs are really the problem, an outdoor tank skips the route entirely. It comes with trade-offs, which our [indoor vs outdoor tank comparison](/blog/indoor-vs-outdoor-oil-tank/) lays out. Granby's instructions say the installer should choose an indoor installation whenever possible. For outdoor installs, they require a weather cover, anchoring to the foundation, and a spot that isn't under house eaves where snow and ice can fall on it. Exposed oil lines, salt air near Shediac, drifting snow and the oil truck's access all enter the decision. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a new oil tank be cut apart to get it down narrow stairs?

No. Roth's installation manual says its tank can't be dismantled or altered to fit through smaller spaces, and that trying destroys its structural integrity and voids the warranty. Old tanks can be opened up for removal. A new tank has to arrive in one piece, so the path has to be measured first.

### What is the narrowest oil tank you can buy?

Among the two makers we looked at, Granby's EcoGard steel tanks are listed at 23 inches wide, in 680, 910 and 1,135 litre sizes. Roth's smaller double-wall models are 28 inches wide. Width isn't the only limit, because length and height matter at turns and under ducts.

### Is it safe to use two small tanks instead of one big one?

It can be, if they are installed to the maker's instructions and the code. Roth's manual describes multiple-tank installs and says it is a use for pathways too small for a larger tank. It also says the combined capacity is capped. Granby requires each of its 2 in 1 tanks to be piped separately.

### Will a tight basement make my replacement cost more?

Possibly. A hard route means more handling, and two tanks or an outdoor move adds work. We publish ranges for standard jobs and don't publish a surcharge, so ask for the written estimate to spell out what the access adds.

### Should I move the tank outside to avoid the stairs?

It is an option, not a default. Outside, the tank needs a cover, a proper base and anchoring, and a spot away from falling snow and ice. For many Southeast NB homes an indoor tank still suits better, so compare both before you decide.
