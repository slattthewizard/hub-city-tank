# Switching From Oil to Electric Heat in New Brunswick: What Happens to Your Tank

If you're dropping oil for straight electric heat, meaning baseboards or an electric furnace rather than a heat pump, the furnace and the oil truck are the easy part to picture. The tank is the part people forget. What happens to it depends on one question: does anything else in the house still run on oil once the furnace is gone?

In a lot of homes the answer is hot water. If your domestic hot water comes from an oil-fired water heater or a coil off the old boiler, the tank stays in service even after the space heating is off oil. If hot water is already electric, or switches at the same time, the tank's job ends and the question becomes what to do with it.

Either way, it's worth running the numbers with dated figures from the people who set the prices, not a guess. For the tank side of the decision (keeping it, replacing it or having it removed), call [(506) 806-0900](tel:+15068060900) for a free written estimate.

## Electric heat doesn't mean a heat pump

The two get lumped together, but electric resistance heat, whether baseboards along the wall or an electric furnace pushing warm air through ducts, works nothing like a heat pump.

A heat pump moves heat that already exists in the outdoor air. A baseboard or electric furnace makes heat directly from electricity, the same way a stove element does. Natural Resources Canada's [Heating with Oil guide](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/sites/nrcan/files/energy/pdf/energystar/Heating-with-Oil_EN.pdf) rates electric baseboards and electric furnaces at 100 percent seasonal efficiency: every kilowatt-hour you pay for becomes a kilowatt-hour of heat in the house. There's no compressor and no outdoor unit, so output doesn't fall off on the coldest night. There's also no multiplier. A heat pump delivers more than one unit of heat for each unit of electricity it uses; resistance heat delivers exactly one.

That difference matters for the tank too. A heat pump conversion often keeps the oil furnace as cold-weather backup, so the tank stays in the plan by design. A straight electric switch is usually all or nothing: the furnace comes out, and the only oil equipment left, if any, is whatever makes hot water. If a heat pump is what you're weighing, our [oil heat versus heat pump cost breakdown](/blog/oil-heat-vs-heat-pump-cost/) and our [guide to converting to a heat pump in Moncton](/blog/oil-to-heat-pump-conversion-moncton/) cover that path. This article is about the other electric option.

## How to compare what oil and electric heat cost to run

Both prices move, oil far more than electricity, so the useful thing to learn is the method. The figures below are an example on one date, 29 September 2026. Redo the math with the numbers posted on the day you read this.

### Step 1: turn a litre of oil into heat

NRCan's guide puts the energy in one litre of No. 2 heating oil at about 38.2 megajoules. Your furnace doesn't deliver all of it to the house. The same guide lists seasonal efficiency of about 60 percent for an old furnace with a cast-iron head burner and 78 to 86 percent for a newer standard model.

One kilowatt-hour is 3.6 megajoules, so the heat you actually get from each litre works out to:

- Older furnace at 60 percent: 38.2 × 0.60 ÷ 3.6 = about 6.4 kWh of heat per litre
- Newer furnace at 78 percent: 38.2 × 0.78 ÷ 3.6 = about 8.3 kWh of heat per litre

If you don't know your furnace's rating, a technician can tell you from the model plate or a combustion test at the next service.

### Step 2: price that same heat on baseboards

Because resistance heat runs at 100 percent, replacing one litre of oil means buying the same number of kilowatt-hours from NB Power. NB Power's [residential rates page](https://www.nbpower.com/en/products-services/residential/rates) lists the residential energy charge at 15.39 cents per kWh after the 2026 increase that took effect in mid-April. That figure is before tax and before the monthly account charge. Your bill adds 15 percent HST, but since January 2025 the province's [electricity rebate](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/corporate/promo/electricity-rebate.html) has taken 10 percent off residential electricity use, so the net tax on the energy charge is about 5 percent.

- Newer furnace: 8.3 kWh × 15.39 cents = about 1.27 dollars of electricity for the heat in one litre of oil (about 1.34 dollars with the net tax)
- Older furnace: 6.4 kWh × 15.39 cents = about 0.98 dollars (about 1.03 dollars with the net tax)

### Step 3: compare it to the oil price

The New Brunswick Energy and Utilities Board sets the maximum retail price for furnace oil. It recalculates the maximum every week from a wholesale benchmark, and it can reset it between scheduled dates when the market moves sharply. On 29 September 2026, the EUB's [current petroleum prices page](https://nbeub.ca/current-petroleum-prices-2) showed a furnace oil maximum of 269.2 cents a litre, HST included. It's a ceiling, so check what your own supplier actually charged.

So on that date, the heat in one litre of oil cost up to about 2.69 dollars from the oil truck and roughly 1.03 to 1.34 dollars from the wall, depending on how efficient your furnace is. On those numbers, baseboards were the cheaper way to buy heat by a wide margin. The break-even is simple: oil only wins when your delivered price per litre drops below the electricity cost worked out in Step 2.

### Step 4: scale it to your house

Take the litres you bought last heating season (your supplier can print a year of deliveries) and multiply by 6.4 or 8.3. That's roughly how many kilowatt-hours baseboards would need to replace that oil. Multiply by NB Power's current rate and add the net tax. If the same oil also heated your hot water, take that share out first, or the electric side will look worse than it is.

| | Oil furnace | Electric baseboard or furnace |
|---|---|---|
| Seasonal efficiency (NRCan guide) | About 60% old, 78 to 86% newer | 100% |
| Who sets the price | EUB weekly maximum, tied to the wholesale market | NB Power rate, approved by the EUB |
| Output on the coldest nights | Full | Full |
| Needs a chimney or vent | Yes | No |
| Annual service | Burner tune-up and filter | None for combustion; wiring and thermostats only |
| During a power outage | Needs power for burner and blower | Needs power, and far more of it |

Two things the table leaves out. An electric switch in an older house may need more panel capacity, and that's an electrician's call. And neither system heats in a power outage without backup, but an oil burner and blower draw a small fraction of what a house full of baseboards does, which matters if you plan to run a generator.

## What happens to the tank when the furnace comes out

Once the oil furnace is disconnected, the tank lands in one of two situations. Work out which one applies before you decide anything else.

If nothing else in the house burns oil, the tank's job is finished. An idle tank doesn't get safer by sitting there, and a steel tank can keep corroding from the inside whether or not it's being filled. It's also the kind of thing an insurer asks about at renewal and a buyer's inspector flags when you sell. Our guide on [how old is too old for insurance](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) covers what insurers tend to look for on an ageing tank.

If hot water is still on oil, the tank stays employed, just at a much smaller job.

## If hot water still runs on oil, the tank stays in the picture

Some homes have a separate oil-fired water heater, or an indirect tank heated by a coil off the boiler. Switch the space heating to baseboards and that equipment keeps calling for oil. One catch with a boiler coil: if the boiler itself is the thing coming out, the hot water goes with it, so check which setup you have before you plan around it.

When the tank keeps feeding a water heater, it still matters in all the same ways. It still has to be sound, it still has to satisfy your insurer, and it still needs watching for water and sludge. A tank that only feeds hot water turns over far less oil in a year, so fuel sits longer and any water that gets in has more time to cause trouble before anyone notices. If the tank is getting old and hot water is its only job, that's still a real decision. Our [oil tank replacement service in Moncton](/oil-tank-replacement-moncton/) covers a straight swap either way.

Plenty of people in this spot move hot water off oil too, to an electric tank or a heat pump water heater. When that happens, the tank's last job disappears and you're in the fully electric situation below.

## Going fully electric: what removal involves

Say you're in a Riverview bungalow with the oil furnace coming out and hot water already electric. The tank has no remaining job. Leaving it is one more thing that can leak, one more thing an insurer or buyer asks about, and one more thing taking up space for nothing.

Removal is a modest job for an above-ground tank. Outdoor steel tank removal runs $500 to $900, and an indoor or basement tank, which takes more time to disconnect, pump out and carry up the stairs, runs $700 to $1,100. A buried tank is a different scale of job, $2,000 to $10,000 depending on depth, access and what the soil shows, with straightforward buried jobs usually at $2,000 to $4,000. Our [oil tank replacement cost page](/oil-tank-replacement-cost/) has the full table.

Whatever oil is left has to come out before the tank does, and that isn't a weekend project. An "empty" tank still holds residual oil, sludge and vapour, which is why cutting or dismantling one yourself is a bad idea. Our [oil tank disposal guide](/blog/oil-tank-disposal/) explains what happens to the oil and the tank once they leave the property.

One more thing that gets forgotten: the chimney or flue the furnace vented through. If nothing else uses it, the connection should be sealed and the chimney looked at, so rain, snowmelt and animals don't have an open route in. If an oil water heater stays behind on that same chimney, have a technician check that the flue still suits the smaller appliance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is electric heat cheaper than oil in New Brunswick?

On 29 September 2026 it was. With the EUB furnace oil maximum at 269.2 cents a litre and NB Power's residential rate at 15.39 cents per kWh, the heat in a litre of oil cost roughly 1.03 to 1.34 dollars to buy as electricity, net tax included, depending on furnace efficiency. Both prices change, so rerun the four steps above with the current EUB and NB Power figures.

### Do electric baseboards or an electric furnace need a bigger electrical panel?

Often, especially in older homes. Heating a whole house with resistance heat draws a lot of current, and a home on 100 amp service may need a panel or service upgrade before the switch. That's a question for an electrician to answer after looking at your panel and the heat load.

### Can I keep using oil for hot water after switching my heating to electric?

Yes, if your hot water comes from a separate oil-fired water heater, it keeps running regardless of what heats the house. If it comes from a coil in the boiler you're removing, it goes when the boiler goes. A tank kept for hot water still needs to be in good shape and acceptable to your insurer.

### What does it cost to remove an oil tank after switching to electric heat?

An outdoor above-ground tank runs $500 to $900 to remove, and an indoor or basement tank $700 to $1,100, because it takes longer to pump, disconnect and carry out. A buried tank costs more, $2,000 to $10,000 depending on depth, access and what the soil shows.

### Do I have to remove the oil tank right away once I switch to electric?

It depends on your insurer and your plans for the house, so ask your insurer before deciding. A tank with no remaining job doesn't get safer with time. If you're wondering about leaving it where it is, our article on whether you can [abandon an oil tank in place](/blog/abandon-oil-tank-in-place/) covers that question.

### Will electric heat keep up on the coldest nights of the winter?

Yes, if the system is sized for the house. Unlike a heat pump, resistance heat doesn't lose output as the temperature drops, so a baseboard puts out the same heat at minus 25 as at minus 5. The trade-off is that it draws the same electricity to do it, so the coldest weeks show up clearly on the bill.
