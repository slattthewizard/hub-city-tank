# Oil Tank Leaning or Legs Sinking: Why It Can't Wait

A full 910 litre oil tank weighs close to a tonne. If yours is tilting, all of that weight is hanging off whichever legs haven't sunk yet, and the copper line feeding your furnace is slowly bending along with it.

That's the part most homeowners miss. An oil tank leaning a few degrees doesn't look like an emergency. It looks like a fence post that's gone a bit crooked, the kind of thing you'll get to in the spring. But a leaning tank isn't standing still. Every freeze and thaw cycle here in Greater Moncton shifts it a little more, and the failure point usually isn't the tank shell. It's the oil line, the fittings, or a rusted leg giving out all at once.

We've seen tanks in Moncton, Dieppe, and Riverview go from "a bit off level" to lying on their side in one wet March week. Here's why it happens, how to check your own tank today, and what it actually costs to fix it now versus later.

## Why a Leaning Oil Tank Can't Wait Until Spring

Steel tanks are built to sit level. The moment a tank starts to lean, three things begin working against you.

First, the oil line. Most outdoor tanks feed the furnace through a small copper line that exits near the bottom of the tank. When the tank tilts, that line bends with it. Copper doesn't like being bent slowly under load. It work-hardens, then cracks, and heating oil starts seeping into your yard or along your foundation. If you've noticed a diesel smell outside or a [wet spot under your oil tank](/blog/wet-spot-under-oil-tank/), a shifting tank is one of the first things we check.

Second, the legs. Standard tank legs are narrow steel feet. On a proper pad they spread the load just fine. On soft ground, cracked concrete, or heaved patio stones, each freeze-thaw cycle drives them deeper on one side. The lean gets worse on a curve, not a straight line. Slow for years, then fast in a single season.

Third, the tip-over risk. A tank leaning past a certain point can go over in a windstorm, under a slab of snow sliding off the roof, or simply when the ground lets go in a thaw. A tipped tank almost always tears its fittings out. At that point you're not fixing a lean anymore, you're dealing with a spill, and spills are a different world of cost.

## What Makes Oil Tank Legs Sink Around Greater Moncton

Southeast New Brunswick is honestly a tough place to keep a tank level. A few local realities do most of the damage.

**Freeze-thaw cycles.** We get dozens of them every winter. Water works under the pad or around the legs, freezes, heaves the ground, then melts and lets everything settle back slightly differently. Multiply that by twenty winters and even a good installation drifts out of level.

**Clay and fill soils.** Plenty of neighbourhoods in Moncton, Dieppe, and Riverview sit on clay-heavy soil or on fill placed when the house was built. Both hold water in spring and shrink in dry summers. Tank legs on small patio stones basically float on that movement.

**Improvised bases.** We still find tanks sitting on two patio stones, a couple of bricks, or wood blocks laid straight on grass. That might've passed decades ago, but it's not a stable base. If you want to know what a proper setup looks like, we've written a full guide to [oil tank stands and pads](/blog/oil-tank-stand-and-pad/).

**Roof water and downspouts.** A surprising number of sinking tanks sit directly under a roof valley or beside a downspout that dumps water right at the legs. The ground there never gets a chance to firm up.

**Corroded legs.** Legs rust from the ground up, and rust at the base weakens exactly the spot carrying the load. A badly flaking leg can buckle without the ground moving at all. If you're seeing scale anywhere on the tank, our post on [rust on oil tanks](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) explains which rust is cosmetic and which is a warning.

None of these causes announce themselves. The lean is just the symptom you finally see, often years after the problem started underneath.

## How to Check Your Tank in Five Minutes

You don't need tools for a basic check. Here's what we'd look at if we were standing in your yard.

1. **Sight down the tank from one end.** Look along the top seam against your siding or fence line. It should read level. Any visible tilt deserves a closer look.
2. **Check each leg where it meets the ground.** Are all four feet flat and carrying weight? A leg you can rock, or one hanging in the air, means the load is riding on three legs or fewer.
3. **Look at the pad or stones.** Cracked concrete, tipped patio stones, or feet that have punched divots into the surface all point to movement.
4. **Follow the oil line with your eyes.** From the tank to where it enters the house, the line should run smoothly. Kinks, sharp new bends, or a line pulled tight are red flags.
5. **Smell and look underneath.** Any oil odour, staining on the pad, or dead grass under the tank means you may already have a slow leak. That's a same-day phone call, not a spring project.

A tank that fails one of these checks isn't automatically a crisis. A tank that fails two or three is telling you something. And if there's any sign of oil on the ground, treat it as urgent. Escaped heating oil is a regulated pollutant, and the US EPA's material on [storage tank releases](https://www.epa.gov/ust) shows how seriously regulators everywhere treat even small amounts in soil.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much lean is too much for an oil tank?

There's no official degree threshold, but any lean you can see from across the yard deserves attention. The real questions are whether it's still moving and whether the oil line is under strain. If the lean has visibly changed over a season, or one leg isn't bearing weight, get it assessed before the next freeze-thaw cycle makes it worse.

### Can I just put blocks or shims under a sinking oil tank leg?

Please don't. Jacking a tank that holds hundreds of litres of oil is genuinely dangerous, and a slipped jack or shim can tear the line out and cause the very spill you're trying to prevent. Shimming also treats the symptom while the ground keeps moving underneath. A proper fix means safely lifting the tank, correcting the base, and checking the line and fittings, which is a job for a licensed contractor.

### Will insurance cover a spill from a tank that tipped over?

Sometimes, but don't count on it. Many policies limit or exclude oil spill claims, and insurers push back hard when a tank was visibly leaning or sitting on an improper base and nothing was done. A documented repair or replacement from a licensed contractor puts you in a much stronger position. Read your policy wording before you need it, not after.

### My tank leans but it's also old. Should I re-level it or replace it?

It comes down to age and condition. A tank under 15 years old with a solid shell is usually worth re-levelling on a proper pad. A tank past 20 years, or one with heavy rust, pitting, or a weeping bottom seam, is a replacement candidate, because a new pad won't stop internal corrosion.
