# Should You Replace Your Oil Tank and Furnace at the Same Time?

If both are old, the short answer is usually yes: coordinating the two jobs saves you a second shutdown, a second round of work on the oil line, and a second round of insurance questions. But "at the same time" does not always mean the same afternoon, and it does not mean one company handles both. Your furnace is your HVAC contractor's job. ## Two Old Systems, One Decision

An oil tank and an oil furnace do not wear out the same way. A steel tank fails from the inside, from condensation and slow corrosion, and it is often an insurance renewal or a fuel supplier's concern about the tank that forces the issue rather than a dramatic leak. A furnace fails from years of heat cycling on the burner, heat exchanger and controls, and it usually announces itself with rising repair bills, uneven heat, or a breakdown on a cold night.

Because the two clocks run on different logic, it is common for one to be clearly done while the other still has a few years left, or for both to hit the wall in the same season simply because they went in together decades ago. Our [guide to oil tank lifespan](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) breaks down realistic tank ages by type and location if you are not sure where yours stands.

## Signs Your Tank Is the One Driving the Clock

The tank side of this is usually the easier call, because insurers and fuel suppliers give you outside signals. Watch for:

- A renewal letter naming your tank's age or asking for an inspection
- A single-wall steel tank at or past 15 years old, which is where many insurers start asking (limits vary by company and tank type)
- Rust blisters, pitting, or a damp stain under the tank
- A fuel delivery driver flagging the tank or refusing to fill it

Any one of these puts the tank ahead of the furnace in the queue, regardless of how the furnace is running. Our post on [repairing versus replacing a leaking tank](/blog/oil-tank-repair-or-replace/) covers what can honestly be patched and what cannot, which matters if you are trying to figure out whether the tank can wait a season.

## Signs Your Furnace Is the One Driving the Clock

There is no official lifespan for an oil furnace. Heating contractors commonly quote something like 15 to 25 years as a general industry range, but condition and service history matter more than the date on the unit. Natural Resources Canada's Keeping the Heat In guide says [oil-fired furnaces and boilers require annual cleaning and tuning](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/home-energy-efficiency/keeping-heat-section-9-operating-your-house-thermostats-water-heaters-furnaces-other-heatingcooling-systems), while natural gas and propane furnaces should be serviced at least every two years. A furnace that has missed years of that annual service is not a furnace to judge by its age alone.

Talk to your furnace contractor rather than us about the technical signs, but the ones homeowners usually notice first are a burner that will not stay lit, rooms that heat unevenly, a service tech who keeps quoting parts instead of a tune-up, and an oil bill that climbs even though nothing else changed. A furnace this far gone is usually the more urgent of the two, since it means no heat, not just a paperwork problem.

## What Coordinating the Two Jobs Saves You

If both systems are near the end, doing the tank and furnace work in the same window, even if two different crews handle it, has real advantages over spacing them out by a year or two.

The house goes without heat once instead of twice. The oil line between the tank and the burner, and the filter on it, get worked on once, so a new line is not run to an old furnace only to be disturbed again a year later. You have one conversation with your insurer about both systems instead of two separate renewal scares a year apart. And if the new furnace is going in a different spot, or the line route is changing, the tank crew can plan the line around the final layout instead of the old one.

None of that requires one contractor to do both jobs. It just means picking a target week and having your furnace contractor and your tank crew talk to the same calendar.

## What You Risk by Replacing Only One

Doing just one side of this is not a mistake, but it carries a specific cost depending on which one you pick.

| If you replace | And keep the old | The risk |
|---|---|---|
| Furnace | Tank | Insurer flags the tank anyway, or it fails within a year or two, forcing a second shutdown and a second round of line work on a furnace that just went in |
| Tank | Furnace | The furnace breaks down in the coldest week, forcing an emergency furnace swap while the new tank's lines get disturbed again anyway |
| Neither | Both | An insurance letter can force the tank issue, and a mid-winter furnace failure can force the other one, each on its own schedule, not yours |

The middle row is the easy one to miss. Picture a tank replaced to satisfy an insurance letter, then a 20-year-old furnace giving out the following January. The tank work was not wasted, but the second shutdown and the emergency furnace call might have been planned together if the furnace's age had been on the radar the first time.

## Which Goes First When You Cannot Do Both at Once

If money or scheduling means staggering the two jobs, a few practical rules help:

A tank with a hard insurance deadline goes first. If your insurer's letter sets a date to replace the tank or lose coverage, that date is not yours to move, and a furnace that is merely getting old can usually wait a season.

A furnace with no heat goes first, too. A cold house is an emergency regardless of what the tank looks like. Our [emergency oil tank service](/emergency-oil-tank-service-moncton/) page covers the tank side of an urgent situation, but a dead furnace is a call to your HVAC contractor, not to us.

If neither system is in crisis but both are clearly aging, the order matters less than keeping the two jobs close together, ideally in the same month. Settling the furnace plan first has one practical advantage: if the new unit sits in a different spot or the oil line route changes, the tank crew knows where the line has to end before they start. If your tank already has a firmer deadline than your furnace, the tank goes first and the furnace follows.

## The Third Option: Skipping the New Oil Furnace Entirely

Before you commit to another oil furnace, it is worth asking whether you want to convert instead, especially if both systems are due at once. A heat pump conversion sidesteps the "replace the furnace" question altogether, though the tank question does not disappear if you plan to keep oil as backup heat. If you are going fully electric, the tank comes out rather than getting replaced, which is a different service and a different price than a swap. If you are staying with oil, or going hybrid, the tank-and-furnace timing question above still applies.

## What to Ask For When You Book the Tank Side

Whichever order you land on, get the tank quote in writing before anything is disconnected. Ask what happens to the oil currently in the tank, whether the fill and vent lines are being reused or replaced, and whether the new tank's size still suits how much oil the new furnace is expected to burn, if your furnace contractor can tell you yet. CSA B139, the installation code for oil-burning equipment, covers both the tank and the appliance it feeds. CSA Group's summary of the 10th edition lists a new requirement for an [annual visual inspection for leakage](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/) in fuel oil tanks, tubing, piping and filters. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it cheaper to replace an oil tank and furnace at the same time?

There is no single combined price, since a furnace contractor and a tank company quote separately. What you usually save is less on the invoice total and more on disruption: one shutdown instead of two, and the oil line worked on once instead of being disturbed again a year later. Ask both contractors to work from the same target date rather than expecting a bundled discount.

### How long does an oil furnace last?

There is no official figure. Heating contractors commonly quote 15 to 25 years as a general range, and Natural Resources Canada says oil-fired furnaces need cleaning and tuning every year, so service history matters. A furnace that has missed several years of service can act older than its age suggests. Your furnace contractor can judge the unit's condition, not just its age.

### Should I replace my oil tank first or my furnace first?

If your tank has a hard insurance deadline, it goes first regardless of the furnace's condition. If your furnace has stopped producing heat, that is an emergency and takes priority over the tank. If neither is in crisis, keep the two jobs close together. Settling the furnace plan first helps if the unit or the oil line route is moving, since the tank crew then knows where the line has to end.

### Can I replace just the furnace and leave my old oil tank alone?

You can, but check the tank's age against what your insurer allows before you do. A tank near or past its typical service life can trigger a renewal letter shortly after you have just paid for a new furnace, which means disturbing the freshly connected lines again for a second job. Our post on [oil tank age and insurance](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) covers what insurers typically look at.

### What happens to my oil tank if I switch to a heat pump instead of a new furnace?

If you are going fully electric, the tank gets drained and removed rather than replaced, which is a different job than a swap. If you are keeping oil as backup heat in a hybrid setup, the tank still has to meet your insurer's age and condition standards, so an old tank does not get a pass just because it is doing less work.

### Does replacing the tank and furnace together require any special permit or inspection?

There is no blanket answer, since permits depend on your municipality and the work involved. Both jobs are installed to CSA B139, the national installation code for oil-burning equipment, whether they happen together or months apart. Ask each contractor which permits apply and what paperwork they hand over, since that record is what your insurer and any future buyer will ask to see.
