# Oil Tank Containment Trays: What a Drip Pan Catches, What It Doesn't, and When a Double-Wall Tank Is the Better Answer

A containment tray under an oil tank catches small amounts of oil: a drip from the filter, a weeping fitting, a slow seep at the bottom seam. It does not stop a tank from rusting through, and a shallow drip pan won't hold the contents of a tank that splits. The tray is a safety net for small failures. It isn't a fix for an old tank.

This guide explains the difference between a small drip pan and a full-size containment tub, how to judge the size, what a tray hides as well as catches, and when a double-wall tank does the same job better. ## Two Different Things Called a Tray

The word covers two products that do different jobs.

**A drip pan, or release barrier.** This is a shallow pan under the filter, the valve and the lower fittings, or under the front of the tank. It catches drips and a slow weep. Nova Scotia's page on [domestic oil tanks](https://novascotia.ca/lae/fuelsafety/petroleum-domestic/) describes these as release barriers that catch indoor leaks, and says they "can be installed with an audible alarm or monitored through a home security system." The idea is to hear about a drip before it becomes a stain on a concrete floor.

**A secondary containment tub.** This is a large pan or basin that the whole tank sits in. The same Nova Scotia page says these units, which it calls tank tubs, are designed to hold 110 percent of the tank's capacity in a spill, and they can be used inside or outside a building. The Co-operators' [safety tips on heating your home with oil](https://www.cooperators.ca/en/support/resource-centre/stay-safe/oil-heating) gives the same 110 percent figure for secondary containment units.

The gap between them is large. For a 910 litre tank, 110 percent is about 1,000 litres. A shallow drip pan holds a tiny fraction of that. If you shop for a tray, find the stated capacity on the spec sheet and compare it with your tank before you assume it can handle more than a drip.

## What a Tray Catches

A tray is good at the failures that start small, which is also how many oil tank problems start.

- **Filter and valve drips.** A weeping filter housing or a valve packing that has started to seep drops oil straight down. A pan catches it before it stains the floor.
- **A slow seep at a fitting.** The gauge, the fill and vent bungs, and the outlet are all joints, and joints can weep. A pan shows the weep as a film of oil you can see.
- **A bottom seam weep.** A pinhole that lets out a few drops a day lands in the pan under the tank, if the pan is in the right place. Our guide to [a wet spot under an oil tank](/blog/wet-spot-under-oil-tank/) explains how to tell this from condensation and from a fitting drip.
- **Oil from a delivery.** A small spill at the fill pipe or from a hose coupling often runs down the tank. A tub that is wider than the tank catches some of it.

## What a Tray Doesn't Catch

- **A ruptured or split tank.** If a seam gives way or a leg collapses, a drip pan is overwhelmed in seconds. Only a full-capacity tub, or a double-wall tank, has a chance of holding that much.
- **Oil that misses the pan.** Oil runs along the underside of a tank, down a leg or along a wall. If the pan isn't wider than the tank on every side, a leak finds the floor.
- **The oil line.** The copper line from the tank to the furnace is a leak risk of its own, and a tray under the tank doesn't cover it. It needs its own checks.
- **Corrosion you can't see.** A tray shows you that oil has already come out. It doesn't show you that the tank is thinning from the inside. By the time oil lands in the pan, the wall has already failed.

There is a second problem. A tray can hide a tank problem as well as catch it. A pan full of rust flakes, water and a bit of oil in the corner of a basement is easy to ignore, and an insurer or buyer's inspector looking at a tank wants to see the bottom and the legs clearly. A tray that is never checked or emptied can end up masking the exact thing you wanted to know about. Look in it each time you check the tank.

## Does the Code or an Insurer Require One?

CSA B139, the installation code for oil tanks, is a paid standard, and we couldn't find a public source saying the current edition requires a tray or tub under a typical residential indoor tank. The public material we checked describes containment as an option for homeowners, not a rule. If you are asking because an inspector, a buyer or an installer told you it is required, ask them for the clause and the edition, and ask your municipality or the installer to confirm in writing before you spend money.

Insurers are a separate question. Some ask about tank age, condition and any containment on a renewal form, and what they accept depends on the company. A tray may help your case, but it won't make a tank that is past your insurer's age limit acceptable. Our pages on [oil tank age and insurance limits](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) and [oil tank inspections for insurance](/blog/oil-tank-inspection-for-insurance/) explain what typically gets asked. When in doubt, ask your insurer what they accept and keep the answer.

## Tray vs Double-Wall Tank

A double-wall tank puts the containment inside the tank itself. The inner tank holds the oil and the outer shell holds what the inner tank lets out. Our guide to [double wall oil tanks](/blog/double-wall-oil-tanks/) covers how the designs differ, including double-bottom tanks that protect the floor of the tank only.

| | Drip pan | Full containment tub | Double-wall tank |
|---|---|---|---|
| Catches filter and fitting drips | Yes | Yes | No, those fittings sit outside the tank |
| Catches a slow bottom weep | Yes, if placed right | Yes | Yes, in the gap between the walls |
| Holds a major failure | No | Yes, up to its capacity | Yes, within the outer shell |
| Takes floor space | A little | A lot | Same as the tank |
| Fits an existing tank | Yes | Usually | No, replaces the tank |
| Helps if the tank is already old | Not much | Somewhat | Replaces the old tank |

A double-wall tank doesn't help with the filter drip, because that happens outside the tank. A pan under the filter does. So the realistic setups look like this:

- **A sound steel tank, young, indoors.** A pan under the filter and the front of the tank is cheap and useful. A full tub is optional.
- **An old steel tank you don't trust.** A tray buys you time but not safety. The tank is the problem.
- **A new tank going in.** Choose between a standard tank with a tub, or a double-wall tank. For many basements, a double-wall tank uses less floor space than a tank plus a tub.

## Practical Notes If You Add a Tray

A few details make the difference between a tray that works and one that is just a pan on the floor.

**Size it to the tank, not the room.** The pan should extend past the tank on every side and past the legs. If you can't see the stated capacity, assume it is a drip pan and treat it as one.

**Check it, don't just own it.** Look in it when you check the gauge. A film of oil, rust flakes or water in the pan is information. A tray is no substitute for a level, solid base under the tank.

**Keep it clear.** A pan with stored boxes, a bucket or old paint in it isn't catching anything. Keep the area around the tank clear as well.

**Add an alarm if the tank is out of sight.** A drip pan with a sensor that sounds locally is much more use than one nobody looks at. Our guide to [oil tank level monitors](/blog/oil-tank-level-monitor/) covers the remote-alert side of this.

**Don't use a tray to excuse an old tank.** If the tank is 20 years old or more, rusting at the seams, or sitting on legs that are sinking, a tray might catch some of the oil when it fails. It won't prevent the failure, the cleanup bill, or the call from your insurer.

## Making the Decision

Here is how to think about it in order.

1. **Look at the tank.** Is it dry, free of rust at the seams and legs, and young enough that your insurer has no concerns? If so, a pan under the filter and fittings is a reasonable addition.
2. **Look at the location.** Is the tank near a sump, a floor drain or a well? Then containment matters more, and a double-wall tank or a full tub is worth pricing.
3. **Look at the age.** If the tank is old, or a weep has already shown up, the choice is not tray or no tray. It is which new tank.
4. **Get it in writing.** Ask your insurer what they accept. Ask your installer what fits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What size containment tray do I need for an oil tank?

For full secondary containment, the stated standard in the Nova Scotia and Co-operators material is 110 percent of the tank's capacity, so about 1,000 litres for a 910 litre tank. A small drip pan under the filter is a different product and holds far less. Check the stated capacity on the spec sheet before you buy.

### Is a drip tray required under an indoor oil tank?

We couldn't find a public source saying CSA B139 requires one under a typical residential indoor tank. Requirements can differ by tank type, installer and insurer. Ask whoever told you it is required for the clause and edition, and ask your insurer what they accept.

### Will a tray stop an old oil tank from leaking?

No. A tray catches oil after it leaves the tank. It doesn't stop corrosion, and a shallow pan won't hold a tank that splits. If the tank is old or rusting at the seams, replacing it is the real fix.

### Can I put a tray under an outdoor oil tank?

Nova Scotia's page says containment units can be used inside or outside a building. Outdoors, rain and snowmelt will fill an open tub, so it needs draining and checking. Many outdoor tanks sit on a pad without a tub. Ask your installer what suits your site.

### Is a double-wall tank better than a tray?

For containing a tank failure, usually yes, because the outer shell is part of the tank and can't be skipped or moved. A tray still has a job under the filter and fittings, which a double-wall tank doesn't cover. If you're replacing the tank anyway, the double-wall option is worth pricing.
