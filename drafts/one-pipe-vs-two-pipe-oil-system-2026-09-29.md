# One-Pipe vs Two-Pipe Oil Systems: Where a Tiger Loop Fits In

If your oil tank sits at or above the level of the burner, the oil line is almost certainly a one-pipe system: a single line from the tank to the burner's fuel pump, with gravity helping. If the tank sits below the burner, pump makers call for a two-pipe system, with a second line carrying surplus oil back to the tank. A de-aerator, of which Tigerloop is one brand, is a third option: a small unit near the burner that lets a single line from the tank do a job that would otherwise need two.

Which one your house needs comes down to where the tank sits compared with the burner and how long the line runs. Both can change when a tank is replaced or moved. None of this is homeowner work. Oil lines should only be changed by a qualified oil burner technician, and nobody should rebuild their own supply line. ## How oil gets from the tank to the burner

The part doing the work is the fuel unit, a small gear pump on the burner. Webster Fuel Pumps and Valves explains the two basic setups in its [Service Technician's Handbook](https://www.websterfuelpumps.com/pump_docs/LL4640.pdf):

- **One-pipe system:** one line, the inlet line, from the tank to the fuel unit.
- **Two-pipe system:** two lines between the tank and the fuel unit, an inlet line and a return line.

Inside the pump, the gears move more oil than the nozzle burns. In a one-pipe setup, that extra oil is sent back around inside the pump itself, so the only oil moving through the line from the tank is what the nozzle burns. In a two-pipe setup, a small plug inside the pump (the bypass plug) is put in, and the surplus oil leaves through the return port and goes back to the tank. Switching a pump between the two modes means adding or removing that plug and changing the piping, which is a technician's job.

## One-pipe systems: made for gravity feed

Webster's handbook says a one-pipe system is used where oil can flow to the burner by gravity, meaning the tank sits above the pump. That's the normal setup for a basement tank next to the furnace, or an outdoor tank at ground level feeding a furnace in the basement below.

The handbook's installation page for its M series pumps also allows a one-pipe line to lift oil from below the pump, but only up to 8 feet, and only if the system is completely airtight. It warns that even a slight air leak can make the pump lose its prime on a one-pipe lift. When that happens, the burner fails to start or runs and quits, and someone has to bleed the air out again.

That's the weakness of one-pipe: it has no way to get rid of air on its own. A loose flare fitting, a pinhole in an old copper line or a worn filter gasket can let air in, and on a gravity-fed system you may never notice. On a lift, the same small leak can leave you without heat.

## Two-pipe systems: built for a tank below the burner

Webster's handbook is clear that a two-pipe system should always be used where the oil supply is below the fuel unit. The return line does two things. It carries away the pump's surplus oil, and it makes the system self-venting: air drawn into the inlet line is discharged with the surplus oil and returned to the tank, as long as the leak isn't too large.

The handbook also explains why trapped air matters. Air caught in the nozzle line expands when the burner shuts off and pushes a squirt of oil through the nozzle. The fix is a tight line with all the air purged. Two-pipe systems have that problem less often, though the handbook notes it can still happen.

Two-pipe systems have limits too. The handbook rates a single-stage pump on a two-pipe system for up to 10 inches of mercury (vacuum) at the pump, and a two-stage pump for up to 15 inches. Its conversion table puts 15 inches at a little over 20 feet of straight vertical lift. But vacuum isn't only lift. The line itself adds resistance. The handbook's quick rule of thumb is about 1 inch of vacuum for every foot of vertical lift, plus about 1 inch for every 10 feet of horizontal run, with its detailed tube-size charts for the real numbers. Filters, elbows and valves add more.

Push the vacuum too high and the oil itself starts to break down. The handbook says that on high lifts and long runs with tubing that's too small, the lighter, more volatile part of the oil separates and the oil looks milky. That's not air, and it's likely at 15 inches of vacuum or more. At 20 to 22 inches, the pump's pressure gauge starts to bounce and delivery at the nozzle falls off. The fix is the right tubing size or, in bigger installations, a supply pump.

## Comparing the three setups

| Setup | Where it's used | How it deals with air |
|---|---|---|
| One-pipe | Tank above the burner (gravity feed); Webster allows up to 8 ft of lift only if the line is completely airtight | Doesn't vent itself; air must be bled out |
| Two-pipe | Tank below the burner; Webster rates single-stage pumps to 10 in. of vacuum and two-stage to 15 in. | Self-venting; air returns to the tank with the surplus oil |
| One-pipe with a de-aerator | A single line from the tank, with the de-aerator doing the venting near the burner | Air is separated and vented at the de-aerator |

## What a de-aerator like Tigerloop does

Tigerloop is one brand of oil de-aerator, developed by Tigerholm. Its [product page](https://www.spxflow.com/tigerholm/products/tigerloop-oil-deaerators/), now on the SPX Flow site, describes it as making "a one-pipe system possible" for oil heating installations. Other companies make similar devices, and naming Tigerloop here isn't a recommendation of one brand over another.

The idea is simple. The de-aerator sits near the burner. A single line runs from the tank to the de-aerator. The burner's pump is piped to the de-aerator as if it were a two-pipe system: it draws oil from the de-aerator and sends its surplus back to the de-aerator instead of to the tank. Inside, a float mechanism lets air separated from the oil escape through a vent while keeping the oil in. So the long run back to the tank stays a single line, and the short loop at the burner gets the self-venting benefit of two-pipe.

The maker lists operating limits, including a maximum oil flow, a maximum de-aerating capacity and a maximum suction in the feed line, so a de-aerator isn't a cure-all for a very deep lift or a very long run. It also won't fix a leaking or badly sized line. The line still has to be sound, tight and the right size, and whether a de-aerator suits a given house, and where it's mounted, is the installer's call under the current CSA B139 installation code and the maker's instructions.

## Why this matters when a tank is replaced or moved

The tank end of the line is where most changes happen. When a tank is replaced in the same spot, the new outlet usually sits at about the same height, and the existing setup carries on. When a tank moves, two things can change: its height compared with the burner, and the length of the run.

Some examples:

- **Basement tank moved outdoors.** An outdoor tank at ground level usually still sits above a basement furnace, so gravity feed often still works. But the line now runs through the wall and outside, where it has to be protected, and the run may be longer. Our comparison of [indoor versus outdoor oil tanks](/blog/indoor-vs-outdoor-oil-tank/) covers the wider trade-offs.
- **Tank moved farther from the house.** Moving a tank away from a roofline that dumps ice on it, or out of the way of a new deck, lengthens the line. By Webster's rule of thumb, every extra 10 feet of run adds about an inch of vacuum where there's any lift.
- **Tank below the burner.** A tank on lower ground feeding a furnace on the main floor, or a basement tank feeding an appliance upstairs, is a lift. That's a two-pipe or de-aerator job, not a plain one-pipe line.

## Getting the line right when a tank changes position

If a tank you're replacing or moving will end up level with or below the burner, or much farther away than before, flag it before the work starts. On site, that means looking at the new tank's height compared with the fuel pump, measuring the run, and deciding whether a one-pipe line, a two-pipe line or a de-aerator is right. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a one-pipe oil system?

A single oil line runs from the tank to the burner's fuel pump, with no return line. Surplus oil circulates inside the pump. Webster's technician handbook describes it as the setup for gravity feed, where the tank sits above the pump, and allows a lift of up to 8 feet only if the line is completely airtight.

### What is a two-pipe oil system, and why do some tanks need one?

A two-pipe system adds a return line that carries the pump's surplus oil back to the tank. It's the setup pump makers call for when the tank sits below the burner, because it's self-venting: air drawn into the inlet line goes back to the tank with the surplus oil instead of stopping the burner.

### What does an oil de-aerator like Tigerloop do?

It sits near the burner and separates air from the oil, venting the air through a float-controlled valve. The line from the tank stays a single pipe, while the pump works in a short two-pipe loop with the de-aerator. It helps where a return line to the tank is impractical, but it has flow and suction limits and doesn't fix a leaking line.

### Will replacing or moving my tank change it from one-pipe to two-pipe?

It can. A tank that ends up below the burner, or a much longer run, can push a one-pipe line past what it handles reliably. A like-for-like replacement in the same spot usually doesn't change anything. Ask for the tank's new position and the line to be checked as part of the written estimate.

### Can I convert a one-pipe system to two-pipe myself?

No. Inside the pump it's only a plug, but the job means running a second line to the tank, making tight fittings and bleeding the system, and a mistake means leaks, air problems or no heat. Oil lines should only be installed or changed by a qualified oil burner technician.

### Why does the oil in my line look milky?

Webster's handbook says milky oil on a high lift or long run with undersized tubing isn't air. It's the lighter part of the oil separating under high vacuum, usually at 15 inches or more. It points to a line that's too long, too small or lifting too far, and a technician should check it with a vacuum gauge.
