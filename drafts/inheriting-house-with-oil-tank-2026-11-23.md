# Inherited a House With an Oil Tank? What to Check Before You Keep It or Sell It

If you've inherited a house that heats with oil, the tank is one of the first things to deal with, and it can't wait the way the paperwork sometimes can. An empty house still has a tank full of oil, a furnace that may or may not be running, and an insurance policy that can change once nobody lives there. The order that works is: tell the insurer, find out how old the tank is and how much oil is in it, keep the house from freezing or running dry, and then decide whether the tank stays, gets replaced, or comes out.

## Call the Insurer First, Before Anything Else

The first call is the insurance company or broker, and the reason is simple. Once the person living in a house is gone, the policy may no longer fit the situation.

Intact Insurance, in a [guide to vacant home insurance](https://www.intact.ca/en/personal-insurance/home/house-insurance/vacant-home-insurance), describes a vacant home as one where the prior residents have moved out with no plans to return and no one else has moved in. It says that when a home is vacant for more than 30 days, insurance coverage usually stops, and that you can buy vacant home insurance to keep the property covered beyond that. It also lists owners of estate properties awaiting probate among the people who run into this. The same page says some protections, such as water damage coverage, can end sooner unless you meet conditions like keeping the heat on or doing regular inspections, and that you should always tell your insurer when occupancy changes. Claims can be reduced or denied if you don't.

That's one insurer's wording. Another company may use a different number of days or different conditions, and the policy on your parent's house is the only document that counts. So ask three plain questions:

1. When does the policy treat the house as vacant or unoccupied, and has that already happened?
2. What conditions apply in the meantime (heat kept on, someone checking the house, shut-off water)?
3. What does the insurer want to know about the oil tank, and will it still insure the tank at its current age?

Write down who you spoke to and when. Ask for the answers by email where you can.

## Find Out How Old the Tank Is

An old tank is usually what turns a quiet estate into a rushed one. Many insurers set their own age limits for oil tanks, and a house that sat unchanged for 20 years may have a tank that's now at or past the line. Our article on [oil tank age and insurance limits](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) explains how those limits tend to work, and our guide to [telling the age of your oil tank](/blog/how-old-is-my-oil-tank/) shows where to look for a date stamp or serial number.

Look at the tank itself first: a label or a stamped plate, usually near the top or one end. If you can't find a date, there are other places to check in the parent's papers: an old installation invoice, a delivery record, a home inspection report from when the house was bought. If none of that turns up an answer, plan on the insurer treating the age as unknown, and that often means treating it as old.

Some insurers use a standard form for this. The insurance industry's standards body, CSIO, publishes a national [Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://www.csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire) for personal lines property insurance. If your insurer sends you something like it, the answers (age, location, condition) are exactly what you've just gathered.

Take photos of the tank, the label and the area around it. You'll want them for the insurer, for a buyer's questions later, and for your own records.

## Check the Oil Level and the Heat

A house can sit through a New Brunswick winter without a problem if the heat is set properly and the tank has oil in it. It can also run out of oil in a few weeks of cold and freeze its pipes.

Three things to sort out early:

- **How much oil is in the tank.** Gauges can stick, so a stick test or a delivery check gives a better answer.
- **Whether there's a standing delivery arrangement.** Some households are on automatic delivery, and the account may be in the deceased's name. Find out who is billed and who can change it.
- **What setting the thermostat needs.** If the house stays heated for the insurer's conditions or to protect the pipes, the furnace has to keep running, and that means oil. If you run out, our piece on [what to do when you've run out of heating oil](/blog/ran-out-of-heating-oil/) covers restarting the system.

If the plan is to drain the plumbing and close the house down for the winter, ask the insurer whether that satisfies its conditions, and have a plumber or someone qualified do the draining. We only deal with tanks.

One more practical point. A tank sitting under a house that nobody visits is a tank nobody is watching. If you see a wet spot, a strong oil smell, rust streaks or a bulge, don't wait for the estate to settle. If oil has reached the ground or water, report it: the regional Department of Environment and Local Government office during business hours, or the 24-hour environmental emergency line at 1-800-565-1633 after hours (911 first if there's any fire risk). Then deal with the tank.

## Four Tank Decisions for an Inherited House

Once the insurer is told and the tank's age and oil level are known, you're choosing between four options. Which one fits depends on what's happening to the house.

### Keep the tank and keep heating

This is the right call if the tank is young enough for the insurer, in sound condition, and someone is living in the house, say a family member or a tenant. Have the tank looked over, confirm what the insurer accepts, keep up the oil deliveries and note the installation date for the file. There's nothing wrong with oil heat in a house you're keeping.

### Replace the tank before you list

If the house is going on the market and the tank is old, rusty or of unknown age, replacing it is often the cleaner way to deal with a buyer's insurer. Our article on [selling a house with oil heat](/blog/selling-house-with-oil-heat/) covers how tank age comes up in a sale. A full swap, old tank removed and new tank installed, runs $3,500 to $6,400 on our [oil tank replacement cost](/oil-tank-replacement-cost/) page, so it's worth getting a written estimate early and deciding whether the sale price absorbs it. Our [oil tank replacement in Moncton](/oil-tank-replacement-moncton/) page explains the service.

### Remove the tank and sell without one

If the house is empty, the furnace is old, and the likely buyer is going to convert to electric heat or a heat pump anyway, taking the tank out removes a question and a risk. Removal runs $500 to $900 for an outdoor steel tank and $700 to $1,100 for an indoor or basement tank, and the written estimate will tell you what applies to this one. Our [oil tank removal](/oil-tank-removal-moncton/) page covers what's involved. Whoever decides on removal should make sure the oil left in the tank, the fill and vent pipes and the furnace all get dealt with together, and that the executor keeps the paperwork.

### Leave it and disclose it

Sometimes the right answer is to do nothing to the tank and be upfront about it. If the estate is selling as is and the tank is old, say so in the listing and tell the agent what you know. Hiding a tank's age doesn't work, since the buyer's insurer will ask, and the buyer may price in a replacement. That's a legitimate choice for an estate that doesn't want to spend money first, as long as everyone knows what they're selling.

## What Not to Do While the Estate Settles

A few shortcuts tend to go wrong in an inherited house.

**Don't drain oil yourself.** Pumping heating oil into pails and jugs in a basement is how small spills start. If the tank is coming out, the oil gets handled as part of that job.

**Don't assume the tank is fine because it's never leaked.** Steel tanks usually fail from the inside, where the owner can't see. A tank that has never leaked can still be old enough for the insurer to refuse it.

**Don't leave it to the buyer.** A buyer's insurer will ask about the tank, and an estate with no answer ends up with a lower offer or a delayed closing.

**Don't skip the insurer call because the house will sell soon.** A sale can take months, and the vacancy conditions run from the day the house is empty, not the day it's listed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does home insurance stop when the owner dies and the house sits empty?

Not automatically, but it can change. Intact Insurance says coverage usually stops after more than 30 days of vacancy, and some protections can end sooner without conditions like keeping the heat on. Other insurers word it differently. Tell the insurer or broker right away and read the policy.

### Can I let the oil run out and drain the house instead?

Running out of oil in winter is how pipes freeze, and the insurer may require heat or regular inspections to keep certain coverage. If you want to close the house down, ask the insurer first whether draining the plumbing satisfies its conditions, and have someone qualified do the draining.

### Should I replace the tank before I sell an inherited house?

If the tank is old or its age can't be proven, replacing it often smooths the sale, because buyers' insurers ask about tank age. If the house will be bought by someone converting to electric heat, removing it may be the better choice. A written estimate for each helps you compare.

### Who pays for the tank work, the estate or the buyer?

That's a question for the estate's lawyer and the real estate agent, not for us. The practical point is that the cost of a replacement or removal is known from a written estimate, so it can be weighed against an offer or built into the asking price.

### What if the tank is leaking and the house is empty?

If oil has reached the soil or water, report it to your regional Department of Environment and Local Government office, or to 1-800-565-1633 after hours. Tell your insurer as well.
