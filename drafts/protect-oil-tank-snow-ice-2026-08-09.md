# How to Protect Your Oil Tank From Snow and Ice: What to Clear and Why

One good nor'easter can drop 40 centimetres of snow on Greater Moncton overnight, and the slab that slides off a metal roof can weigh more than a small car. If that slab lands on your oil tank's fill pipe, you're suddenly looking at a repair bill, a missed oil delivery, or in the worst case a spill that costs more than the house's furnace and tank combined.

Here's the good news. Learning to protect your oil tank from snow takes about ten minutes after each storm, a plastic shovel, and a bit of knowledge about which parts of the tank actually get hurt. This guide walks you through exactly what to clear, why it matters, what winter damage costs around Moncton, Dieppe, Riverview and the rest of Southeast New Brunswick, and the moments when you should put the shovel down and call somebody instead.

## Why Snow and Ice Are Harder on Oil Tanks Than People Think

A residential oil tank is a tough piece of steel. Snow doesn't hurt the tank shell much on its own. The damage almost always happens at the skinny, brittle parts bolted to it.

Think about what's attached to a typical outdoor tank. There's a 2-inch fill pipe and a vent pipe sticking up like periscopes, a small copper or coated steel oil line running out the bottom, a gauge on top, and four steel legs standing on a pad. Every one of those is more fragile than the tank itself, and every one of them is a target for ice.

Winter attacks them four ways:

- **Weight from above.** Roof avalanches and heavy wet snow can shear off a fill pipe, crack a vent, or smash a gauge in one hit.
- **Pressure from the side.** Snowplow windrows and snowblower discharge pack hard around the tank and can shove it off level or pinch the oil line.
- **Freeze and thaw underneath.** Water gets under the pad or legs, freezes, heaves, and leaves you with a leaning tank and a stressed oil line.
- **Blockage.** Ice caps over the vent so the whistle can't sound during a delivery, and buried fill pipes mean the driver either skips your house or has to guess.

None of this is rare. Environment and Climate Change Canada's forecasts at [weather.gc.ca](https://weather.gc.ca/) will tell you when the next dump is coming, but they can't shovel your fill pipe out for you.

## The Five Spots to Clear After Every Storm

You don't need to excavate the whole tank down to bare steel after every snowfall. You need these five spots clear, in this order.

### 1. The vent pipe and whistle

The vent is the small pipe with a mushroom cap. When the driver fills your tank, air escapes through it and makes a whistling sound, and the driver keeps pumping as long as they hear it. If ice caps the vent, the whistle goes silent, and a careful driver will stop the fill while a rushed one might not. A blocked vent during a delivery is one of the most common causes of residential overfill spills, and we wrote a whole piece on how that little part works in our guide to the [oil tank vent whistle](/blog/oil-tank-vent-whistle/).

Brush the cap clear by hand or with a soft broom. Never chip at it with a metal tool.

### 2. The fill pipe

This is the capped 2-inch pipe the driver connects to. It needs to be visible from the driveway and reachable without the driver wading through thigh-deep snow. Shovel a path to it and clear a working area about a metre around it. Buried fill pipes cause skipped deliveries, and skipped deliveries in a Moncton February are how people end up with no heat and frozen pipes.

### 3. The oil line at the base

The line running from the bottom of the tank into your house is usually 3/8-inch copper. It's the most delicate part of the whole system. A snowblower, a plow blade, or even a hard push with a steel shovel can kink or crack it. Clear around it gently, by hand, with a plastic shovel, and never let anyone blow or push snow toward that side of the tank.

### 4. The legs and the pad

Check that all four legs are still sitting flat and the tank hasn't started to tilt. Ice heaving under one corner is gradual, so compare against what you remember from fall. A tank that's visibly leaning is stressing every connection on it.

### 5. The path the delivery driver walks

Drivers drag a heavy hose from the truck to your fill pipe, often in the dark. An icy, unshovelled path is a good way to get a "could not deliver" card in your mailbox. Sand or salt the walkway the same as you would for the mail carrier.

## Roof Ice Is the Number One Tank Killer in Our Area

Of everything winter throws at oil tanks, sliding roof snow and falling ice cause the most expensive calls we get. A sheet of ice coming off a steel roof two storeys up hits with enormous force, and tanks tucked under the eaves take the hit.

Denise in Riverview found that out two winters ago. Her tank sat under the back eave of a steel-roofed bungalow, and a February thaw sent the whole roof load down at once. The scary part is what didn't happen: if the crack had been below the snow line and her next delivery had gone into a broken pipe, she'd have been looking at a cleanup starting north of $10,000, and that's before anyone talks about her insurance deductible.

## How to Protect Your Oil Tank From Snow: A 10-Minute Routine

Here's the routine we give our own customers. After any storm that drops more than 10 centimetres, or any freezing rain event, walk out with a plastic shovel and a broom and run through this list.

1. **Brush the tank top, gauge and vent cap clear.** Hands and broom only. No metal tools, no hammering, no chipping ice off pipes.
2. **Shovel a path and a clearing around the fill pipe.** A metre of working room is plenty.
3. **Hand-clear around the oil line side of the tank.** Slow and gentle near the copper.
4. **Eyeball the legs.** Still level? Still flat on the pad? Note anything that's changed.
5. **Look up.** Is snow loading on the roof edge above the tank? Deal with it before the next thaw.
6. **Check for stains.** Any pink, red or brown tint in the snow near the tank, or an oily smell, means stop and call right away.

A few things you should never do. Don't pour boiling water on frozen pipes, because the shock can crack fittings and the water refreezes into a worse ice jacket. Don't use a torch or heat gun anywhere near an oil tank. Don't let the snowblower throw its discharge across the tank, and ask your plow contractor to push the pile somewhere else. Marking the tank and fill pipe with tall driveway stakes in November solves most plow problems before they start, and it's on our checklist in the [oil tank winter preparation guide](/blog/oil-tank-winter-preparation/).

Gary in Salisbury learned the snowblower lesson the hard way. All winter his blower buried the tank in a hard-packed drift, and by late January the vent cap was iced solid. During his February fill the whistle never sounded, the driver caught it late, and a few litres of oil came back up the fill pipe onto the snow. Contaminated snow and soil had to be dug out and hauled away, and the bill came to $1,900. A thirty-second brush of the vent cap after each storm would have prevented all of it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I shovel the snow off the top of my oil tank?

Yes, but gently. A metre of wet snow adds real weight to the gauge, fittings and vent, and it hides damage you'd otherwise spot. Use your hands, a broom or a plastic shovel, and never chip at ice that's frozen onto pipes or fittings. If ice won't brush off, leave it and let a mild day do the work.

### Can I pour hot water on a frozen fill pipe or vent?

No. Boiling water can shock and crack cold metal fittings, and everything it touches refreezes into a thicker ice jacket within the hour. Never use a torch or open flame near an oil tank either. If a vent or fill pipe is iced solid before a delivery, call your oil company or call us and we'll free it safely.

### Will snow piled against my tank make it rust faster?

Over time, yes. Snow packed against steel holds moisture on the shell for months, and the repeated wet-dry cycles feed corrosion, especially along the bottom seam and around the legs. Keeping a gap of bare air around the tank all winter is one of the simplest ways to stretch its life. If you're already seeing scale or flaking, have it looked at before next heating season.

### Who's responsible if the snowplow buries or hits my oil tank?

On your own property, your plow contractor is generally responsible for damage they cause, but proving it after the fact is messy and slow. It's far easier to prevent: mark the tank and fill pipe with tall reflective stakes in the fall and tell your contractor exactly where the pile can't go. If a plow or blower does make contact with the tank or line, treat it as damage until proven otherwise and get it inspected.
