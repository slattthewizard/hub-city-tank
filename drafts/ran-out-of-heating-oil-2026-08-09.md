## First, Make Sure You Actually Ran Out of Heating Oil

Oil tank gauges lie. A lot. The float mechanism inside a standard gauge can stick, the disc can hang up on the guide rods, and a gauge that's been reading a quarter tank for three weeks might have been stuck the whole time. So before you order an emergency delivery, confirm the tank is really dry.

Give the gauge a gentle tap. If the red indicator drops, you've got oil and your problem is somewhere else in the system. If you want certainty on an outdoor tank, remove the gauge cap and check the level with a clean wooden dowel, the same way delivery drivers do. If your gauge has a habit of sticking, we've covered the fix in our post on [what to do when your oil tank gauge isn't working](/blog/oil-tank-gauge-not-working/).

While you're at it, rule out the simple stuff. Check that the furnace emergency switch (the red wall switch that looks like a light switch) didn't get bumped off. Check the breaker. Check the thermostat batteries. One more check before anything else: look under and around the tank. If you see a wet spot, smell a strong oil odour, or the tank looks like it's weeping along a seam, stop right there. ## What Happens Inside the Tank When You Run Dry

Running out of oil isn't just an inconvenience. It's hard on the whole fuel system, and knowing why helps you avoid the follow-up problems.

Every oil tank builds up sludge over the years. It's a mix of rust particles, condensation water, and degraded fuel that settles on the tank bottom, below the fuel pickup. When the oil level drops to the last few centimetres, the pickup starts pulling from that layer. Sludge gets drawn into the fuel line, the filter, and sometimes the burner nozzle itself. That's why a furnace that ran dry often runs rough afterward, even with a full tank. We explain the whole mess in our post on [oil tank sludge](/blog/oil-tank-sludge/).

The second problem is air. Once the line runs dry, air fills the fuel line and the pump loses its prime. Most furnaces can't clear that air on their own, which is why the burner may need to be bled before it'll fire again. More on that below.

Neither problem means disaster. It just means a run-out usually costs you a bit more than the oil itself, so it's worth preventing the next one.

## The Diesel Stopgap That Gets You Through the Night

Here's the trick every long-time oil-heat household in Southeast New Brunswick knows: your furnace will run on road diesel. Furnace oil and diesel are close cousins, and 20 or 40 litres of diesel from the nearest gas station will keep the heat on until your delivery arrives.

Heather in Salisbury used this exact move last February. Her tank ran dry on a Saturday morning and the weekend emergency fee quoted to her was $250 on top of the oil. Instead, she bought two 20-litre jerry cans of diesel for $54, poured them into the fill pipe, and booked a regular Monday delivery with no fee. The diesel bridged the weekend for about a fifth of the cost.

A few rules to do it safely:

- Use a proper yellow diesel container, not a red gasoline can that's held gas. Even a small amount of gasoline in an oil tank is dangerous.
- Pour it into the outdoor fill pipe, the same one the delivery driver uses. Don't open unfamiliar plugs or fittings on the tank.
- After pouring, wait 20 to 30 minutes before restarting so any stirred-up sediment can settle below the pickup.
- Never use gasoline, and don't bother with additives for a one-night bridge.

One caution: if your tank is very old, visibly rusty, or you've had moisture problems, adding fuel and firing everything back up isn't automatically the right move. A tank that ran dry because it's been quietly weeping fuel is a different emergency altogether.

## How to Restart Your Furnace After Running Out of Oil

Oil's in the tank. Now the furnace has to actually burn it, and this is where people get into trouble.

Start simple. Wait until at least 20 to 30 minutes after the fill so sediment settles. Make sure the emergency switch and thermostat are on, then press the red reset button on the burner. Once. If the furnace fires and keeps running, you're done.

If it doesn't fire, do not keep pressing reset. This is the single most important rule of the whole process. Every press sprays unburned oil into the combustion chamber, and when the burner finally does light, all that pooled oil can ignite at once. Oil techs call it a puffback, and it can blast soot through the whole house or worse. One press, maybe two a few minutes apart, then stop.

The usual reason a furnace won't restart after running dry is air in the fuel line. Clearing it means opening the bleeder valve on the fuel pump while the burner runs, letting the air and froth escape until clean oil flows. If you're handy, know exactly where your bleeder valve is, and have a container and wrench ready, it's a five-minute job. If any part of that sentence gave you pause, this is where DIY should end. A tech will bleed the line, swap the fuel filter that the run-out probably fouled, and check the nozzle, typically for $150 to $350 all in.

That's money well spent, because a proper restart after a run-out includes a filter change. Skipping it means the sludge your tank just fed into the system stays there, and you'll be chasing rough starts and lockouts all winter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take to get heat back after running out of heating oil?

If you can get fuel the same day, you can usually have heat back within a few hours. The furnace itself only needs a working fuel supply, a primed line, and one press of the reset button. The wait for the delivery truck is almost always the longest part, which is why the diesel stopgap is so popular on weekends.

### Can I put diesel in my heating oil tank?

Yes, ordinary road diesel from any gas station is a safe short-term substitute for furnace oil, and 20 to 40 litres will typically run a furnace for a day or two. Use a proper diesel container, pour it into the outdoor fill pipe, and wait 20 to 30 minutes before restarting. It costs more per litre than furnace oil, so it's a bridge, not a long-term plan.

### Do I need to bleed the fuel line myself?

Only if you're comfortable doing it. Bleeding means opening the pump's bleeder valve while the burner runs until air-free oil flows, and it's straightforward for someone handy who knows their furnace. If you're not sure where the valve is or the burner still won't fire after one or two reset attempts, stop and call a technician, because repeatedly hitting reset can cause a dangerous puffback.

### Can running out of oil damage my furnace or tank?

A single run-out rarely causes lasting damage, but it stirs sludge off the tank bottom into your filter, fuel line, and nozzle, which is why a filter change afterward is smart. Repeated run-outs are a red flag that your gauge is failing or your tank has enough sludge or corrosion inside to block the pickup. If it keeps happening, have the tank inspected before winter does the diagnosing for you.
