# Finishing a Basement With an Oil Tank in It: What to Decide Before the Drywall Goes Up

You can finish a basement with an oil tank in it, but the tank has to stay reachable, visible and removable. Tank makers write that into their installation instructions: clear space around the tank, a label you can read, and room for a service person to get at it. A wall built tight around the tank breaks all three. The cleanest plan is to settle the tank first. If it is old, replace it before you frame. If you are heading to a heat pump or another fuel, take it out before you frame.

This guide covers what to keep open if the tank stays, what to check on the oil line and the fill and vent pipes, and how to decide between keeping, replacing and removing. ## Why the tank should be decided first

A finished basement hides things. Drywall, flooring and a new ceiling cover the tank's legs, the oil line, the filter and any weeping. They also add a problem for later: whatever you build around the tank has to come back down when the tank is replaced, and a new tank has to get in. Roth's installation manual for its double-wall tanks says the tank must fit through all doors, stairs, halls and turns on the way to its spot, and can't be cut down to fit. A closet with a narrow door that was built around the old tank can leave no way to bring a new one in.

So the question isn't "how do we hide the tank." It is "how old is it, and do we want it here in ten years." Our guide to [indoor vs outdoor oil tanks](/blog/indoor-vs-outdoor-oil-tank/) helps with that last part if you are weighing a move outside.

## What manufacturers say about space and access

CSA B139, the installation code, is a paid standard. Two manufacturers' manuals that anyone can read cover the same ground.

Roth's installation manual for its double-wall tanks says:

- Leave at least 2 inches between the tank and any wall, post or structural member. A full tank's sides can expand horizontally as much as 2 inches, and the gap also lets you inspect the sides.
- It cites CSA B139 clause 2.6.3.7 as requiring a minimum clearance of 460 mm (18 inches) along one side and one end for service access.
- It cites CSA B139 as requiring at least 2 feet between the tank and the heating appliance for installations in Canada.
- It recommends 2 feet of clearance above the tank to remove the leak detector.
- The certification label must be visible after installation.
- Water from wash areas or water softeners must be directed away from the tank, since the outer tank can corrode from prolonged wet or salty contact.

[Granby's installer instructions](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0018_Ga-Granby-2-in-1-Installer-Instructions-1.pdf) for its 2 in 1 tanks match the spirit of it: the tank goes on a flat, stable surface away from heat sources, corrosive atmosphere and potential mechanical damage, with the label visible and about 2 inches from walls. You can read the full [Roth installation manual (PDF)](https://www.roth-america.com/app/uploads/2021/02/DWT_Installation_Manual_2023.pdf) for the context around each line. These are two makers' rules for their own tanks. Your tank may be a different make, and the code edition and your insurer can add to them.

Together they point to one design rule: the tank gets its own open space, not a nook. That means a framed alcove with room to walk around it and a full-size door, or just an open corner of the utility room.

## Building around a tank without walling it in

If the tank stays, a few habits keep a finished basement from causing trouble later:

- **Frame a separate mechanical room, not a tight closet.** Keep the furnace, the tank and their lines together, with a door wide enough to carry a tank through. Check with your installer before you commit to a size, and see [oil tank dimensions](/blog/oil-tank-dimensions/) for what a typical tank needs.
- **Don't box the tank in with drywall.** The tank needs to be seen, not only for the label. Rust, weeping, a wet spot at a seam or a leaning leg are what an inspector and an insurer look for, and each is invisible behind a panel.
- **Keep storage away from it.** Boxes, paint and Christmas decorations stacked against the tank block the clearances above and make a leak harder to spot. Don't store anything flammable beside the furnace and tank.
- **Mind water.** A laundry tub, a water softener or a basement that gets damp after the spring thaw all sit badly beside a steel tank. Roth's manual says to keep wash-area and softener water away from the tank.
- **Use a hard, level floor under the tank.** A full 1000 L Roth tank weighs about 1,900 lb with oil, according to its manual. Don't put the tank on a floating subfloor, carpet or a new platform built from lumber unless the maker's instructions allow it.

## The oil line, the fill pipe and the vent

These three are easy for a renovation to damage.

**The oil line.** The copper line from the tank to the furnace is the weak link in many older basements. The Government of the Northwest Territories' [Homeowner's Guide to Oil Tanks](https://www.gov.nt.ca/sites/ecc/files/brochures/homeowners_guide_oil_tanks.pdf) says buried fuel lines should be avoided because they are more susceptible to rupture and difficult to inspect, and that a line leak can go undetected for months. That is a northern territory's guide, but the point holds on a Moncton slab. If your line runs under the floor, a reno is the time to reroute it overhead where it stays visible, rather than burying it under new flooring. Our guide to [oil line replacement](/blog/oil-line-replacement/) covers when to sleeve and when to replace.

**The fill pipe and the vent.** These run from the tank through the outside wall. A new wall, a new ceiling or insulation upgrade can pinch, bend or cover them. Roth's manual also says a tank should have no more than 25 feet of fill piping, because more oil in the pipe can overfill the tank after the vent whistle stops. Our guide to [fill and vent pipes](/blog/oil-tank-fill-and-vent-pipes/) explains sizes and rules. Get your installer to check the pipes before the contractor covers the wall.

**The smell.** Oil vapours soak into drywall, carpet and wood. A tank that weeps or an overfill that dripped onto a slab leaves a smell that is much harder to deal with once the floor is covered. If you can smell oil now, sort it out before the finishing starts. See [oil smell in the basement](/blog/oil-smell-in-basement/).

## Keep, replace or remove: the decision

Here is a way to sort it before the contractor starts.

| Your tank | What makes sense |
|---|---|
| Recent, sound, in a spot that suits the new layout | Keep it. Frame a service space around it, with the label, lines and legs visible. |
| Old or rusty, or in the middle of the planned room | Replace it before framing, and place the new one where the new layout wants it. |
| Heat pump going in, or the house is leaving oil | Remove it before finishing. See our [basement oil tank removal](/blog/basement-oil-tank-removal/) guide. |
| Tank must stay but the layout buries it | Change the layout, or move the tank. A hidden tank is the one that goes unchecked. |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I build a wall around my oil tank?

Not a tight one. Manufacturers call for clearance around the tank, a visible label and service access. Roth's manual cites a code minimum of 18 inches on one side and one end for servicing, plus 2 inches of expansion clearance. A mechanical room with a full-size door works. A closet that seals the tank in doesn't.

### Can an oil tank go inside a finished room?

It depends on the layout and the code, and we know of no single New Brunswick rule that settles it. Check with your installer and your insurer before you build. Whatever the layout, the tank needs space to be inspected and a way out for replacement.

### Should I replace my oil tank before finishing the basement?

If it is old, rusty or in the way, yes. Doing it first means nothing has to be torn out, and the new tank can come in through an open route. A sound, recent tank can stay with a proper service space.

### What happens to the oil line under a new basement floor?

Avoid burying it. The NWT guide says buried fuel lines are more likely to rupture and are hard to inspect. If your line already runs under the slab, a renovation is a good time to reroute it overhead.

### Will an oil tank hurt resale value of a finished basement?

It can matter to some buyers and insurers, mostly when the tank is old or hidden. A newer tank with clear access and tidy lines is easier to explain than a tank boxed in behind drywall.
