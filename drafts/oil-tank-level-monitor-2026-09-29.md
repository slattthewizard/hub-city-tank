# Oil Tank Monitors: What a Level Sensor or Leak Alarm Can (and Can't) Tell You

An oil tank monitor is a sensor that reports your oil level, and sometimes a spill, straight to your phone instead of making you walk out to the tank and read a gauge. For a Southeast New Brunswick homeowner juggling an insurance letter, a rust streak, or just a long driveway in February, that convenience is real.

So is the limit. A monitor watches one thing at a time, either how many litres are left or whether oil has shown up somewhere it shouldn't, and it says nothing about the steel or fibreglass sitting under all of that. This guide walks through the two kinds of level monitor sold for home tanks, the leak alarms that do a different job entirely, and the tank problems a level or leak sensor won't see.

## Level Monitors: The Two Kinds Sold for Home Tanks

Residential oil tank monitors on the market fall into two families, and both exist to answer the same question: how many litres are left right now.

**Wireless float gauges.** These work with the same float and arm already sitting inside your tank, driving the mechanical dial on top (our guide to [a stuck oil tank gauge](/blog/oil-tank-gauge-not-working/) covers how that float mechanism works and how it fails). A wireless version either replaces the gauge head with one that has a sensor built in or fits a reader over the existing gauge, so the same rise and fall that used to move only a dial now also sends a signal to an app over wifi or a cellular network. Because it depends on the same float, it inherits the same weaknesses. A stuck or sunken float reports a wrong number just as confidently as it always did. The wrong number just shows up on your phone now instead of under the dome.

**Ultrasonic senders.** These mount through an opening on top of the tank and never touch the oil. A small transducer sends a short pulse of sound straight down and times how long the echo off the oil's surface takes to bounce back, then converts that into a level. No float, no arm, nothing inside the tank to stick, sink, or jam. The trade-off is that the reading depends on a clean, reasonably flat surface to bounce off. Foam and ripples right after a delivery can throw a reading off until the oil settles, and a tank that sits at an angle reads the level at the sensor's spot, not the true average. Many ultrasonic units also have a short blind zone just under the sensor, so readings on a brim-full tank can be rough.

Both kinds report roughly the same information: current litres or a percentage, your recent burn rate, and a low-level alert before the tank runs dry. Neither one looks at the tank itself.

### Fitting one is tank work, not gadget work

Either type means opening a fitting on top of a tank that holds oil: swapping the gauge head, or using a spare opening for an ultrasonic sender. The gauge guide linked above warns against unscrewing fittings on a tank with oil in it, and that applies here too. A badly sealed port lets vapour out and, on an outdoor tank, lets rain and snowmelt in, and water inside a tank is what drives rust from the inside out. Have whoever services your furnace fit it, and ask them to check the threads and seal while they're up there.

## What a Level Monitor Can Catch

- **A run-out before it happens.** That's the main selling point, and it works. An alert at 25 or 20 percent gives you time to order before the furnace does.
- **A sudden drop that doesn't fit the pattern.** If the app has learned your normal burn rate and the level falls much faster than that over a day or two, with no delivery to explain it, that's worth checking that day. It could be a fast leak, a valve left open, or oil being siphoned.
- **A slow drift in daily use.** A rising burn rate over a few weeks, with no cold snap to explain it, can be a burner that needs a tune-up, but it can also be oil leaving through a line or fitting. Rule out a leak with a look around the tank and along the oil line before blaming the furnace.
- **A drop when nothing should be burning.** If the furnace is shut off for summer and oil doesn't heat your water, the level should sit still. Any steady fall over a few weeks then deserves a look, and this is one of the few times a monitor can hint at a slow leak.

## What a Level Monitor Cannot Catch

- **A slow weep.** A pinhole at a seam or a weeping fitting might lose a litre or two a day, or much less. In heating season that's lost in the day-to-day swing of normal burn, and it may never trip a "sudden drop" alert at all. A weep that slow can soak into a basement floor or the ground outside for months before a number looks wrong on a screen, if it ever does. If you've noticed dampness near the tank, [a wet spot under your oil tank](/blog/wet-spot-under-oil-tank/) walks through what to check.
- **Internal corrosion and pitting.** Steel tanks usually rot from the inside, where water settles on the bottom under the oil, and no level reading shows it. A service tech can check for that water with water-finding paste on a gauge stick, which tells you far more about the tank's future than a percentage on an app.
- **External rust, weeping seams, or a leaning stand.** None of that changes how much oil is in the tank until the day it fails outright, so none of it shows up as a number.
- **The annual inspection.** The 10th edition of CSA B139, the installation code for oil-burning equipment and tanks, made an annual visual inspection of fuel oil tanks, tubing, piping and filters for leakage a requirement, according to [CSA Group's summary of that edition's changes](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/). New Brunswick's guidance also tells homeowners to have the tank inspected once a year by a licensed installer. A reading on your phone is not that inspection. It's a litre count, not a set of eyes on the tank.

## Leak Alarms Are a Different Device With a Different Job

A level monitor answers "how much oil is left." A leak alarm answers "is oil somewhere it shouldn't be," and the two are built differently.

**Containment tray with a float switch.** New Brunswick's own guidance on residential oil tanks recommends setting the tank in a containment tray or dyke and fitting a Canadian Standards Association-approved leak detection alarm, according to [gnb.ca's guidelines on oil tank maintenance and spill prevention](https://www.gnb.ca/en/topic/environment-resources/waste-management/oil-tank-maintenance.html). The tray catches drips and slow weeps that would otherwise run straight into the ground or a basement floor, and a small float switch inside it trips the moment enough oil pools to lift it. It's one of the few devices on this list built to catch a slow leak rather than a level swing.

**Interstitial alarms on double-wall tanks.** If your tank has an inner tank and an outer shell with a gap between them, a sensor can sit in that gap and sound the moment liquid shows up there, meaning the inner wall has already failed while the outer wall is still holding. This only applies if you have a genuine double-wall tank; our guide to [double wall oil tanks](/blog/double-wall-oil-tanks/) covers who tends to want one.

**Stand-alone puck sensors.** Some homeowners add a simple sensor on the floor under a tank's legs or stand, wired or wireless, that sounds an alarm the moment it gets wet. Be careful here. Most household water-leak sensors work by conductivity, and heating oil doesn't conduct electricity, so a sensor sold for a leaky water heater may never trip on a puddle of furnace oil. Sensors built for fuel oil use a float or an optical element instead, so check the packaging says it detects oil or non-conductive liquids. Even the right one only catches a leak that has already reached the floor.

## If an Alarm Trips, or a Reading Looks Wrong

Stop and look before assuming the sensor is wrong. Check for a smell, a sheen, or a wet patch around the tank and its fittings first. If oil has left the tank and reached the floor, the ground, or a drain, treat it as a possible reportable release rather than a maintenance item. The province asks you to report when you know or have reason to believe oil has escaped: call 911 first if there's any fire risk, then the regional Department of Environment and Local Government office during business hours, or the 24-hour environmental emergency line at 1-800-565-1633 after hours. Our guide on [reporting an oil spill in New Brunswick](/blog/report-oil-spill-nb/) covers the details. If you can't find anything and the alarm keeps tripping, or a level reading has stopped making sense, that's a job for someone who can look at the whole tank, not just the sensor.

## A Monitor Helps. It Doesn't Replace a Look at the Tank

A level monitor saves you trips to the tank and can stop a run-out, and a leak alarm in a containment tray is built to catch the slow drip a level reading misses. What neither can do is tell you the tank is fine. They don't replace the annual inspection CSA B139 calls for, or a free written estimate from someone standing in front of the tank. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does an oil tank monitor replace the vent alarm on my tank?

No. The vent alarm, often called the vent whistle, is a fitting in the vent line that whistles while a fill pushes air out and goes quiet as the tank nears full, which tells the driver when to stop. A level monitor tracks how much oil is left day to day and does nothing during a fill. See our guide to the [oil tank vent whistle](/blog/oil-tank-vent-whistle/) for how that device works and why silence during a fill matters.

### Can an oil tank monitor detect a leak?

A level monitor can only infer a leak from an unexplained drop in oil level. It catches a fast leak, but a slow weep can go unnoticed for weeks or months, especially in heating season. A dedicated leak alarm, such as a float switch in a containment tray or an interstitial sensor on a double-wall tank, detects oil directly where it collects. Neither one tells you why the oil is there or how bad the underlying problem is.

### Do I still need to check my tank if I have a monitor?

Yes. A monitor reads a level or a sensor, not the condition of the tank, the stand, the fittings, or the fill and vent pipes. A short walk-around after deliveries and in bad weather still catches things a sensor can't, and CSA B139 calls for an annual visual inspection for leaks regardless of what any monitor shows.

### Are ultrasonic oil tank senders accurate?

Generally yes, since they have no float to stick or sink, but the reading depends on a clean surface to bounce a sound pulse off. Foam and ripples right after a delivery can throw a reading off until the oil settles, and a tank sitting at an angle reads the level at the sensor's spot rather than the true average. Treat any single unusual reading with a bit of patience before assuming the worst.

### Will a monitor tell me if my oil tank is rusting or near the end of its life?

No. A monitor measures oil level or detects liquid in one specific spot. It has no way to see internal pitting, seam corrosion, or a stand that's starting to lean. Those are physical checks a person has to make, not something a sensor reports.

### What should I do if my leak alarm goes off?

Check the tank and the ground around it for a smell, sheen, or wet patch before doing anything else. If you find oil that has reached the floor, soil, or a drain, treat it as a reportable spill rather than something to reset and forget. If you find nothing but the alarm keeps tripping, have the tank and the sensor both looked at.
