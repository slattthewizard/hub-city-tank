# Outdoor Oil Tank Clearances: How Far From the House, the Windows, and the Property Line

Homeowners planning an outdoor tank replacement usually ask the same question once they walk the yard: can the new tank go back in the exact spot the old one sat, or does it have to move? The honest answer is that placement is set by a mix of the national installation code, the tank manufacturer's own instructions, and plain physics like snow load and drainage, not one simple chart. This guide pulls the specific numbers from documents that publish them: a Nova Scotia fuel safety bulletin on the 2009 edition of CSA B139, Prince Edward Island's FAQ on its home heat tank rules, and the installation instructions for Granby and Roth tanks. CSA B139 is the national installation code these documents explain, but we couldn't find a New Brunswick plain-language clearance chart for home tanks like the ones Nova Scotia and PEI publish. So every number below is labelled with where it comes from. None of them is a New Brunswick rule on its own, and your installer should confirm what applies on site.

## Why placement isn't just "wherever it fits"

A tank sitting too close to a wall, a window, or a vent isn't only a tidiness problem. Four things go wrong when clearances get squeezed:

- **The tank has nowhere to move.** Some tanks bulge slightly when full. Roth says the sides of its double wall tank can push out as much as 2 inches, and a tank jammed against a wall or post puts pressure on both.
- **Nobody can service it.** A technician needs to walk around the tank to check the legs, the outlet, and the fittings. A tank wedged into a corner tends to get skipped at the next inspection.
- **Vent and fill fumes can drift toward the house.** That's the reason for the door and window setbacks covered below.
- **Snow, ice, and runoff do more damage.** A tank tucked under a roof edge or beside a downspout takes a beating every winter. Our guide on [protecting an oil tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/) covers what that looks like in practice.

## Distance from the house wall and from the appliance itself

There are really two different "distance from the house" numbers, and it's easy to mix them up.

The first is simple clearance from a wall or post so the tank can expand and be inspected. [Roth's installation manual](https://www.roth-america.com/app/uploads/2021/02/DWT_Installation_Manual_2023.pdf) for its double wall tanks calls for a minimum of 2 inches between the tank and any wall, post, or fixed structure, because the tank's sides can move outward by as much as 2 inches once it's full. The same manual cites CSA B139 for a minimum of 460 mm, about 18 inches, along one side and one end of the tank so a technician has room to work.

The second number is the separation between the tank and the oil fired appliance it feeds, meaning the furnace or boiler. That one mostly matters for indoor tanks. Roth's manual gives it as a 2 foot minimum under CSA B139 for installations in Canada, against 5 feet under the American NFPA 31 code. [Prince Edward Island's home heat tanks FAQ](https://www.princeedwardisland.ca/sites/default/files/publications/home_heat_tanks_-_frequently_asked_questions.pdf) states a matching figure for that province: at least 2 feet between the tank and the closest part of any fuel fired appliance, with a minimum 18 inch clearance along one end and one side, and a minimum 2 inches between the tank and the wall.

Different documents, but the same shape: a couple of inches so the tank can breathe, roughly 18 inches for a technician to work, and about 2 feet before you reach a burner or boiler. If your installer quotes a different figure for your specific tank, that's normal. Ask which code edition and which manufacturer's instructions they're using.

## Distance from windows, doors, and other vents

This is the one most homeowners actually mean when they ask about "distance from the house": how close the fill pipe or the vent whistle can sit to a window or door.

Prince Edward Island's FAQ is specific here, and the underlying logic applies anywhere oil vapour and a fill pipe opening could reach an opening designed to let air in. It sets:

- A minimum of 2 feet between the vent and fill pipe opening and any door or window designed to be opened.
- The same 2 foot minimum between the vent and fill pipe opening and a dryer vent, an air exchange vent, or any similar vent, except a power intake vent, which needs 3 feet.
- A vent pipe that must terminate at least 6 inches above the entry to the fill pipe.

The same FAQ answers a common follow-up: the vent and fill pipe can terminate 2 feet below the opening of a window. The point of these rules is straightforward: keep the smell of a fill up, and the small amount of oil that can weep from a loose cap, away from anywhere air gets pulled into the house.

## Distance from the property line, a well, and other lot lines

[Nova Scotia's fuel safety bulletin on the 2009 edition of CSA B139](https://novascotia.ca/lae/fuelsafety/docs/FSB0906.pdf) spells out a rule that matters wherever tight suburban lots and outdoor tanks meet. Asked whether a new tank can go where an old one sat on the property line, it answers no: under clause 7.5.4 of that edition the tank must be at least 5 feet from the property line, and there are no grandfather rules for new installations. Granby's installation guidelines make the same point more generally, saying tank location shall respect the local code for distance to property lines.

The same bulletin covers a requirement that surprises homeowners with a tank near a driveway or in a carport: under the 2009 edition, unless the tank's location already protects it, the tank and anything attached to it need protection from vehicles, which in practice means posts or bollards.

For rural properties on a private well, which describes plenty of farmhouses around Salisbury and Memramcook, Prince Edward Island's FAQ gives well separations for new home construction: at least 5 metres (about 16 feet) between the well and the oil tank for tanks of 1,200 litres or less, and at least 15 metres (about 49 feet) for larger tanks. New Brunswick's own well setback figure may differ from PEI's, so treat this as the shape of the concern, keeping fuel storage well clear of a wellhead, rather than a confirmed New Brunswick number. ## Roof edges, drainage, and the ground under the tank

Two placement principles from Granby's and Roth's installation guides aren't distance measurements, but they matter just as much through a Moncton winter.

[Granby's installation guidelines](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0007_Na-ULC-S602-INSTALLATION-AND-MAINTENANCE-GUIDELINES.pdf) for its steel tanks say that, if possible, a tank should not sit directly under house eaves where falling snow, ice, or dripping water can reach it, with protection added if there's no other spot. They also say a tank shouldn't sit in intimate contact with a wall, because leaves and other organic matter build up against the shell and cause external corrosion. Roth's manual makes the same drainage point from the other direction: a tank installed outside has to be protected from runoff coming off higher ground or an adjacent roof, including rainwater that has picked up acids from a wood stove chimney overhead.

Both manuals also stress what the tank sits on. For a masonry base, Roth calls for a one piece concrete slab, a minimum of 4 inches thick for its smaller residential models and extending 6 inches past the tank on every side, with the sod dug out at least 6 inches and replaced with tamped crushed stone, and it points Canadian installers to CSA B139 for the specific pad requirements. Every figure above comes from a specific document from another province or a manufacturer, and the code those documents explain has been revised since (the current CSA B139 is the 2024 series). Treat this table as a starting point for the conversation with whoever inspects or installs your tank, not a substitute for their site visit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How far does an oil tank need to be from a window?

Prince Edward Island's home heat tanks FAQ sets a minimum of 2 feet between a vent or fill pipe opening and any door or window designed to open, and says the pipes can terminate 2 feet below a window opening. That's PEI's figure, not a New Brunswick table, so confirm what applies with whoever installs or inspects your tank.

### Can a new oil tank go back on the property line if the old one sat there?

Not under the 2009 edition of CSA B139 as Nova Scotia's fuel safety bulletin explains it. The bulletin gives a 5 foot minimum from the property line for a new tank and says there are no grandfather rules for new installations, so an old tank's spot doesn't carry over to its replacement. Ask your installer how the current edition treats your lot.

### Does an outdoor tank need room to expand against a wall?

Yes. Roth's installation manual for its double wall tanks calls for a minimum of 2 inches from any wall or fixed structure, because Roth says the tank's sides can move outward by as much as 2 inches once it's full of oil. Other tank manufacturers set their own minimums, so the exact number depends on the tank being installed.

### How close can an oil tank be to a well?

Prince Edward Island's FAQ sets separations of 5 metres for tanks up to 1,200 litres and 15 metres for larger tanks, for new home construction. That's a PEI figure. If your property is on a private well, particularly in a rural area like Salisbury or Memramcook, ask your installer to confirm what applies to your specific setup.

### Why shouldn't an outdoor tank sit under the roof eave?

Granby's installation guidelines say that, if possible, a tank shouldn't sit directly under a house eave, where falling snow, ice, and dripping water land on it, and that it needs protection if there's no other spot.
