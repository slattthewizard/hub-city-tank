# How Much Oil Is in My Tank? Reading a Stick Test and an Oil Tank Chart

The quickest way to find out how much oil is in your tank is to stick it: lower a clean stick to the bottom, pull it out and measure the wet line. The harder part is turning inches into litres. The common 910 litre steel tank has rounded ends, so it doesn't fill at an even rate, and a simple "half the height means half full" guess can be off by a lot near empty. The fix is to read your depth against the manufacturer's chart for your tank's size and orientation.

This guide covers how to stick a tank safely, how to read a real manufacturer chart for a 910 litre tank, how far off the straight-line shortcut can be, and what to do when the stick and the gauge disagree.

## Know your tank's shape and which way it stands

Most basement and outdoor tanks in Southeast New Brunswick are the same basic design: a steel tank on four legs with flat sides and rounded top and bottom edges. Manufacturers call this shape obround. Our guide to [residential oil tank sizes](/blog/residential-oil-tank-sizes/) covers the common capacities and how to tell them apart.

The same obround tank can be installed two ways, and the chart is different for each:

- **Vertical.** Standing on its narrow edge, tall and slim. This is the one you see in most basements. A 910 litre vertical tank stands a bit under four feet tall, not counting the legs.
- **Horizontal.** Lying on its wide side, low and broad, about two feet tall.

Granby Industries, a Quebec tank maker, publishes separate charts for both. Its standard steel tanks are listed in both orientations, and the charts cover the 910 litre (200 imperial gallon) size along with smaller and larger ones. Other makers' tanks of the same nominal size are close but not identical, so use your own maker's chart if you can find the name on the tank's label.

There are also upright double-wall tanks, like Roth's, which have a completely different shape and their own chart. More on those below.

## How to stick your tank

The method takes about five minutes.

Find the fill pipe, the capped pipe the delivery driver uses. On many outdoor tanks it comes straight up out of the top of the tank. Unscrew the cap and lower a clean, dry wooden dowel straight down until it touches bottom. Don't drop it or jab it, since a hard hit can stir up sludge sitting on the floor of an older tank. Pull it straight up and look for the wet line.

Measure that line as precisely as you can, to the nearest quarter inch or the nearest half centimetre. Near the bottom of an obround tank, a single inch can mean the difference between a few litres and a few dozen, so rounding matters.

If your fill pipe runs through the wall and bends on its way into a basement tank, a straight stick won't reach the bottom. Don't force it, and don't take the gauge or any other fitting off the tank to get a stick in. Ask your oil supplier or service technician to check the level instead.

Two safety points. Only remove the fill cap, which is made to come on and off. And if you smell oil, see staining on the tank or floor, or find a wet spot near the base, stop and treat it as a possible leak first. ## Reading Granby's chart for a 910 litre vertical tank

Granby's [capacity chart for vertical tanks](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/GranbyInd_Capacity-Chart_VerticalTanks-1.pdf) lists the volume, in litres and imperial gallons, for every inch of depth (with the centimetre equivalent) for its 455, 680, 910 and 1,111 litre tanks. For the 910 litre size, the chart runs to 46 inches. It carries two warnings worth repeating: precision is about plus or minus 2 percent, and the chart isn't meant for buying or selling product.

Here are reference points from the 910 litre (200 imperial gallon) column. The litre and imperial gallon figures are Granby's. We added the US gallon column, calculated at 3.785 litres per US gallon, because many charts you'll find online are in US gallons.

| Depth on the stick | Litres (Granby) | Imperial gallons (Granby) | US gallons (calculated) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 in (7.6 cm) | 31.1 | 6.84 | 8.2 |
| 6 in (15.2 cm) | 84.1 | 18.50 | 22.2 |
| 12 in (30.5 cm) | 213.8 | 47.02 | 56.5 |
| 23 in (58.4 cm) | 460.4 | 101.28 | 121.6 |
| 30 in (76.2 cm) | 617.4 | 135.81 | 163.1 |
| 36 in (91.4 cm) | 751.9 | 165.39 | 198.6 |
| 40 in (101.6 cm) | 836.8 | 184.07 | 221.1 |
| 44 in (111.8 cm) | 903.7 | 198.79 | 238.7 |

Look at how the numbers climb. The first 6 inches hold only 84 litres. Through the middle of the tank, every inch adds a steady 22.4 litres or so. Near the top, the gain per inch shrinks again. That's the rounded bottom and top of the obround shape showing up in the numbers.

If your 910 litre tank lies on its wide side, use Granby's [capacity chart for horizontal tanks](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/05/si0012-ca-capacity-chart-for-horizontal-tanks.pdf) instead. It runs only to 23 inches for the 910 litre size, because the tank is so much lower. On that chart, 12 inches of oil is 499 litres (110 imperial gallons), a little over half the tank, and 23 inches is 951 litres. Reading a horizontal tank against the vertical chart, or the other way round, will give you nonsense.

## Why straight-line math misleads on an oval tank

If you can't find a chart, the tempting shortcut is proportion: divide the stick depth by the tank's full height and multiply by the capacity. Granby's own numbers show how far off that gets.

Take the 910 litre vertical tank, with the chart topping out at 920.9 litres at 46 inches:

- At **6 inches**, straight-line math says 6 ÷ 46 × 920.9 = about 120 litres. The chart says 84.1. The shortcut overstates what you have by about 36 litres, more than 40 percent.
- At **23 inches**, halfway, the shortcut gives 460.4 litres, exactly what the chart says.
- At **40 inches**, the shortcut says about 801 litres. The chart says 836.8, so near the top the shortcut understates.

The pattern holds for any obround tank. The shortcut is fine through the middle and wrong at both ends, and it's most wrong near empty, which is exactly when you care most. If all you have is the proportion method, treat any reading in the bottom quarter of the tank as less oil than the math says, and order sooner.

A word on online calculators and American charts. A lot of dipstick charts online are for the American "275 gallon" tank, which is 275 US gallons, about 1,041 litres. That's a different tank from a 910 litre (200 imperial gallon) Canadian tank, so its numbers won't match yours even though the tanks look alike.

## Upright double-wall tanks: use that maker's chart

Upright double-wall tanks, like Roth's poly inner tank with a steel outer shell, don't share the obround shape at all. Roth publishes a [fill chart for its EcoDWT plus 3 tanks](https://www.roth-america.com/app/uploads/2022/03/DWT-Fill-Chart.pdf) listing fuel content in US gallons, litres and percent full for each inch (and millimetre) of fuel level, with separate columns for its 400, 620, 1,000 and 1,500 litre models and a separate 1000LH version. Match the model on your tank's label to the right column. The chart also notes that a fuel oil tank can only be filled to 95 percent of capacity, to leave room for the oil to expand as it warms, so the columns stop at 95 percent.

## When the stick and the gauge disagree

This comes up a lot, and the usual answer is to trust the stick.

The gauge on top of most tanks is a float that rides on the oil and moves an indicator in a clear vial. Floats can stick, sink or jam, and the gauge guide linked above covers the common failures. A stick has no moving parts. If the two disagree by more than a little, have the gauge checked rather than relying on it.

Two things can make the stick itself mislead:

- **Water under the oil.** Water is heavier than oil and settles at the bottom, so a stick measures oil and water together. You'll overestimate your oil. A tube of water-finding paste on the end of the stick changes colour where it touches water, which shows how deep the water layer is. Our guide to [water in your oil tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/) covers what to do about it.
- **Sludge on the floor.** A stick that stops on a thick sludge layer reads a little short, and the sludge itself isn't oil your burner can use anyway. A stick that comes up black and gritty at the tip is telling you something about the inside of the tank. Our article on [oil tank sludge](/blog/oil-tank-sludge/) explains what it means.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I convert inches of oil on a stick into litres?

Find your tank's size and whether it stands vertical (tall and narrow) or horizontal (low and wide), then read your depth against the manufacturer's chart for that size and orientation. On Granby's vertical chart for a 910 litre tank, 12 inches is 213.8 litres and 23 inches, the halfway mark, is 460.4 litres.

### Is there a chart for the standard 910 litre oval oil tank?

Yes. Granby publishes capacity charts for its standard steel tanks in both the vertical and horizontal orientations, with a 910 litre (200 imperial gallon) column in each. Other makers' tanks of the same size are similar but not identical, so use your own maker's chart if you can identify it, and treat any chart as accurate to within a few percent.

### Why does my stick reading not match my gauge?

The gauge is a float that can stick, sink or jam, while a stick has nothing to fail. When they disagree, trust the stick and have the gauge checked. Also check the stick for water with water-finding paste, because water at the bottom makes a stick overstate how much oil you have.

### Why is my tank emptier than the straight-line math says?

Because an obround tank is rounded at the bottom. On Granby's 910 litre vertical chart, the first 6 inches hold only 84 litres, while straight-line math says about 120. The proportion shortcut is close through the middle of the tank but overstates what's left near empty.

### Can I use an American 275 gallon tank chart?

Not on a 910 litre tank. A 275 gallon American tank is measured in US gallons, about 1,041 litres, and it's a different size from a 200 imperial gallon (910 litre) Canadian tank. Use a chart that lists your tank's capacity in litres or imperial gallons.
