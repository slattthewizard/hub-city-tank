# Oil Tank and Your Mortgage: Why the Lender Cares and What to Have Ready

A lender doesn't look at your oil tank. Your insurer does, and the lender won't release the money until an insurer has agreed to cover the house. That is how an old oil tank ends up holding up a mortgage. The chain runs tank, then insurance, then lender, and a break at the first link stops the other two.

This guide explains why that chain exists, how a purchase or a refinance can stall on it, and what paperwork to line up so it doesn't. It also ends where the problem usually ends: with a decision about the tank. ## Why a lender cares about an oil tank at all

Home insurance isn't required by law. The Insurance Bureau of Canada says so on its [how to buy home insurance page](https://www.ibc.ca/ns/home/buying-home-insurance), and in the same place it says that before you get a home loan, your lender will likely ask you to buy home insurance, name them as the mortgage lender on your policy, and give them proof of purchase. So the requirement comes from the lender's side, not from the government.

The reason is simple. The house is the security for the loan. If it burns or is badly damaged and nobody has coverage, the lender is left holding a loan against a house worth much less than it was. Proof of insurance is how they avoid that.

It's worth being clear about one thing that confuses people. CMHC mortgage loan insurance is a different product. CMHC's [explainer on mortgage loan insurance](https://cmhc-schl.gc.ca/en/consumers/home-buying/mortgage-loan-insurance-for-consumers/what-is-mortgage-loan-insurance) says it protects your lender if you can't make your payments. It doesn't protect you, and it isn't home insurance. A buyer with a small down payment can have the first and still need the second.

Now bring the tank back in. A heating oil tank is one of the few parts of a house that can create a big bill by itself. A leak can soak into the floor, the soil and sometimes the groundwater, and the owner at the time can be responsible for cleanup. An [Insurance Business article](https://insurancebusinessmag.com/ca/news/environmental/experts-homeowners-should-be-responsible-for-their-oil-tanks-164472.aspx) reports the Insurance Bureau of Canada warning that homeowners can be fully responsible for clean-up costs after an oil spill from their tanks, and that removing the oil and remediating the area can run to hundreds of thousands of dollars. Insurers know this, so they ask about tanks before they agree to cover a house.

That doesn't mean your lender has an oil tank rule. Lender policies differ, so ask yours. The lender needs insurance, and the insurer cares about the tank.

## How a tank stalls a purchase

Take a hypothetical. Say you're buying a 1970s bungalow in Riverview with a steel tank in the basement. Your offer is accepted, you have a financing condition, and you call a broker for a home insurance quote. The broker asks what the house heats with, then asks the age of the tank. If the answer is "I don't know," the broker's next question is usually whether you can find out.

That's where the schedule gets tight. The insurer may want the age, the make, a photo of the label, and sometimes a written inspection before it will bind coverage. The seller may not have any of it. The label may be rusted off. The inspection takes time to book. Meanwhile the lender's file is waiting on one document: proof of insurance.

The usual outcomes are these:

- The insurer accepts the tank as it is, and nothing changes.
- The insurer accepts it on a condition, such as an inspection report or a replacement by a set date.
- The insurer won't cover the house with that tank, and the buyer and seller have to settle who pays to replace it before closing.

The third one is the one that makes closings stressful, and it's the one to prepare for. Our guide to [buying a house with an oil tank](/blog/buying-house-with-oil-tank/) covers the offer side of this in more detail, including what to ask for in conditions. The point here is narrower: insurance is the gate, so ask the insurer early, not at the end of the condition period.

## Refinancing, renewing and other times it comes up

A purchase is the common case, but it isn't the only one. Any time a lender wants fresh proof of insurance, the tank can come up again. That includes a refinance, a switch to a new lender, or a home equity line against the house.

The usual trigger is an insurer's own review of the policy rather than anything the lender asks. Insurers update their questions over time, and a tank that was fine when the policy started can fall outside the current guidelines as it ages. Your mortgage paperwork will spell out what your own lender expects if coverage changes, so read that part before you let a policy lapse.

So an old tank is a mortgage issue even when you aren't moving. If you've had a letter from your insurer, start with our guide on [what to do after an insurer's tank deadline letter](/blog/insurance-deadline-replace-oil-tank/). The mortgage is one more reason not to let that deadline slide.

## What to have ready

If you're buying, selling or refinancing, the insurer will ask for the same handful of facts. CSIO, the Canadian insurance industry body that maintains common forms, publishes a national [Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire) for personal lines. Many brokers use it or something close to it, so it's a good preview of what you'll be asked. These are the facts worth gathering before you call:

### The tank's age and label

Age is usually the first thing an insurer asks. The label or data plate on the tank usually shows the maker, the model, a serial number and the date of manufacture. Photograph it while you can still read it. If it's worn off, our guide to [telling the age of your oil tank](/blog/how-old-is-my-oil-tank/) lists the backup clues, and our guide to [oil tank age and insurance limits](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) explains how insurers use the number. An age you can't prove is often treated the same as an old tank.

### The installation record

If the tank was replaced at some point, the installer's invoice is the best single document. It should show the date, the make and model, the serial number and the capacity. Our guide to [paperwork after a new oil tank installation](/blog/new-oil-tank-paperwork/) lists what a good file contains, including the tank's own documents and a record that the old tank left properly.

### A written condition check

If the tank is middle-aged or the label is missing, a written inspection can answer the insurer's questions on paper. Our guide to [oil tank inspection for insurance](/blog/oil-tank-inspection-for-insurance/) explains what that record covers and how it differs from your own walkaround. Ask your broker whether they need it before you book one, since requirements differ from one insurer to the next.

### Evidence for any tank that was removed

If a previous owner took out an old tank, whether a buried one or an indoor one, the removal receipt is the proof. A buyer's lawyer and a buyer's insurer both ask about this, and "we think it was removed" doesn't carry much weight.

### The insurer's answer, in writing

The most useful document is the insurer's written confirmation that it will cover the house with the tank as it stands. If it says yes with conditions, get the conditions in writing too. With that in hand, the lender's request is a formality.

## Who pays when the tank is the problem

We know of no New Brunswick rule that says who pays to replace a tank when a sale hinges on it. It's a negotiation between buyer and seller, and the common outcomes are a price reduction, a closing credit, or a replacement done before possession.

What helps in that negotiation is a number. A written estimate for replacing the tank turns "the tank is a problem" into a figure both sides can discuss. If you're the seller, the reverse holds. Having a recent installation invoice or an inspection in hand before listing keeps the tank from becoming a late surprise.

## The decision: keep, inspect or replace

The tank in a mortgage situation usually falls into one of three buckets.

**Keep it.** The label is readable, the age is within what your insurer accepts, there's no rust or wet spot, and the insurer has confirmed in writing. Get the paperwork into one folder and move on.

**Inspect it first.** The age is borderline, the label is missing, or the insurer wants more than a photo. A written inspection can settle it. If the report is clean and the insurer accepts it, you've spent a little and avoided a replacement.

**Replace it.** The insurer has said it won't cover the house with that tank, or the tank has visible rust, weeping seams or an unreadable label and is old enough that insurers rarely accept it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I get a mortgage on a house with an oil tank?

Oil-heated homes are bought and financed in New Brunswick, so the tank alone doesn't rule out a mortgage. What your lender will ask for is proof of home insurance, and the insurer decides whether it will cover the house with that tank. Ask an insurer about the tank early in your financing condition period.

### Does my lender check the oil tank?

Lender policies differ, so ask yours. What's well documented is that lenders typically want you to buy home insurance and show proof. The insurer is the one who asks about the tank's age and condition, which is how the tank ends up affecting the mortgage.

### What if the insurer refuses because of the tank?

Ask exactly what they need: a newer tank, an inspection, or proof of age. If the answer is replacement, bring a written estimate to the seller and negotiate a price reduction, a credit or a replacement before closing. A second insurer may answer differently, so it's reasonable to check more than one.

### Does the tank matter if I'm refinancing?

It can. A refinance or a new lender means fresh proof of insurance, and an insurer's questions about tank age can change over time. If your insurer has already written to you about the tank, deal with that first, because a lapsed policy is a problem for your lender as well as for you.

### Does CMHC mortgage insurance cover the oil tank?

No. CMHC mortgage loan insurance protects the lender if you can't make your payments, and it isn't home insurance. It doesn't pay for tank problems or spills. Your home insurance policy, and whatever it says about oil tanks, is the document that matters.
