# Switching From Oil to Propane: What to Do With the Oil Tank

If you're switching from oil to propane, the oil tank stops being your fuel supply the day the new system goes live. The propane company brings its own tank and its own piping, so the old steel or fibreglass oil tank in your basement or beside the house becomes a leftover. In most cases the right move is to remove it, not leave it sitting there. The only real questions are when, and what you do about the oil still inside.

This guide covers only the oil-tank side of a switch. It doesn't compare fuels or talk about propane equipment, because that's your propane company's job. We deal with tanks. ## Why the oil tank becomes redundant

Propane and heating oil are stored in completely different tanks. Propane sits under pressure in a pressure vessel; furnace oil sits at normal pressure in a steel or fibreglass tank with a fill pipe, a vent and an oil line. Nothing about the propane tank can use the oil tank, and the oil tank can't hold propane. So once the new fuel is connected, the oil tank has no job.

Your propane company will tell you whether the new propane tank belongs to them or to you, and who is responsible for it. That's between you and them, and we won't speak for any supplier's terms. What matters for you is simple: whoever owns the propane tank, the oil tank is yours, and it is still your responsibility.

## Why not just leave the oil tank in place

It's tempting to leave an unused tank where it sits. It's quiet, it isn't in the way, and removal costs money. There are good reasons not to.

**It keeps ageing.** An empty or part-empty steel tank still corrodes, and a part-empty tank has more air space inside for condensation to form. Our guide to [how long oil tanks last](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) covers how steel and fibreglass tanks age. An idle tank doesn't pause that clock.

**It holds oil.** Almost every tank has fuel in it, plus a layer of water and sludge on the bottom. Oil that sits for years with no one looking is oil that can find a weak seam.

**It shows up on your insurance and at sale time.** A buyer's inspector sees an oil tank and asks about it. An insurer may ask about its age and condition even if it's idle. A removed tank, with paper, ends the question. Our guide to [selling a house with oil heat](/blog/selling-house-with-oil-heat/) goes through how that plays out with buyers.

**Your province may expect it gone.** The federal Oil to Heat Pump Affordability program page, [Eligibility criteria for the Oil to Heat Pump Affordability program](https://canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/energy-efficiency/home-energy-efficiency/canada-greener-homes-initiative/eligibility-criteria-oil-heat-pump-affordability-program.html), says that if you plan to decommission or remove an oil tank you should check your plans with your home insurance provider and your local authority before you start, and that a province or territory may require removal of a tank no longer in use. That page is about a heat pump program that is closed to new applications, and it isn't about propane, but the caution applies to any switch away from oil. It also says it's your responsibility to verify and follow the relevant regulations.

## When to remove it: the timing

You have two sensible windows.

### Before the propane goes in

If you know the date, burn the oil down first. Stop ordering oil once you've decided to switch, run the furnace on what's in the tank, and book the removal for after the propane system is working. That way the oil you paid for gets used for what you bought it for, and there's less to pump out. Our guide to [what to do with leftover heating oil](/blog/leftover-heating-oil/) goes through this in detail, including why you shouldn't run the tank bone dry on purpose.

The catch is heat. Don't plan to burn the oil down in a way that leaves you cold if the propane install slips. A furnace that's still on oil is your heat until the new system is signed off.

### After the propane is running

Once the propane heat works, the oil furnace is off. This is the point to book the removal. Don't let a part-full tank sit "until spring." If it's a New Brunswick winter and the tank is outdoors, snow and ice sliding off a roof or a leaning stand are exactly the things that cause trouble on an idle tank nobody is checking.

## What happens to the oil still in the tank

There's nearly always some left. The burner never draws a tank to zero, and water and sludge stay on the bottom.

Whoever removes the tank pumps out what's inside. Ask for the estimate to say how the oil is handled and whether that's included. Don't try to drain it yourself into pails or drums. Our [leftover oil guide](/blog/leftover-heating-oil/) explains why.

Two points that are worth saying plainly:

- **Don't top up a tank you're about to remove.** If you're on automatic delivery, cancel it in writing as soon as you decide to switch, so a truck doesn't fill a tank that's about to go.
- **Don't try to use the oil for something else** because it's "clean." Furnace oil belongs in an oil burner or in the hands of whoever pumps it out.

## The fill pipe risk

This detail is easy to miss. The oil tank's fill pipe runs to the outside wall. After a switch, that pipe is still there, still capped, and still looks like a working fill point. A driver sent to your address by an automatic delivery account, or by mistake, can connect to it.

Cancel deliveries in writing. Then make sure the fill pipe and vent come out with the tank, or are sealed solid and marked out of service if they can't. Our guide to [oil tank fill and vent pipes](/blog/oil-tank-fill-and-vent-pipes/) explains what these pipes are and why they're replaced at a swap; after a switch away from oil, the only question is whether they come out or are sealed.

## Should you keep the oil tank as backup?

Sometimes a household wonders about keeping oil as a backup to the new propane system. Usually it doesn't make sense, for plain reasons:

1. **Two fuel systems means two sets of upkeep.** An oil tank needs yearly attention and the furnace it feeds needs servicing, even when it hardly runs.
2. **Idle oil goes stale.** Oil that sits for a season or two in a tank that isn't being turned over is worse oil, with more water to settle and cause sludge.
3. **A propane system has its own supply and its own plan.** The Canadian Propane Association's [winter preparedness page](https://propane.ca/safety/winter-preparedness/) advises filling your propane tank early before cold weather raises demand, keeping tank access clear of snow, and asking your provider about scheduled visits so you don't run empty. That's the propane side of avoiding a cold house, and it doesn't involve keeping oil.

The exception is a home where propane is going to heat only part of the house, or where you're running a trial before you commit. In that case the oil tank is a working tank again, and it has to be sound. If it's old, rusty or leaning, our [small oil tank for backup heat](/blog/small-oil-tank-backup-heat/) guide covers whether downsizing makes sense. If you want a backup of any kind, keep the tank you'd trust and remove the one you wouldn't.

## The decision

Put together, the plan is short:

- **Decide the date.** Propane working, then removal.
- **Cancel oil delivery in writing.**
- **Burn down what you can**, without leaving yourself cold.
- **Tell your insurer**, and check what your municipality and province expect.
- **Remove the tank, fill pipe and vent together**, with oil pumped out by whoever removes it.
- **Keep the invoice.** It is the proof the tank is gone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to remove my oil tank if I switch to propane?

We can't point to a rule that says you must in every case. Rules differ by province and by municipality, and the federal program page linked above says a province or territory may require removal of a tank no longer in use. Check with your insurer and local authority, and expect a buyer or insurer to ask about an unused tank sooner or later.

### Can I use my old oil tank to store propane?

No. Propane is stored under pressure in a vessel built for it, and an oil tank isn't. Your propane company supplies its own tank. The oil tank isn't part of the new system and can't be converted.

### What happens to the oil left in my tank?

Whoever removes the tank pumps out what's left, including the water and sludge at the bottom. Burn the oil down before removal if your timing allows, but don't run it dry on purpose. Ask the estimate how the leftover oil is handled.

### Can I keep the oil furnace as a backup?

You can, but the tank has to be in good shape and kept up as an active tank, and you'd be running two fuel systems. For most homes that isn't worth it. If you do keep it, have the tank checked, and tell your insurer how the home is heated.

### Who cancels my oil delivery?

You do. The account is yours, and no one else will know to stop it. Write to the oil company with the date of the switch and ask to close the account or mark the address as having no oil tank. Keep a copy.
