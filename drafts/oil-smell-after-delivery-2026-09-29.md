# Oil Smell After a Delivery: What's Normal and What Isn't

A faint oil smell for a few hours after your tank gets topped off is common, and on its own it usually isn't a problem. A little splash at the fill cap, some fumes pushed out through the vent as the tank tops off, a whiff that clings to the yard around an outdoor tank on a still day: all normal, and all should fade within a day.

What isn't normal is a smell that hangs on past a day, gets stronger instead of weaker, or shows up with a wet patch on the tank, the wall, or the basement floor. That combination usually means the delivery didn't cause the problem so much as it exposed one: a loose fitting, an overfilled tank, or a weak spot that only weeps when the oil is sitting right at the top.

This guide sticks to the delivery-day case. ## Why a Fill Can Leave a Smell Behind

A delivery moves a large amount of oil into your tank in a short time. Roth's [installation manual for its double-wall tank](https://www.roth-america.com/app/uploads/2021/02/DWT_Installation_Manual_2023.pdf), for example, gives a fill rate of 150 to 300 litres a minute. A few things happen along the way that have nothing to do with a leak.

**Splash at the fill cap.** The driver connects a hose to the fill pipe, and disconnecting it afterward almost always leaves a drop or two on the cap threads. On an outdoor tank this dries or washes off in the next rain. On an indoor fill pipe that runs through a wall, a bit of that smell drifts into the basement before it airs out.

**Fumes pushed out the vent.** As oil rises inside the tank, it pushes the air ahead of it out through the vent pipe. That's the same air movement that makes the vent whistle hum during a normal fill. The air that leaves the tank carries oil vapour with it, so the area around the vent cap can smell of oil for a while afterward, especially if the tank was filled close to capacity. Outdoors, that vents into open air and disperses fast. If your vent sits near a window or a basement air intake, you'll notice it more.

**The tank settling after a fill.** A tank that's been running low for weeks sits differently once it's full. Sediment on the bottom shifts, a stand or leg that was taking uneven weight can flex slightly, and any part that was barely holding on, a gasket, a compression fitting, gets tested by the added weight and pressure it hasn't felt in a while. Most of the time nothing comes of it. Sometimes it's the first sign of something that needed attention anyway.

None of this means every delivery smell is fine to ignore. It means the smell alone, on the day of the fill, isn't automatically bad news. What matters next is how it behaves over the following day or two.

## What's Normal in the First Day

| What you notice | How long it lasts | What it usually means |
|---|---|---|
| Faint smell right at the fill cap or vent | A few hours to overnight | Normal splash or venting; should wipe or air off clean |
| Smell drifting from outside toward a window or basement | Fades by evening | Still likely normal, especially on a calm day or near a low spot by the foundation |
| Smell still noticeable the next day | More than 24 hours | Worth a look: a loose fitting, or a weep the full tank exposed |
| A wet patch at the fill pipe, the cap, or the base of the tank | Any duration | Get it checked; could be a loose connection or an overfill |
| Smell strongest right where the tank sits, with a stain that's growing | Any duration | Treat as a possible tank leak and get it looked at promptly |

The line to watch is the 24-hour mark. Fumes and splash residue air out. Oil that's actually escaping doesn't get better on its own, it either holds steady or gets worse.

## When It's a Loose Fill Pipe Fitting, Not the Delivery

The fill pipe runs from your tank's fill collar out to the wall, roof, or an outdoor stub where the driver connects the hose. On older setups, especially galvanized steel pipe that's been through a lot of Southeast New Brunswick winters, the joints along that run can loosen or corrode: the coupling at the tank collar, an elbow where the pipe changes direction, the cap itself.

A gentle trickle rarely finds a weak joint like that. A full-speed delivery, with oil moving fast and the line under real pressure for a few minutes, will. If the smell traces to a specific spot along the fill pipe rather than the tank body, that's usually good news: it's a fitting problem, not a tank problem, and it's a contained repair rather than a replacement. Our guide to [oil tank parts](/blog/oil-tank-parts-explained/) walks through the fill pipe, vent, gauge, and valve if you want to know what you're looking at before anyone comes out.

Wipe the joint dry, note where the wet spot reappears, and don't crank on any fitting yourself. Threaded connections on an old steel fill pipe can be brittle, and forcing one can turn a small weep into a real problem.

## Overfill: When the Tank Went in Too Full

Most home tanks filled from outside have a vent alarm, usually a small whistling fitting in the vent line, that hums while oil is flowing and goes quiet once the level reaches the alarm's tube near the top. That silence is the driver's signal to stop, and it's meant to leave some air space at the top of the tank.

When that space gets squeezed too thin, whether from a gauge that's read wrong, a whistle that cut out late, or oil that expands as it warms through the day, the tank has nowhere for that expansion to go. A little oil can back up the fill pipe or push out through the vent, leaving a smell right where the two pipes meet the tank. Our full breakdown of [the vent whistle and why silence during a fill matters](/blog/oil-tank-vent-whistle/) covers how that alarm works and what makes it fail.

If your delivery topped off right at the gauge's full mark and the smell is centred on the fill or vent fittings rather than spread around the tank's base, an overfill is the first thing to rule out. It's also the easiest to confirm: check the gauge against what you expect from the litres delivered, and look for oil staining specifically around the top fittings rather than the bottom.

## The Weep That Only Shows Up When the Tank Is Full

This is the case that catches people off guard. A steel tank corrodes from the inside, and a pinhole or weak seam partway up the side, or a loose fitting near the top, stays dry while the oil level sits below it. Fill the tank right to the top after a long stretch of running low, and oil reaches that spot for the first time in weeks. A thin spot lower down also has more oil pressing on it when the tank is full, so a slow weep can show up or speed up after a big fill.

That's why a smell that shows up for the first time right after a big fill, on a tank that's been running low for a while, deserves more attention than the same smell after a routine top-up. If you find a wet or oily film on the tank body itself, not just at a fitting, that's the pattern covered in our guide on [what a wet spot under your oil tank means](/blog/wet-spot-under-oil-tank/), and it's worth working through that checklist right away.

Age matters here too. Oil weeping through the body of an older steel tank, rather than from a fitting, is telling you something a fitting repair won't fix. ## If Oil Reached the Ground, a Drain, or Water

Most delivery-day smells stay contained to a fitting or a cap. But if you can see oil on the ground outside, in a floor drain, a sump pit, or anywhere near a well, that's no longer a wait-and-see situation.

In New Brunswick, a leak or suspected leak has to be reported. The province's [Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation](https://www.canlii.org/en/nb/laws/regu/nb-reg-87-97/latest/nb-reg-87-97.html) says anyone who suspects or detects that petroleum is leaking or has leaked from a tank system must notify the province immediately, and outside normal office hours it names 1-800-565-1633. The federal [report an environmental emergency](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program/report-emergency.html) page lists that same toll-free number for New Brunswick, answered by the Canadian Coast Guard's Maritimes office. Our post on [reporting an oil spill in NB](/blog/report-oil-spill-nb/) covers what the call involves.

Reporting doesn't make the situation worse. Waiting to see if it dries up on its own is what turns a contained spill into a bigger cleanup.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal to smell oil after a delivery?

Yes, for a few hours to about a day. A little splash at the fill cap and some fumes pushed out the vent as the tank tops off are both normal and should fade on their own. If the smell is still noticeable the next day, or it's getting stronger rather than weaker, that's no longer just delivery residue and it's worth a closer look.

### How long should the oil smell last after a fill up?

Most delivery-day smells clear within a few hours to overnight, faster outdoors than indoors. Give it about a day. A smell that's still there 24 hours later, especially one centred on a specific fitting or on the tank body itself, points to something that needs attention rather than time.

### What if I smell oil outside right after a delivery, but there's no visible spill?

Check the fill cap and the vent fitting first; those are the two most common sources of a lingering outdoor smell. A calm day with no wind can also hold a normal delivery smell close to the ground longer than usual. If there's no oil on the ground and the smell fades within a day, it was most likely ordinary venting or splash.

### Could a loose fill pipe fitting really cause a smell days after the delivery is done?

Yes. A joint that's marginal can hold once the pressure of a fast fill eases off, then weep slowly for days afterward at a much lower rate. If the smell persists and traces to a specific joint along the fill pipe rather than the tank itself, that's usually exactly what's happening, and it's a contained fix rather than a tank problem.

### When does an oil smell after a delivery mean the tank itself is leaking?

Two signs stand out: the smell is strongest at the tank's base or bottom seam rather than at the fill or vent, and it comes with an actual oily film or wet patch on the tank body. Combine that with a tank older than 15 years and a fill that topped it off after running low for a while, and a tank problem is the more likely explanation.

### Do I have to report an oil smell after a delivery to anyone?

A brief smell from splash or venting isn't a leak. But New Brunswick's Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation says anyone who suspects or detects a leak from a tank system must report it immediately, and outside office hours the number is 1-800-565-1633.
