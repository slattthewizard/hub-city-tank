# Mobile Home and Mini Home Oil Tanks: Stands, Clearances and Replacement

If you heat a mobile home, a mini home or a trailer in Southeast New Brunswick with oil, your tank almost certainly sits outside on a stand, a few feet from the skirting, with no basement to put it in. That changes a few things: how the tank is supported, what an insurer's inspection form asks about, and what replacement involves when the tank gets old or the stand starts to move.

This guide covers the parts of an outdoor tank setup that matter most at a mobile or mini home: the stand and the ground under it, clearances near skirting and windows, what insurance paperwork typically records, and what a straight replacement costs in Greater Moncton.

## Why Mobile and Mini Homes Almost Always Mean an Outdoor Tank

A mini or mobile home doesn't have a basement, so the indoor-versus-outdoor question that most homeowners face doesn't really apply. The tank goes outside, on a stand, exposed to the same freeze-thaw winters and snow as any other outdoor tank in Westmorland, Albert or Kent counties, plus salt air near the coast. We've written a full comparison of [indoor vs outdoor oil tanks](/blog/indoor-vs-outdoor-oil-tank/) if you want the general case; this article is about the specific conditions an outdoor tank faces when the home it feeds has no basement.

That lack of a basement matters for the tank in a practical way. The tank, usually its filter, and the first stretch of oil line all live in the weather year round, and there's no dry mechanical room to protect them. On a park lot or a small rural lot, the tank can also end up closer to the skirting, the driveway or a neighbour's property line than it would at a full-size house.

## The Stand, the Ground, and Frost Heave Under a Mini Home

An outdoor tank at a mini home carries the same weight problem as any other outdoor tank. Furnace oil weighs roughly 0.85 kilograms per litre, so the oil alone in a full 910 litre tank (a common size) comes to about 770 kilograms, before you add the tank itself, all resting on four legs. We've covered the mechanics of that in our guides to [oil tank stands and pads](/blog/oil-tank-stand-and-pad/) and to [oil tank leaning or sinking](/blog/oil-tank-leaning-sinking/), and the same physics apply here: freeze-thaw cycles heave and settle the ground under the pad, clay and fill soils hold water that makes the heaving worse, and a stand on bare ground or old patio stones moves more than one on a proper concrete base.

If the ground under your stand is fill, or was dug up and levelled when the home was set, it can keep settling for years, and a small lot may leave less room for a wide gravel base. None of that is a reason to skip the base work. It's a reason to check it more often.

One manufacturer's installer instructions put some specifics on this. Granby's [installer instructions for its 2 in 1 fuel oil tanks](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0018_Ga-Granby-2-in-1-Installer-Instructions-1.pdf) say the tank must be anchored to its foundation if it's installed outside (or in an area subject to earthquakes or floods). The same document recommends at least 2 inches (50 millimetres) of clearance from walls or other obstructions for normal tank expansion and visual inspection, says outdoor tanks should be protected with barriers, including against collision with vehicles, and says tanks shouldn't sit directly under house eaves where falling snow, ice or dripping water can hit them. Those are one maker's instructions for one tank line, not a universal rule, so confirm the requirements for your tank and site with whoever installs it.

## Clearance Around Skirting, Windows and the Property Line

Skirting creates a specific problem an outdoor tank at a house doesn't usually have: a continuous wrap around the base of the home that's easy to build right up against the tank's stand, blocking the access a technician needs to check the legs, the fill, the vent and the filter.

Keep the area around the tank clear on all sides. That means skirting panels stop short of the stand rather than boxing it in, snow gets cleared rather than banked against the legs, and nothing gets stored against the tank the way people sometimes stack firewood or storage totes against a shed. Our guide to [protecting your tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/) covers the winter side of this in more detail.

The insurance industry's national form for this, the [Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire) published by CSIO, gives a sense of what gets checked on an outdoor tank. For an outdoor installation, it asks for the shortest distance from the tank to the nearest window, door, air intake vent, property line, drilled well, septic bed, drainage ditch, storm drain, manhole cover and open water source, and whether the tank is painted with a light-coloured, rust-proof paint. It doesn't set any minimum distances itself. It's the record an insurer or installer fills in, and the list of distances tells you what the people writing the policy pay attention to.

| What the form records (outdoor tank) | What that distance or detail is about |
|---|---|
| Distance to window, door, air intake vent | Fumes and fire risk near openings into the home |
| Distance to property line | How close a spill would be to a neighbour's land |
| Distance to drilled well or open water source | Drinking water and groundwater |
| Distance to septic bed | Oil reaching the septic field |
| Distance to drainage ditch, storm drain, manhole cover | Where a spill would run if it got away |
| Light-coloured, rust-proof paint | Corrosion protection on an exposed steel shell |

On a small lot, several of those distances can be tighter than they'd be on a bigger property, and plenty of rural homes around [Salisbury](/oil-tank-service-salisbury/), Memramcook and Sackville run on a private well, which makes the well-distance line on the form more than a formality. If you're not sure how your tank measures up on any of these, someone can measure it up when they come out to look at the tank.

## What Insurers Tend to Ask About a Mini Home's Tank

Beyond the clearance measurements, the same CSIO questionnaire records the tank's year of manufacture, its construction, what it's sitting on (support and stand type), its condition (rust, corrosion, weeping, wet spots, dents), and whether the tank, fuel line and valve are protected from impact, with vehicles and ice given as examples. It also asks whether the installer was a certified oil burner technician. None of that is specific to mini homes; it's a national form. What can be different at a mini home is the site: if the tank sits near a driveway or under a roof edge, the impact question is the one to have a good answer for.

If you've received a letter from your insurer about a tank at a mini or mobile home, the same questions apply as at any other house: how old is the tank, is it sitting on a stable base, and can it be reached and inspected without pulling skirting apart. Confirm what your own policy requires with your broker, since each insurer sets its own rules, and some use their own questionnaire instead of the CSIO one.

## Replacing an Oil Tank at a Mobile or Mini Home

A tank swap at a mobile or mini home follows the same steps as any outdoor replacement: pump out what's left in the old tank, disconnect the lines, remove the old tank and its stand, set a new base, install the new tank, fit new connections, and transfer the clean oil back or refill. A few things tend to shape the quote at a mini home specifically. Access matters, since a tight gap between the home and a fence, shed or neighbouring unit means more careful handling than a wide-open lot. It often makes sense to redo the stand and pad at the same time as the tank, because a new tank set on a base that's already failing inherits the same problem. And if skirting panels need to come off or be reset to get at the tank or the line, mention it when you book the estimate so it's accounted for up front.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a mobile or mini home need a different type of oil tank than a house?

Generally no. The tank choices, steel or fibreglass, single wall or double wall, are the same products used at any home. What's different is that it almost always sits outside on a stand, since mobile and mini homes don't have a basement. That means the tank has to be one rated for outdoor use, and the maker's outdoor installation instructions apply.

### How much clearance does an outdoor tank need from skirting?

There's no single number that applies everywhere; it depends on the tank, the site and the code your installer works to. The practical rule is to leave enough room that someone can reach all sides of the stand, the fill, the vent and the filter without removing skirting first. Granby's 2 in 1 installer instructions recommend at least 2 inches (50 millimetres) from walls for expansion and inspection; confirm the figure for your own tank with your installer.

### Can frost heave move a tank stand at a mobile home?

Yes. Freeze-thaw cycles heave and resettle the ground under a stand each winter, and clay or loose fill that holds water makes it worse. A stand on bare ground or loose patio stones moves more than one on a solid base. If your stand shows a gap under a leg or a visible lean, get it looked at before the next freeze-thaw cycle.

### Do insurers treat a mini home's oil tank differently than a house's?

Not on paper. The national CSIO questionnaire records the same details for any home: the tank's year of manufacture, construction, condition, support type, impact protection, and for outdoor tanks the distances to windows, doors, wells, septic beds and property lines. Each insurer sets its own rules on top of that, so confirm your own policy's requirements with your broker.

### What does it cost to replace an oil tank at a mobile or mini home?

Since these are almost always outdoor installs, the relevant ranges are the outdoor tank removal, new tank, and full swap figures on our [oil tank replacement cost](/oil-tank-replacement-cost/) page. Tight access near skirting or a stand that needs full rebuilding can move a quote toward the higher end of those ranges. A written estimate will give you a firm number for your site.

### Does an outdoor tank at a mini home need to be anchored to the ground?

It depends on the tank. Granby's installer instructions for its 2 in 1 tanks, for example, say the tank must be anchored to its foundation when it's installed outside. Other makers set their own requirements, so check the instructions for your tank model with your installer rather than assuming one setup applies to every make.
