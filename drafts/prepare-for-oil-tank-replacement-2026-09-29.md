# Getting Ready for Oil Tank Replacement Day: A Homeowner's Checklist

You've booked the swap. Now what? Most of the work on replacement day belongs to the crew, but there are a handful of things worth doing beforehand: a bit of prep on the tank itself, a clear path to it, a plan for the pets and the few hours the heat is off, and a short list of what to ask for before the truck pulls away.

None of it takes long. For most houses the whole list fits into an hour or so, spread over the week before the appointment and the morning of.

## The Week Before: Lock In the Plan

If you haven't already, get a written estimate before you pick a date. A full swap in our area runs $3,500 to $6,400 CAD depending on the tank you choose, where it sits, and how easy it is to get the old one out; our [oil tank replacement cost guide](/oil-tank-replacement-cost/) breaks down what pushes a job toward either end of that range. Once the number and the tank type are settled, booking the day is the easy part.

Two things worth confirming with whoever does the estimate:

- Whether your tank is outdoor, basement, or an awkward spot like a garage or crawl space, since that changes how much room the crew needs and roughly how long they'll be there.
- Roughly how many litres you expect to have in the tank on the day you've picked, so the visit and the job line up.

If the appointment is more than a week or two out, keep ordering oil as usual in the meantime. Don't let the tank sit low for weeks on end just because a date is on the calendar; a nearly empty tank invites condensation and rust the same way it does at any other time of year, a point we get into in our post on [keeping your oil tank full over the summer](/blog/keep-oil-tank-full-summer/). The low-tank window that matters is the last few days before the swap, not the whole gap between booking and the appointment.

## How Low Should the Tank Be on Replacement Day?

You don't need to run the tank bone dry, and you shouldn't try to. A lower tank simply means less oil for the crew to pump into holding drums and filter back into the new tank, which can shave a bit of time off the job. Around a quarter tank is plenty. The last few centimetres at the bottom are where water and sludge collect, and a furnace drawing from a nearly empty tank is more likely to pull that muck into the filter and stall. There's also no advantage to draining the last few litres yourself, and doing it wrong (loosening a fitting, tipping a drain valve open) is exactly the kind of thing that turns a routine prep job into a spill on your own property.

How fast you get to a quarter depends on the season. In a cold snap a house can burn a quarter of a 910 litre tank in a week or two; in May or September it might take a month. Look at the gauge when you book, and ask your supplier to hold or shrink the last delivery rather than skip it outright. If the swap gets pushed back, don't skip an order out of fear of "wasting" oil before the new tank goes in. The oil in the old tank gets filtered and moved into the new one, so a quarter tank that lasts an extra week costs you nothing extra. A tank that runs completely dry before the crew arrives leaves you in exactly the situation we cover in our guide on [what to do if you run out of heating oil](/blog/ran-out-of-heating-oil/): a cold house, a scramble for fuel (often with an after-hours delivery fee from your supplier), and a burner that may need its line bled before it will restart.

## Clearing the Path: Basement, Yard, and Everything Between

The crew needs a clear route from the tank to the nearest usable door, roughly a metre wide, and a spot for the truck to back in close. A few minutes of clearing beforehand keeps the job moving and protects your floors and furniture.

**For a basement tank:** clear anything stored along the route, from the tank to the stairs to the exit. Older Moncton and Riverview bungalows often have narrow stairwells and tight doorways, and a standard tank that won't fit through in one piece has to be emptied and taken apart on site by the crew, with the vapours managed, which takes longer. That's crew work, never a homeowner job. Ask at the estimate stage whether your access looks like a whole-tank removal or a cut-apart job, so you're not surprised on the day.

**For an outdoor tank:** move vehicles so the truck can pull in, and check the ground around the stand. In winter, clear snow and ice from the path and from the tank itself, especially if it sits under a roof edge where sliding snow tends to pile up around the legs and stand. Our guide on [protecting your oil tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/) covers what to watch for the rest of the season, but on replacement day the goal is simpler: give the crew a clear, level area to work in.

**Either way:** keep pets shut in a room away from the work area for the day, and let anyone else in the house know the furnace will be off for a stretch mid-job. If you've got a soft or sloped yard, mention it when you book; a plywood path or timing the job for drier ground can save a lot of hassle.

## A Few Hours Without Heat: How to Stay Warm

Your furnace or boiler gets shut off for the swap and stays off until the new tank is connected, filled, and the burner's been fired and tested. Our [step-by-step guide to the replacement process](/blog/oil-tank-replacement-process/) walks through what happens during that window if you want the full picture; from the homeowner's side, the main thing is planning around a few heatless hours rather than a whole day.

A mild day makes this a non-issue. On a cold one, close doors to rooms you're not using, and if you want extra warmth, stick to an electric space heater kept clear of anything flammable. Never bring a fuel-burning heater indoors as a stopgap, even for a short outage. Health Canada's [guidance on preventing carbon monoxide exposure](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/pollutants/carbon-monoxide/preventing-exposure.html) is direct on this point: kerosene, propane, and oil space heaters don't belong in enclosed spaces unless they're specifically designed for indoor use. If anyone in the house is elderly, very young, or has a health condition that makes a cold few hours risky, plan for them to be somewhere else, or at least somewhere warmer, while the work happens.

## If What You Find Isn't Low Oil, It's a Leak

While you're walking around the tank to clear a path or check the gauge, take a second look at the tank itself. A wet spot on the floor or ground, a strong oil smell that wasn't there before, or a rust weep along a seam isn't a preparation task, it's a different problem. Don't try to move a leaking tank, tighten a fitting, or drain it yourself. Our guide on [what to do with a leaking oil tank](/blog/oil-tank-leaking-what-to-do/) covers the first steps, and New Brunswick's page on [reporting a contaminant release](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/corporate/promo/contaminated-sites-program/reporting.html) says a suspected petroleum leak should be reported to the Department of Environment and Local Government right away (the regional office during business hours, the environmental emergencies line after hours). ## When the Crew Leaves: What to Ask For

Before the truck pulls out of the driveway, make sure you've got a few things in hand:

- **Written confirmation of the install date**, often on a tag attached to the new tank as well as on the invoice. Keep a photo of it on your phone in addition to any paper copy.
- **Paperwork for your insurer.** If a tank-age letter started all this, your insurer will usually want proof the old tank is gone and details of the new one (install date, material, who installed it). Ask your broker exactly what they need before replacement day, and make sure you have it before the crew leaves rather than chasing it down later.
- **A record that the old tank was hauled away**, not left in the yard or driveway. Steel tanks are almost entirely recyclable, so there's no reason a removed tank should linger.
- **A quick walkthrough of the new setup.** Where's the gauge, the vent whistle, the filter, and did the fill pipe move? Two minutes on this saves confusion the next time an oil truck shows up.
- **Confirmation the furnace is running.** Ask to see or hear it complete a full cycle before you consider the job done, not just that it fired once.

If anything on that list is missing, ask before the crew leaves. It's much easier to sort out on the spot than after the fact.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How low should I let my oil tank get before replacement day?

About a quarter tank is plenty. It gives the crew less oil to transfer into holding drums and back into the new tank, which can trim time off the job. Don't run it dry: the bottom of the tank is where water and sludge sit, and a nearly empty tank is more likely to clog the filter. Draining the last few litres yourself risks a spill for no real benefit.

### Will I be without heat for the whole day of the swap?

No. The furnace or boiler is off only while the tanks are being switched over, typically a stretch of a few hours in the middle of the day, not the whole day. Our guide to the replacement process has the full breakdown of what happens step by step and roughly how long each part takes.

### What should I do with pets during the appointment?

Keep them shut in a room away from the work area, whether that's the basement, garage, or yard. There will be a crew moving equipment and an old tank through your home or property, and an anxious or curious pet underfoot slows the job down and isn't safe for anyone.

### Do I need to be home the entire time?

It helps to be reachable, especially at the start for questions about access and at the end to review the paperwork and confirm the furnace is running properly. Some homeowners step out for part of the middle of the job once the crew is underway; just make sure someone can let them know if plans change.

### What if my tank runs dry before the scheduled appointment?

Get fuel in the same way you would at any other time, and see our guide on running out of heating oil for the options in Southeast New Brunswick. A small delivery or a short-term top-up doesn't cause any trouble for the swap, since whatever is in the old tank gets filtered and moved into the new one. What you want to avoid is a cold house and a furnace that has sucked sludge into its filter.

### What paperwork should I keep after the new tank is installed?

Keep the installation record or tag showing the date, any documentation your insurer asked for, and confirmation that the old tank was removed and taken for recycling. Photograph the tag as a backup to the paper copy, and send whatever your broker requested sooner rather than later, since renewal time is when it usually gets asked for again.
