# Your Oil Tank Filter Keeps Clogging? Here's What That's Telling You About the Tank

If your heating oil filter is plugging every few weeks, the filter is not the problem. It is the messenger. A cartridge filter sits on the oil line, often right at the tank outlet, to catch whatever the tank is shedding into the fuel: water, sludge, and rust flaking off the steel from the inside. One clogged filter right after a delivery is normal. A pattern of clogs, season after season, means something inside the tank needs a look, not another cartridge.

This guide covers what the filter catches, why the housing sometimes weeps after a change, how often it should be inspected and swapped, and how to read a fast-clogging filter as a signal about the tank behind it. ## What the Oil Tank Filter Actually Catches

The filter housing sits on the fuel line between the tank outlet and the burner, usually a canister with a replaceable cartridge inside. On a basement tank it's often right at the tank valve. With an outdoor tank it's often mounted indoors instead: General Filters' instructions for its common canister filter tell installers to put it in a heated space, because water collected in the bowl can freeze and damage the filter or make it leak. Its job is narrow: strain out whatever is riding in the fuel before it reaches the pump and the nozzle, an opening small enough that even fine grit can block it.

Three things typically show up in a spent cartridge from a residential tank. Rust particles, shed from the inside of a corroding steel tank. Sludge, the thick black mix of aged fuel, water, and microbial waste that settles on the tank bottom. And water itself, sitting below the oil and getting drawn up whenever the level runs low or a delivery churns the bottom. A cartridge can hold back a little water, but it isn't built to deal with a tank that has a steady supply of it.

The filter is part of the same system that CSA B139, the national installation code for oil-burning equipment, singles out for attention. Its 10th edition (2019) added a requirement for an annual visual inspection for leakage covering fuel oil tanks, tubing, piping, and fuel oil filters together, not the filter alone, according to [CSA Group's summary of that edition's changes](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/). That pairing is not an accident. The filter and the tank are one system, and a filter problem is often a tank problem showing up downstream.

## Why an Oil Filter Housing Leaks After a Change

A filter housing that weeps a few drops right after a cartridge change is usually a workmanship issue at the fitting, not a sign of anything wrong with the tank. The gasket between the canister top and base has to seat evenly all the way around, and a nick, a fold, or a bit of grit under it will leave a slow seep. Over-tightening the centre bolt can distort that same seal instead of improving it. If the filter head has a bleed screw, it has to be closed fully once a clean stream of oil comes out during bleeding, and a screw left a quarter-turn loose will keep dripping. Bleeding and cartridge changes are normally a service technician's job; if you do it yourself, shut the burner power and the oil valve off first.

None of that points to the tank itself. A weep at the filter housing stays right at the housing, dripping onto the floor below that one fitting. A tank that is leaking behaves differently: it weeps along a seam or the bottom of the shell, often with a rust blister or flaking paint nearby. If you are trying to tell the two apart, our guide to a [wet spot under an oil tank](/blog/wet-spot-under-oil-tank/) walks through the paper towel test and the other tells that separate a loose fitting from a failing tank.

## How Often Should an Oil Tank Filter Be Checked and Changed

CSA Group's summary of the code doesn't mention a cartridge-change interval. What it describes is an inspection: under the 10th edition of CSA B139, the annual visual leak check that covers the tank also covers the tubing, piping, and filters, as noted above. For the cartridge itself, look to the filter maker. [General Filters' installation sheet for its 1A-25 series](https://manuals.plus/general-filters/1a-25b-fuel-oil-filter-manual) says the cartridge must be replaced annually, and that on two-pipe systems, where the full pump flow passes through the filter, it may need replacing more often. Other brands may say something different, so check the instructions for the filter you have. On the tank side, manufacturer Granby Industries says on its [questions and answers page](https://www.granbyindustries.com/en-us/petroleum-tanks/questions-and-answers/) that "all tanks should be inspected and serviced annually."

That makes the yearly service the natural time for a new cartridge, along with a look at the nozzle and the rest of the burner. A clean tank should get through a full year on one cartridge. A tank that is shedding water, sludge, or rust will plug a filter well before the year is up, sometimes more than once. That gap between "changed once a year at service" and "changed three times before Christmas" is the pattern worth paying attention to.

## What a Fast-Clogging Filter Says About the Tank Behind It

A single clogged filter after a fuel delivery is not a diagnosis. Deliveries stir the tank bottom, and a slug of sediment heading for the filter right after a fill-up is a known, mostly harmless nuisance. What matters is repetition. Two, three, or more clogs in one season, especially if they show up after every delivery or every cold stretch, means the tank is the source, not the filter.

**Water.** Condensation forms on the inside of a steel tank as temperatures swing through a New Brunswick year, and that water sinks below the oil since it is heavier. It sits quietly until the level runs low or a delivery churns it up, at which point it heads for the pickup line and the filter right behind it. Our guide to [water in your oil tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/) covers where it comes from and how it is removed.

**Sludge.** Aged fuel breaks down over time, and the heavier compounds that fall out combine with water and microbial waste at the fuel-water boundary to form a thick black paste on the tank bottom. It is the single most common cause of a filter that clogs again and again, and our article on [oil tank sludge](/blog/oil-tank-sludge/) goes through the causes and the fixes in detail.

**Rust.** Steel tanks corrode from the inside out, starting along the bottom where water and sludge sit against the steel year after year. The flakes that come off are grit, and grit packs a filter fast. A tank that keeps producing rust flakes is a tank whose walls are thinning, which is really a question of the tank's age and condition rather than its filter. Our guide to [how long oil tanks last](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) has the numbers on when steel tanks typically reach that point.

If the furnace keeps locking out even with a fresh cartridge in, the problem may sit further along: a partly blocked line, the pump screen, or the nozzle. Our tank-to-burner guide on a [furnace not getting oil](/blog/furnace-not-getting-oil/) traces that path in order.

## Filter Changes Buy Time. They Don't Fix the Tank

Swapping a plugged cartridge clears the immediate blockage, and that is genuinely useful when the furnace needs to run tonight. What it does not do is change whatever is shedding into the fuel. If the tank is producing water, sludge, or rust, it keeps producing it after the new filter goes in, and the next clog is already on its way.

That is the trap with a fast-clogging filter: each individual fix is small, so it is easy to keep paying for cartridge after cartridge on a tank that is telling you, clearly, that it is the actual problem. At some point the honest comparison is not filter versus filter. It is filter versus tank.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I change my oil tank filter?

At least once a year for most home systems. General Filters' instructions for its 1A-25 canister filter call for a new cartridge annually, and more often on some two-pipe systems. The yearly service Granby Industries recommends for its tanks is the natural time to do it. If yours clogs more than once between services, look at the tank rather than wait for the next scheduled change.

### Why does my oil filter housing leak after I change it?

Almost always a fitting issue rather than a tank problem: a gasket that did not seat evenly, a centre bolt tightened too hard, or a bleed screw left slightly loose after bleeding the air out. Check those three points first. A weep confined to the housing itself, rather than spreading along the tank's seam or bottom, points to the fitting, not the tank.

### Can an additive stop my filter from clogging?

Additives can slow new sludge from forming and help a tank tolerate light contamination, but they do not remove sludge that has already settled and packed a filter. In a heavily fouled tank, a strong dispersant can loosen a large amount at once and clog the very next filter. Our sludge guide, linked above, covers what additives can and cannot do.

### Does a fast-clogging filter mean my tank is leaking?

Not on its own. A clogging filter means the tank is shedding water, sludge, or rust into the fuel, which is a sign of an ageing or contaminated tank, but it is a different symptom from an active leak. A leak shows up as a wet spot or an oily weep on the tank itself, and the wet-spot guide linked above explains how to tell the two apart.

### Is a clogging filter covered by my home insurance?

Filter changes are routine maintenance and would not be an insurance matter on their own. What can matter to an insurer is the underlying cause, since a tank that is corroding internally or holding water is exactly the kind of tank condition insurers ask about at renewal. If repeated clogging traces back to the tank's age or condition, that is worth mentioning the next time your policy comes up for review.
