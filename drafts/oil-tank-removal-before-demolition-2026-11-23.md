# Oil Tank Removal Before Demolition: What Has to Happen Before the House Comes Down

If the house you are tearing down heats with oil, the tank is the first job, not the last. It should be pumped out, disconnected from its lines and taken away before demolition starts, and the lot should be checked for a buried tank before an excavator gets near the foundation. A demolition crew that finds a tank halfway through the job, or knocks a wall onto a full one, turns a routine teardown into a spill.

None of the Moncton, Dieppe or Riverview permit pages we read makes tank removal a formal condition of a demolition permit, so this guide doesn't claim one. What it covers is the order of work that keeps a demolition clean, what the permit offices actually say, how to check for a forgotten buried tank, and what each step costs. ## Why the tank comes out before the walls do

A heating oil tank in a basement can hold many hundreds of litres of oil, and its fill pipe, vent and oil line all run through the structure you are about to take apart. Pulling a house down around it risks three problems:

- **Oil in the debris.** A crushed or split tank spills into the basement, the foundation fill and the demolition waste, and every load that leaves the site is then contaminated.
- **Fire and vapour.** An empty tank still holds vapour, and cutting or crushing one isn't something to do with an excavator. Our guide to [basement oil tank removal](/blog/basement-oil-tank-removal/) explains why the tank is cleared and vented before anyone opens it.
- **A bigger cleanup bill.** Oil that reaches soil has to be dealt with. Our price table puts contaminated soil remediation at $10,000 and up, and that is before you count the delay.

There is an example of how a municipality writes this down. The City of Saint John's 2020 tender for demolishing a house on Broad Street states that any oil tank on site must be removed and disposed of as required by the Department of Environment. It also says that if contamination of the building materials or site is noted during demolition, work stops and the Department of Environment is contacted, and the work doesn't resume until it approves. You can read it in the [City of Saint John demolition tender (PDF)](https://saintjohn.ca/sites/default/files/2020-11/2020-083001T%20Bid%20Document_0.pdf). That is one city's terms for one project, not a rule that applies to your lot, but it shows the sequence demolition contractors work to.

None of them names a tank. That doesn't mean nobody asks. A demolition contractor, an inspector or your insurer may want proof that the tank is out. Ask the permit office what it wants to see, and ask your demolition contractor whether the tank is in their scope or yours. ## The order of work

1. **Find everything first.** Walk the property and the basement. Note the tank, its label, the fill and vent pipes outside, the oil line and filter, and any second tank. Write down what you find, with photos.
2. **Stop deliveries.** Tell whoever fills the tank that the house is coming down, so no one delivers into a tank that is about to be removed. Do this early, because an automatic fill can happen while the work is being scheduled.
3. **Pump out the oil.** The oil left in the tank has to be pumped out by whoever removes it. Don't pour it on the ground or into a drain. Our guide to [leftover heating oil](/blog/leftover-heating-oil/) covers what happens to it.
4. **Disconnect the lines.** The oil line is drained and capped, and the fill and vent pipes outside the wall are removed or sealed. A fill pipe left standing after the house is gone is an invitation to a delivery into nothing.
5. **Take the tank out.** An empty tank comes out whole if it can, or is opened and cut up in place by someone who clears the vapour first. This isn't a job for the demolition crew with a torch, or for the owner.
6. **Keep the paperwork.** Get a receipt that shows the tank was removed and disposed of, with the date and the address. Give a copy to your demolition contractor and keep one for your insurer and your lawyer.
7. **Then demolish.** With the tank, lines and pipes gone, the rest of the job is ordinary demolition.

## Check for a forgotten buried tank

An older house may have started out with a buried tank that was later replaced by an indoor one, and the buried one wasn't always dug up. The usual signs are a capped pipe sticking out of the ground near the foundation, a vent pipe with no tank behind it, or a patch of ground that doesn't match the rest. Our guide to [how to find a buried oil tank](/blog/find-buried-oil-tank/) walks through the signs and the searching methods.

Do this before demolition, not during it. If an excavator finds a tank mid-job, the work has to stop. Saint John's tender is the example here, since it calls for work to stop when contamination turns up. If you see oil in the soil or smell it at any point, stop digging and report it: your regional Department of Environment and Local Government office during business hours, or the environmental emergency line at 1-800-565-1633, which the Government of Canada's [environmental emergency contacts page](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program/report-emergency.html) lists as a 24-hour number for New Brunswick. Call 911 first if there is any risk of fire or explosion. Then call us. ## Gutting is different from demolishing

If you are stripping a house down to the studs rather than knocking it down, the tank may not have to go. It still has to be protected, and the same questions about its age, the oil line and the pipes apply. If the tank is old, a gut renovation is the cheapest time to replace or remove it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to remove the oil tank before demolishing a house?

The Moncton, Dieppe and Riverview permit pages we read don't say so. In practice the tank has to be dealt with, because demolishing over a tank risks a spill. The City of Saint John's own demolition tender requires any oil tank on site to be removed and disposed of as the Department of Environment requires. Ask your permit office what it needs.

### Who is responsible for the tank, the owner or the demolition contractor?

That depends on your contract, so settle it in writing before work begins. Some demolition contracts include the tank, and some leave it to the owner. Either way, the tank should be pumped out and removed by someone set up to do it, and you should keep the disposal receipt.

### What if there is oil left in the tank?

It has to be pumped out before the tank is removed. Don't drain it onto the ground or down a drain. Whoever removes the tank handles the oil, and our leftover heating oil guide explains your options.

### What if we find a buried tank during demolition?

Stop work around it. Don't keep digging. If there is oil in the soil or a smell, report it to the regional Department of Environment and Local Government office, or to 1-800-565-1633 after hours. Then have the tank assessed and removed before the excavation continues.

### Can I just fill the tank with sand and leave it?

Not before demolition. A tank left under a new build or a levelled lot is a problem for the next owner. Our guide on whether you can abandon an oil tank in place covers when closure in place applies and when it doesn't. For a house being torn down, plan on removal.
