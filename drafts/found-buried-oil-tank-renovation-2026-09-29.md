# Your Excavator Just Hit a Buried Oil Tank. Here's What to Do Next

An excavator bucket, a fence post auger, or a landscaping crew's shovel just hit something that clanged instead of crunched, and now there's a curved steel wall sitting half exposed in a hole that was never supposed to have one in it. Stop work right there. Don't try to lift it, drag it the rest of the way out, or pry it open to see what's inside.

Most of the time, what you've found in a Southeast New Brunswick yard is an old buried heating oil tank, a leftover from the decades when burying the tank was the tidy way to install one. Finding it doesn't automatically mean disaster. It does mean the project pauses until someone checks it for signs of a leak and tells you what comes next.

Here's how to handle the next hour, the next few days, and the decision after that.

## Stop, Then Look. Don't Dig Any Further

The first move costs nothing and matters the most: stop the machine, back everyone away from the hole, and resist the urge to poke at the tank with a shovel or a pry bar to see what you're dealing with.

A few reasons that's worth the discipline:

- **An old tank isn't necessarily empty.** Even one that's been out of service for decades can still hold litres of sludge, water, and residual oil sitting in the bottom seam.
- **Rusted steel can be thinner than it looks.** A bucket that already dented it once can punch through it on the next pass, turning a dry find into an active spill.
- **Vapour is a real hazard.** A tank that held furnace oil can hold flammable vapour long after the liquid is gone, so no smoking and no sparking tools anywhere near the hole.

Keep kids, pets, and anyone not directly involved well back, and take a few photos from a safe distance for whoever you call next. That's the extent of what you should do yourself.

## What You've Probably Found

If it's curved steel, roughly the size of a large chest freezer or bigger, sitting a metre or two down, it's very likely an old residential heating oil tank. Burying the tank was a common way to install home heating oil for much of the last century, because it freed up basement space and kept the yard tidy. When a house switched to an above-ground tank, electric heat, or later a heat pump, the buried one often just stayed put.

That's why these turn up in places nobody expected: under a garden bed added decades later, along a fence line where a shed used to stand, or in the corner of a lot at a cottage property that changed hands more than once. A capped fill pipe or vent stub is sometimes still attached, even if you never noticed it above ground before the digging started.

This article covers what to do the moment you've already found one by surprise. If you're planning ahead of a dig and want to check first, our guide on [how to find a buried oil tank](/blog/find-buried-oil-tank/) covers the warning signs to look for before you ever break ground.

## Three Things to Check From a Safe Distance

Once the equipment is off and everyone's clear, there are three things worth noting without getting any closer than you already are.

1. **A fill pipe or vent stub.** Even a broken-off stub of steel pipe near the tank is a strong sign this is a fuel tank rather than scrap or a septic fitting.
2. **A petroleum or diesel-like smell.** Take a moment near the hole, not right over it, and see if there's an odour that wasn't there before you started digging.
3. **Sheen or stained, wet soil.** Look for an oily film on any water pooling in the hole, or soil that's darker and wetter than the ground around it.

Any one of those, especially a smell or a sheen, changes what happens next. It's the difference between a straightforward removal and a job that starts with soil samples before anyone commits to a plan. If you see none of those signs and the tank is just dry rusted steel, that's a good sign, but it still isn't a green light to keep digging around it yourself.

## Does a Buried Oil Tank Have to Be Reported in New Brunswick?

New Brunswick's duty to report is about contamination, not about owning an old tank. The province's page on [reporting a contaminant release](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/corporate/promo/contaminated-sites-program/reporting.html) says the Department of Environment and Local Government must be told when someone knows or has reason to believe a contaminant has been released into, or is present in, the environment, and that a known or suspected release is reported by phone right away. A dry, intact tank with no sign of leaking is a different situation from stained soil, but if anything makes you suspect oil got out, report it. We cover the reporting rules in detail in [do you have to report an oil spill in New Brunswick](/blog/report-oil-spill-nb/).

A smell, a sheen, or stained soil around the tank you've hit counts. Treat it as a spill. If there's any fire or explosion risk, call 911 first. Otherwise, the province's reporting page says to call the nearest department regional office during business hours, or the environmental emergencies line at 1-800-565-1633 after hours. [Environment and Climate Change Canada's emergency contacts page](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program/report-emergency.html) lists 1-800-565-1633 as the 24-hour number for New Brunswick.

If your project is under a building permit, a quick call to your municipality's building department is worth it too. They may want to know a tank was found before you pour a slab, footing, or patio over that part of the yard.

## What Happens After the Call: Pump-Out, Removal, and Soil Samples

Once someone qualified is on site, the process generally follows the same order regardless of how the tank was found:

1. **Fully expose the tank** so it can be inspected and opened safely.
2. **Pump out any liquid or sludge** left inside, and dispose of it properly.
3. **Decide removal or abandonment.** A tank that's accessible usually comes out whole or in sections. One trapped under a slab, addition, or footing that's already built may be a candidate for abandonment in place instead, where it's cleaned, tested, and filled with an inert material rather than excavated. Our post on [abandoning an oil tank in place](/blog/abandon-oil-tank-in-place/) walks through when that option genuinely makes sense.
4. **Take soil samples** around and beneath where the tank sat, whether it's removed or closed in place.
5. **Backfill and document.** Clean fill goes back in once samples pass, and you get paperwork confirming the tank is dealt with, which matters the day you sell or refinance.

## If This Happened Mid-Renovation, Build the Pause Into the Schedule

A buried tank showing up partway through a reno is frustrating, but the timing works in your favour compared to finding out later. Get the assessment scheduled before you pour anything permanent over that spot. Deciding removal or abandonment is a lot cheaper before a garage floor or an addition footing goes down than after it's cured.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to report finding a buried oil tank in New Brunswick?

Not for simply finding one sitting there dry and intact. The province's reporting duty is about oil that has been released into, or is present in, the environment, known or suspected. If you notice a smell, a sheen, or stained soil around the tank, report it: the nearest Environment and Local Government regional office during business hours, or 1-800-565-1633 after hours. When in doubt, make the call.

### Is it safe to keep digging once I've hit a buried tank?

No. Stop the equipment and back away. A tank that held heating oil can still hold vapour and sludge even after decades of sitting unused, and striking, dragging, or prying it open with a bucket or bar risks a puncture. Let someone qualified assess it before any more digging happens near that spot.

### How do I know if the tank I found still has oil in it?

You can't tell from the outside. A tank that looks bone dry can still have litres of sludge sitting in the bottom seam, and one that looks fine on the surface can be thin as foil in spots you can't see. It needs to be pumped out and opened by someone equipped to do that safely before anyone can say what shape it's really in.

### What does it cost to deal with a tank found during a renovation?

A straightforward removal with no sign of a leak typically runs $2,000 to $4,000 in this area, and can climb toward $10,000 depending on depth, access, and how high the water table sits. If soil samples come back contaminated, remediation adds $10,000 or more on top of that. Soil samples decide which end of that range you land on.

### Can I just leave the tank where it is and build over it?

Sometimes, but only if it's properly pumped, cleaned, tested clean, and filled with an inert material, a process called abandonment in place. It's usually reserved for tanks sitting under something too costly to disturb, like an existing slab or footing. That's a different thing entirely from backfilling over a tank nobody has touched, which just buries the same problem under a new slab. Your insurer and any future buyer will want paperwork showing it was done properly.
