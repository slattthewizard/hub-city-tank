# Bottom Outlet vs End Outlet Oil Tank: Why the Fitting Position Matters

Every residential oil tank sends fuel to the furnace through an outlet fitting, and on most tanks that outlet sits in one of three spots: on the tank floor, on the end wall near the bottom, or on top with a tube reaching down inside. That single design choice, made at the factory, changes where water and sludge settle inside the tank and how the corrosion pattern develops over the years.

It sounds like a small detail. It isn't. In Newfoundland and Labrador, outlet type is written directly into the regulation that sets how many years an older single-wall steel tank is allowed to stay in service. ## What "Outlet Type" Means on an Oil Tank

A residential tank has two connections that matter for this comparison, and they do opposite jobs. The fill pipe and vent, up on top, are where oil goes in. The outlet is where it comes back out, headed down the line toward the filter, the shut-off valve and eventually the burner. Our guide to [oil tank parts](/blog/oil-tank-parts-explained/) covers the fill, vent, gauge and valve in more depth; this article is about that one remaining fitting, the outlet.

On most steel tanks sold in Canada, that outlet sits in one of three places:

- **Bottom outlet.** The fitting is set into the underside of the tank, at or near the lowest point of the tank floor.
- **End outlet.** The fitting is set into the vertical end plate, low down, near where the end meets the curved body.
- **Top outlet.** The fitting sits on top of the tank, connected to a dip tube that runs down inside to draw oil from near the floor.

Newfoundland's regulation groups end outlet and top outlet together and treats bottom outlet as its own category, which tells you a lot about which two layouts behave more like each other.

## How Outlet Position Changes Where Water and Sludge Sit

Water is heavier than heating oil, so it sinks to the lowest point inside the tank no matter where the outlet is. Granby's [installation guidelines for its Canadian steel tanks](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0007_Na-ULC-S602-INSTALLATION-AND-MAINTENANCE-GUIDELINES.pdf) put it bluntly: most premature failures of steel oil tanks are caused by water and sludge that build up at the bottom of the tank. What the outlet position changes is whether that low point has a way out.

On a bottom outlet tank, the outlet is the low point, as long as the tank is installed with a tilt toward it. Granby's guidelines call for a minimum slope of 10 mm per 500 mm toward the fuel outlet, set with the leg lengths, and ask that the slope be rechecked during maintenance. Water that settles there sits right at the fitting, where small amounts are drawn into the supply line and caught at the filter, and where a technician can drain it. For a bottom outlet tank, Granby allows the water to be drawn off through a drain valve, at least 1 inch, fitted in the outlet.

An end outlet or top outlet tank works differently. The oil is drawn from a point that isn't the very bottom, so water and sludge can settle below the draw point and stay there. The oil still comes out fine day to day, but checking and clearing water becomes a separate step. Granby's guidelines say a tank set up to draw from the top must have a dedicated opening on top used only for checking and removing water. Our post on [water in your oil tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/) covers how that condensation builds up in the first place and what a pump-out involves, regardless of outlet type.

That's why outlet position matters for corrosion. A steel tank rusts from the inside where water sits longest. A bottom outlet tank that's pitched correctly gives that water a way out through the line and the drain. A tank that draws from above the bottom leaves it sitting on the steel floor until someone removes it. Neither design is immune: a bottom outlet tank installed dead level, or never checked for water, can rust through along the bottom just the same.

## Newfoundland Sets Tank Age Limits by Outlet Type

This is the part that surprises most homeowners who dig into oil tank rules online: outlet type isn't just a design footnote, it's law in at least one Atlantic province.

Newfoundland and Labrador's [Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations, NLR 60/03](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/Legislation/sr/Regulations/rc030060.htm), section 15, set a maximum service life for aboveground single-wall mild steel tanks. The outside category covers tanks registered before January 1, 2011, and outlet type is one of the variables, alongside steel gauge and whether the tank sits outside or inside a building that protects it from the elements. This table is Newfoundland and Labrador law only:

| Location (NL rule) | Outlet type | Steel gauge | Maximum service life in NL |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | End or top outlet | 14 gauge | 10 years |
| Outside | End or top outlet | 12 gauge | 15 years |
| Outside | Bottom outlet | 14 gauge | 15 years |
| Outside | Bottom outlet | 12 gauge | 20 years |
| Inside a structure | End or top outlet | 14 gauge | 15 years |
| Inside a structure | End or top outlet | 12 gauge | 20 years |
| Inside a structure | Bottom outlet | 14 gauge | 20 years |
| Inside a structure | Bottom outlet | 12 gauge | 25 years |

In every pairing, the bottom outlet version gets five more years before it has to come out. Tanks with a monitored secondary containment, and tanks made of materials other than mild steel, aren't on this table: their service life is set by the provincial administrator, capped at 50 years.

That's Newfoundland law, not New Brunswick's. Prince Edward Island also ties tank expiry to outlet type in its own way: its [home heat tanks FAQ](https://www.princeedwardisland.ca/sites/default/files/publications/home_heat_tanks_-_frequently_asked_questions.pdf) says five years come off the expiry date when a single-wall metal tank is changed from a bottom outlet to a top feed, and that bottom outlet tanks there must be pitched toward the outlet at no less than 1 in 50. We haven't found a New Brunswick rule that sets a replacement age by outlet type. What the Newfoundland table does show, using numbers a regulator was willing to put in law, is that outlet type is treated as a real factor in how long a single-wall steel tank holds up, not a minor spec.

## How to Tell Which Outlet Type Your Tank Has

Start with the manufacturer's nameplate. It's usually on the top or upper end of the tank and often names the model, which tells you the outlet layout if you can match it to the maker's spec sheet.

After that, look at where the line leaves the tank. A bottom outlet tank has the fitting underneath, close to the legs or stand, with the line dropping straight down before turning toward the furnace. An end outlet tank has the fitting low on the vertical end plate, near the bottom of the tank, with the line running out sideways. A top outlet tank has no visible fitting down low at all; the connection is on top, next to the fill and vent, and the actual draw point is a tube you can't see once the tank's in place.

Brand matters here too. Granby's Canadian [EcoGard double-bottom tank](https://www.granbyindustries.com/products/ecogard-double-bottom-oil-tank/) is listed with a 1/2 inch or 2 inch bottom outlet and a visual monitoring system for the primary tank, so a failure of the inner bottom shows up before oil reaches the floor. The same page lists top draw ready models, so even within one product line the outlet setup can differ. Roth's double-wall tanks take a different approach, with a polyethylene inner tank, which is one of the points we cover in our [Granby vs Roth comparison](/blog/granby-vs-roth-oil-tanks/).

## Where Outlet Type Comes Up With Insurers and Inspectors

Outlet type isn't something most homeowners think about until someone else asks. The insurance industry's national [Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://www.csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire), published by CSIO, asks for the fuel supply line outlet location on the tank, alongside questions about fill and vent pipe material, the filter, the supply line and impact protection. If your insurer sends you that form, or a home inspector asks, this is the detail they mean.

None of that means an end outlet or top outlet tank automatically fails an inspection. It means the tank's overall condition gets weighed against a design that made water harder to reach, so an inspector or insurer may look a little closer at the seams and the floor before signing off.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the actual difference between a bottom outlet and an end outlet oil tank?

The difference is where the fitting sits that carries oil out to the furnace. A bottom outlet tank has that fitting on the tank floor, at the lowest point, while an end outlet tank has it on the vertical end plate, low down but away from the true bottom. A top outlet tank puts the fitting on top with a tube reaching down inside. All three deliver oil the same way day to day; they differ in how close the outlet sits to where water and sludge settle.

### Does outlet type really affect how long an oil tank lasts?

It's one factor among several, but it's real enough that Newfoundland and Labrador writes it into provincial regulation. Under Newfoundland's NLR 60/03, a single-wall bottom outlet steel tank is allowed five more years of service than an end or top outlet tank of the same gauge and location. Steel gauge, indoor versus outdoor placement, and how well the tank has been maintained still matter at least as much.

### Is there a rule like Newfoundland's for oil tanks in New Brunswick?

We haven't found one. Newfoundland and Labrador's NLR 60/03 sets service lives by outlet type, and PEI adjusts expiry dates for outlet changes, but we have not seen a New Brunswick rule that sets a tank's replacement age by its outlet. For most NB homeowners, the deadline that matters sooner is the age limit in their own insurance policy, so check that first.

### How can I tell which outlet type my own tank has?

Check the manufacturer's nameplate first, then look at where the oil line physically leaves the tank. A fitting underneath, near the legs, means bottom outlet. A fitting low on the vertical end wall means end outlet. If you can't find a fitting down low at all and the connection is up on top next to the fill and vent, it's a top outlet design with an internal dip tube.

### If I have an older end outlet tank, does it need to come out right away?

Not necessarily. Outlet type is one piece of the picture, not a verdict on its own. A tank's actual condition, its age against the typical ranges in our [oil tank lifespan guide](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/), visible rust or staining, and any insurance deadline matter more than outlet type by itself. If your tank is getting up there in years and happens to be an end or top outlet design, that's a reason to get it looked at sooner rather than later, not a reason to panic today.
