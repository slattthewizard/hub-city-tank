## What Is a Double Wall Oil Tank, Exactly?

Picture a tank inside a tank. The inner tank holds your heating oil, and on most modern designs it's seamless high density polyethylene, a thick plastic that can't rust because no steel ever touches the oil. Around that inner tank sits an outer shell of galvanized steel.

That outer shell is the whole point. If the inner tank ever fails, the oil stays put inside the steel jacket instead of spreading across your basement floor or soaking into the ground beside your foundation. Most double wall designs, including the Roth tanks we install every week, build the outer shell to hold at least 110 percent of what the inner tank carries. Many also include a leak indicator between the two walls, so a failure shows up as a warranty call instead of an environmental file.

Now compare that to what most Moncton homes still run: a single layer of welded steel, usually 910 litres, standing on legs in the basement or against the side of the house. It works fine right up until corrosion eats through, and steel tanks almost always rust from the inside out. By the time you spot rust on the outside, the wall can already be paper thin. There's no backup layer, no margin, nothing between the oil and your floor.

## Double Wall vs Single Wall Oil Tanks: What's Actually Different

On paper the difference is one extra layer. In practice, the two tanks fail in completely different ways, and your insurance company knows it.

When a single wall tank lets go, everything it holds is on the loose. Even a slow weep at a bottom seam can put litres of oil into concrete over a winter. When a double wall tank's inner shell fails, the oil sits in the outer jacket and waits for someone to deal with it. One failure is a mess measured in tens of thousands of dollars. The other is a phone call.

Lifespan is different too. A single wall steel tank in New Brunswick typically gives you 15 to 25 years, less if it lives outside in salt air or sits on damp ground. A double wall tank's poly inner shell doesn't corrode at all, which is why Roth advertises a 30-year limited warranty on its residential tanks along with leak protection coverage. Confirm the current terms when you buy, but the gap in expected life is real.

## Who Actually Needs a Double Wall Tank in Greater Moncton

Not everyone. But if any of these sound like your house, the extra cost starts looking cheap.

**Your insurer is asking questions.** More and more companies age-limit steel tanks at 15, 20, or 25 years, and some now ask directly whether a tank has secondary containment. A few jurisdictions have gone further than insurers. Massachusetts, for example, passed a [homeowner oil heating law](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/homeowner-oil-heating-system-upgrade-and-insurance-law) that requires leak protection on many home systems. New Brunswick hasn't done that for residential tanks, but insurers here are quietly heading the same direction on their own.

**Your tank sits over finished space or near a drain.** A basement tank beside a sump pit or floor drain has a direct path to your weeping tile and the groundwater under your house. Oil that reaches a sump doesn't stay your problem alone, it becomes the neighbourhood's.

**You're on a well or near water.** Plenty of homes in Salisbury, Memramcook, and rural Westmorland and Kent counties draw drinking water from private wells. A leak that touches a well can mean drilling a new one on top of the cleanup. Cottages and year-round homes near the shore in Shediac and Pointe-du-Chêne carry the same risk with the water table sitting that close.

## Double Bottom, Double Wall, and Other Labels That Get Mixed Up

Tank shopping comes with some confusing vocabulary, and the difference matters when your insurer asks what you own.

A **double bottom** tank, like Granby's popular 2-in-1 line, is a steel tank with a second layer of steel under the bottom, where most corrosion leaks start. It catches a bottom failure and usually includes a leak indicator. It does not protect against a seam split, a wall perforation, or an overfill, and some insurers treat it as a single wall tank for underwriting. It's a good middle option, not full containment.

A **true double wall** tank wraps the entire inner tank, bottom, walls, and all, in a containment shell. That's the Roth design and Granby's comparable models. When someone says secondary containment, this is what they mean.

**Fibreglass** tanks solve the rust problem a different way, with a single wall that can't corrode. They're a legitimate option with their own trade-offs on price and fittings.

## When a Single Wall Tank Is Still a Fine Choice

We'll be straight with you: we sell plenty of single wall tanks, and for some homes they're the right call.

If your tank lives outdoors on a solid pad, away from wells, drains, and waterways, and your insurer has no containment requirement, a new single wall steel tank gives you two decades of service for the lowest upfront cost. If you're planning to convert to a heat pump within a few years and just need to bridge the gap, spending the minimum on a code-compliant tank can be the smarter play.

The honest rule of thumb: the more expensive a leak would be at your specific address, the stronger the case for the second wall. A tank in a dirt-floor crawlspace next to a sump pit is not the place to save $1,200. A tank on a concrete pad thirty metres from anything sensitive might be.

## How to Tell What You Have, and When to Call

Start with the label. Every tank carries a manufacturer's plate or sticker with a model and serial number, usually near the top or on an end. A Roth double wall is easy to spot: rectangular, grey galvanized steel outside, taller than it is wide. A traditional steel tank is the rounded oval shape most of us grew up with.

While you're down there, look at the bottom seams and legs with a flashlight and run a paper towel under the tank. Any wet sheen, blistering paint, or oil smell means stop and call a professional today, not next week. Don't try to drain, patch, or move a tank yourself. A full 910 litre tank weighs close to a tonne, the fittings are old, and one wrong move turns a warning sign into a spill.

And if oil has already reached soil, a drain, or water, New Brunswick requires the spill to be reported. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does a double wall oil tank last?

The polyethylene inner tank doesn't rust, so 25 to 30 years of service is a realistic expectation, and Roth backs its residential tanks with a 30-year limited warranty. The galvanized outer shell is protective, not oil-wetted, so it ages slowly. Keep the tank on a stable base and have the oil line and filter checked at each service and it'll likely outlast your furnace.

### Will my insurance company make me switch to a double wall tank?

Most New Brunswick insurers don't require double wall tanks yet, but many age-limit steel tanks and a growing number ask about secondary containment on renewal forms. If your current tank is past 20 years, expect a letter sooner or later. Replacing with a double wall usually ends the conversation permanently, because the tank's warranty outlasts any age limit they'd apply.

### Can a double wall oil tank sit outside through a New Brunswick winter?

Yes, as long as it's an outdoor rated model on a proper pad. The galvanized shell handles snow and rain, and the poly inner tank isn't bothered by cold. Outdoor tanks in our climate still need a sheltered spot away from roof avalanche zones, a sturdy stand, and a yearly look at the fill and vent fittings before heating season.

### Is a double wall tank worth it if I might switch to a heat pump soon?

If your conversion is one or two years out, a double wall tank is probably more tank than you need, and we'll say so at the estimate. If the timeline is fuzzy or five years away, remember that an aging single wall tank can force the decision for you in the middle of February. Price both options, check what your insurer will accept in the meantime, and pick the one that keeps you covered until the ductless units go in.
