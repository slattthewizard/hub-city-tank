# Soil Remediation in New Brunswick: What Happens After Oil Gets Into the Ground

Soil remediation is the assessment, excavation, testing, and disposal work that happens once heating oil has reached the ground around a home, not just the tank or the floor above it. ## What Soil Remediation Means

Cleaning up a spill and remediating soil are two different scopes of work, and the difference matters for your budget.

A contained spill on a concrete floor or a driveway gets absorbed, scraped up, and hauled away. Nasty, but the ground itself was never touched. Soil remediation starts once oil has soaked past a hard surface and into dirt, whether that's a lawn, a garden bed, or the fill under a basement floor. Once oil is in the soil, the job stops being cleanup and becomes an environmental file: someone has to define how far the contamination travelled, dig it out or treat it, prove with lab results that what is left meets the province's criteria, and document all of it.

The testing, the reporting, and getting the file closed cleanly are what drag a soil job out and push the price up.

Two situations lead to a lot of residential soil work: a buried steel tank that has been leaking quietly for years before anyone notices, and an outdoor above-ground tank that weeps into the ground beside the foundation over a winter nobody was watching closely. ## The Remediation Process, Step by Step

Most residential files follow roughly the same sequence, whether the oil came from a buried tank, an outdoor tank, or an indoor one that leaked through a dirt floor.

| Step | What happens | Who is typically involved |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Discovery | A leak, sheen, smell, or stained soil is found, often during a tank removal, a renovation, or a real estate deal | Homeowner, contractor, or home inspector |
| 2. Notification | A fresh spill is reported by phone to the provincial regional office (or the after-hours line); contamination found later, such as during a tank pull, is reported on a historical contamination form | Homeowner, contractor, or consultant |
| 3. Site assessment | Soil (and sometimes groundwater) samples are taken to map how far the oil travelled | A site professional (environmental consultant) |
| 4. Excavation and disposal | Contaminated soil is dug out and trucked to an approved facility; clean backfill goes in | Excavation contractor, working from the assessment |
| 5. Confirmation sampling | New samples confirm what remains meets the provincial criteria | The same site professional |
| 6. File closure | A closure report and a Record of Site Condition go to the province | Site professional, on the owner's behalf, and the Department of Environment and Local Government |

Step three is where most of the surprises live. A consultant can't know how far 300 litres travelled through sandy soil near Shediac or heavier soil further inland without test holes and lab results, and the first round of results can mean sampling a few more spots. Every extra sample point adds cost, but skipping it just means the contamination gets found later, often by a buyer's inspector, which is a far worse time to find it.

Excavation itself is often the fastest part. A small excavator can move a lot of soil in a day. What takes the time, and the money, is proving the hole is clean before it gets backfilled.

## New Brunswick's Rules for Contaminated Sites

New Brunswick regulates this under the [Contaminated Sites Regulation](https://www.canlii.org/en/nb/laws/regu/nb-reg-2024-57/latest/nb-reg-2024-57.html) (NB Reg 2024-57), made under the Clean Environment Act, which came into force on August 1, 2024. The province's [contaminated sites program page](https://www.gnb.ca/en/topic/environment-resources/waste-management/contaminated-sites.html) says the program covers everything from the release of a contaminant and notifying the department through site assessment, remediation, monitoring, and closure, and that it's laid out in the regulation plus six ministerial protocols.

A few pieces of that framework matter to a homeowner:

**A site professional runs the file.** The province's site assessment protocol says the responsible party must engage a site professional to plan, carry out, and oversee the assessment. That's a person whose qualifications meet the requirements in the Clean Environment Act and the regulation. They design the sampling, compare the lab results to the provincial criteria (New Brunswick uses the Atlantic Risk-Based Corrective Action, or Atlantic RBCA, approach), and at the end they submit the closure report and Record of Site Condition on the owner's behalf. A homeowner doesn't sign off on their own soil.

**Some small spills take a shorter route.** The program page describes a Limited Impact Release: a release can qualify if it's current rather than historical, involves petroleum hydrocarbons, affects only soil, has no groundwater or ecological impacts, and can be cleaned up within 30 days. A department inspector screens each reported release to decide whether it can be handled that way or has to go through the full contaminated sites process. A fresh, contained spill reported promptly has a better chance of staying small. A leak that sat for years and reached groundwater does not.

**Historical contamination has its own deadline.** Contamination found during a tank pull or an excavation, where nobody knows when the oil got there, is reported with a Notification of Historical Contamination form. The province's notification protocol gives 30 days from becoming aware of it, meaning from the confirming lab results, and says any concentration above the lab's detection limit must be reported.

**Closed doesn't always mean closed forever.** The file closure protocol sets out the criteria that could lead to a file being reopened, for example if new information turns up. That's one more reason a rushed or corner-cut remediation can cost you twice.

**Files are on the public record.** When a site is designated as contaminated, the province's program overview shows a Land Gazette notice being added to the property's PID, and the program page links a GeoNB map layer showing open and closed contaminated site files across the province.

None of this is unique to oil tanks. The same regulation covers any contaminant. But home heating oil tanks are a big share of the workload: the province's [guidelines on oil tank maintenance and spill prevention](https://www.gnb.ca/en/topic/environment-resources/waste-management/oil-tank-maintenance.html) say nearly 40% of the oil spills reported to the department each year come from domestic oil tanks.

## Reporting: Business Hours Versus the 24-Hour Line

For a fresh spill or leak, anyone who knows or has reason to believe a contaminant has been released must notify the Department of Environment and Local Government, and the province wants that call made right away, by phone.

During business hours (8:15 a.m. to 4:30 p.m., Monday to Friday), the report goes to the regional office closest to the spill. For Greater Moncton that's 506-856-2374. Outside those hours, it goes to the National Environmental Emergencies Centre at 1-800-565-1633, a 24-hour line that the federal [environmental emergency contacts page](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program/report-emergency.html) lists for New Brunswick under the Canadian Coast Guard's Maritimes Regional Office. If there's a fire or explosion risk, 911 comes first.

Reporting early does two things. It gets a proper assessment started before the contamination has time to travel further, which can be the difference between a short cleanup and a full file. And it gives you a documented timeline if an insurance claim ends up depending on when the spill was found and how quickly you acted. ## What Soil Remediation Costs

There's no fixed price for soil remediation because the scope varies so much from one property to the next. Our [oil tank replacement cost](/oil-tank-replacement-cost/) page lists contaminated soil remediation at $10,000 and up, and that's a starting point, not an average: once excavation, disposal, lab work, and a site professional's reports are added up, even a modest dig adds up quickly.

What pushes a file well past that starting point is depth, distance travelled, and what sits nearby. A tank that leaked for one winter into a small patch of sandy soil is a very different bill from a buried tank that seeped for a decade toward a foundation, a ditch, or a well. Groundwater makes it bigger again, because it usually means monitoring wells and more rounds of sampling. Access matters too. Digging in an open side yard is nothing like digging under a deck or beside a foundation wall.

The number that matters is the one that comes out of your own site assessment, not a range from a blog post.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does soil remediation involve?

It means assessing how far spilled heating oil has travelled through the soil, excavating and disposing of the contaminated material at an approved facility, backfilling with clean soil, and confirming with lab samples that what remains meets New Brunswick's criteria. A site professional oversees the assessment and submits the closure report and Record of Site Condition to the province on the owner's behalf.

### How much does soil remediation cost in New Brunswick?

Our cost page lists contaminated soil remediation at $10,000 and up. Treat that as a starting point: excavation, disposal, lab testing, and a site professional's reports all add up, and depth, how far the oil travelled, groundwater, and access to the site push the number higher from there. There's no flat rate, and the real figure comes from your own site assessment.

### Do I have to report contaminated soil to the province?

Yes. A fresh spill is reported by phone to the regional office during business hours, or to the 24-hour line at 1-800-565-1633 after hours. Contamination found later, for example when an old tank is pulled, is reported on a Notification of Historical Contamination form within 30 days of the confirming lab results. Your site professional usually handles that paperwork.

### Can I sell my house if a soil remediation file is still open?

You can, but it gets harder. Designated sites carry a notice on the property's record and appear on the province's contaminated sites map, so buyers, their lawyers, and their lenders will likely find it. Most will want to see a closed file with lab results, not an open one with the outcome unknown. Closing the file before you list is usually easier than negotiating around it.

### Does home insurance pay for soil remediation?

Sometimes, if the release was sudden and accidental, your policy covers oil escape, and your tank met the insurer's conditions. Slow leaks and tanks past an insurer's age limit are often where claims run into trouble. A denied claim doesn't remove the requirement to deal with the contamination.
