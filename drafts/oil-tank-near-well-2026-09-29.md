# Oil Tank Near a Well: What Rural Southeast NB Homeowners Should Know

If your home runs on a private well, an oil tank sitting close to the wellhead is a bigger deal than it looks. A tank leak on municipal water is a cleanup problem. A tank leak near a well can also be a drinking water problem, and it can reach the water before anyone notices a smell in the basement.

This matters most outside the Moncton core, in the rural parts of Salisbury, Memramcook, and the farmhouses and older bungalows scattered through Westmorland, Albert, and Kent counties, where the house heats with oil and the water comes from a drilled or dug well on the same lot.

## Why a Tank Near the Wellhead Is a Different Kind of Risk

A basement floor holds a spill for a while. Soil does not. Oil that reaches bare ground soaks in and moves downward, and once it reaches groundwater it can travel with it. A well draws from that same groundwater, which is why a spill on a well lot gets treated differently from one on a lot with town water.

Say your outdoor tank sits on a stand thirty feet from the wellhead, on a slope that runs toward the well. A slow weep at the tank's bottom seam, the kind that might go unnoticed for weeks on a lawn, has a much shorter path to the water than the same leak on the far side of the house. Distance and slope both matter, and so do the soil and how deep the well draws from.

## Signs Fuel Oil Might Be in Your Well Water

[Health Canada's guidance on testing well water](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/environment/drinking-water/well/test.html) lists volatile and semi-volatile compounds as a concern when a fuel spill has occurred nearby, and names a leaking home heating oil tank as an example. It also points out that these compounds can reach you by breathing them in or through the skin while bathing, not only by drinking. Watch for:

- A fuel or diesel smell from the tap, even faint
- An oily taste, or water that tastes "off" in a way that is new
- A sheen or rainbow film on standing water in a sink or toilet tank
- The smell showing up after you run water that has been sitting in the pipes overnight

An oily-looking film alone is not proof. Iron bacteria can leave a similar sheen and are common in well water for reasons that have nothing to do with a tank. The tell is smell: if there's any fuel odour with it, treat it as a real possibility. And the absence of a smell doesn't prove the water is clean, so if a spill has happened near the well, test anyway.

## Stop Using the Water First, Ask Questions Second

If you smell fuel oil in your well water, don't wait for a lab result to change your habits. Stop drinking it and cooking with it, don't give it to pets, and because Health Canada notes exposure through the skin and breathing while bathing, hold off on long hot showers and baths too. Bottled or trucked water is the short-term fix while you sort out the source.

This is also the point to arrange a test for petroleum compounds, not the routine bacterial test most private wells get. New Brunswick's [Testing Private Water Supplies](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/ocmoh/healthy_environments/content/testing_private_watersupplies.html) page recommends routine testing twice a year for total coliform and E. coli, after the spring thaw and during the fall rainy season. That test tells you nothing about fuel oil. The same page says water should be tested for organic compounds if a petroleum spill has happened near the well, if the well is in an area that may be exposed to petroleum products, or if the water has a noticeable chemical taste or smell. Tell the lab you suspect heating oil when you book, and ask whether the lab holds national certification, as the province suggests.

1. **Stop using the water** for drinking, cooking, and pets, right away.

## Where an Oil Tank Should Sit Relative to a Well

There's no single number we can hand you here that applies to every property, and printing a made-up distance would do more harm than good. New Brunswick's [well construction page](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg/environment/content/land_waste/content/reference_manual/well_construction_well_water_testing.html) explains that the province's Water Well Regulation sets minimum distances when a new well is drilled, from things like buildings, roads, septic tanks, leaching fields, and sewer lines. Its setback table doesn't list a heating oil tank, and those rules govern where a new well can go, not where an existing tank has to sit.

So on an older property, the practical answer is about the tank. What matters is the same short list every time:

**Distance and slope.** Farther is better, and downhill from the well is better than uphill, because groundwater and surface runoff both tend to move downslope. You want a spill to run away from the wellhead, not toward it.

**Soil type.** Sandy or gravelly soil lets a spill travel fast and far. Dense clay slows it down, though it doesn't stop it.

**Tank type and visibility.** An outdoor tank on legs is easy to inspect from every side. A buried tank near a well is the higher-risk case, because you can't see it weeping until the damage is done. Our post on [indoor vs outdoor oil tanks](/blog/indoor-vs-outdoor-oil-tank/) covers the general trade-offs, and a well on the property tips the scale further toward a tank you can easily see.

**Depth and casing of the well itself.** A shallow dug well is generally more exposed to surface contamination than a deep drilled well with proper casing, though neither is immune to a serious spill close by.

The province's [guidelines on oil tank maintenance and spill prevention](https://www.gnb.ca/en/topic/environment-resources/waste-management/oil-tank-maintenance.html) add a few points that fit a well lot well: put the tank where it can be reached from all sides for inspection, set it on a poured concrete slab, choose a double wall tank for an outdoor install, and consider a containment tray or dyke and a leak detection alarm. The same page notes that homes inside a designated wellfield protected area (the zone around a municipal water supply) have special considerations for oil tanks, so if you're in one, call the regional office before you place a new tank.

## Replacing a Tank Near a Well

If your outdoor tank has been sitting near the wellhead for fifteen or twenty years, rust and a private well are not a combination worth waiting out. Steel tanks often corrode from the inside, so the outside can look fine right up until it isn't. A replacement is the natural time to choose a better spot, farther from the well and downslope of it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How close can an oil tank legally be to a water well in New Brunswick?

We haven't found a single published New Brunswick distance for an existing home heating oil tank, and we won't invent one. The province's Water Well Regulation sets distances for siting new wells from buildings, septic systems, and similar sources, but the setback table on its well construction page doesn't list oil tanks. On an existing property, keep the tank as far from the wellhead as the lot allows, downslope if possible, and easy to inspect.

### My well water smells faintly of oil. How urgent is this?

Treat it as urgent even if the smell is faint. Stop drinking and cooking with the water, don't give it to pets, and arrange a test for organic compounds, which the province recommends when a petroleum spill has happened near a well or the water has a chemical taste or smell. Report the suspected release too, and have the tank looked at to find the source.

### Does the routine private well water test check for oil contamination?

No. New Brunswick's routine recommendation is testing for total coliform and E. coli twice a year, after the spring thaw and in the fall rainy season. That panel says nothing about fuel oil. If you suspect a spill has reached your well, tell the lab specifically so they run a test for petroleum or organic compounds.

### Is a leak near a well more expensive to clean up than a normal spill?

It can be, because contamination that reaches groundwater usually means a more thorough site assessment and remediation than a spill contained to a basement floor or a small patch of lawn. Contaminated soil remediation starts at $10,000 and up on our cost page, and testing the well, monitoring it, or supplying water while it's sorted out adds to the bill.

### Should I move my tank if I'm worried about my well?

If your tank sits close to the wellhead or on ground that slopes toward it, a planned replacement is the best time to ask about a new spot. Relocation can add line and pipe work, so get it priced on the written estimate, but it's far simpler to move a tank a few metres during a planned swap than to deal with a contaminated well after the fact.
