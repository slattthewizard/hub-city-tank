# Oil Tank Overfill During a Delivery: What Happens and What to Do

An oil tank overfill happens when more oil goes into the tank than it can hold, usually because the vent whistle didn't warn the driver, the vent was blocked, or a fitting let go under pressure. The oil has nowhere to go but out, through the vent pipe, back up the fill pipe, or through a stressed seam, and it lands on your driveway, your foundation, or your basement floor. If that has just happened at your house, shut off what you safely can, keep people and pets clear, and call [(506) 806-0900](tel:+15068060900).

Most residential deliveries in Greater Moncton go by without a drop hitting the ground. When one does not, it is almost always one of two things: the tank's overfill warning failed, or something on the fill side (a hose, a cap, a fitting) gave out mid-delivery. Either way, the next 30 minutes matter more than anything that happens after.

This guide covers how an overfill happens during a delivery, what you will notice, the first steps to take, who is generally involved in sorting out the cleanup, and how to report it. Reporting and cleanup costs each have their own guide, linked further down.

## How an Oil Tank Overfill Happens During a Delivery

A delivery driver fills your tank without being able to see inside it. Their main real-time signal is the vent whistle, a small alarm fitted where the vent pipe enters the tank. It whistles as the incoming oil pushes air out through the vent and goes quiet once the oil level reaches it, near the top of the tank. As long as that whistle works and the driver stops when the sound cuts out, an overfill is unlikely.

Trouble starts when that signal is wrong or missing, or the vent can't do its job. A few ways it goes sideways:

- **A blocked or iced vent** means air can't escape as oil goes in. There's no whistle, pressure builds inside the tank, and oil can be forced back out the fill pipe or through a weak seam.
- **A missing or broken whistle** stays silent from the start, so the driver has no warning of when to stop. This is why many drivers won't fill when they can't hear one.
- **Pumping on after the whistle stops**, or trusting a stuck gauge that shows more room than the tank really has.
- **A loose or damaged fill pipe fitting** lets oil escape at the connection itself, even if the tank still has room.
- **A cracked or corroded seam**, usually near the bottom of an older steel tank, gives way under the added pressure and weight of a full tank.

## What You Will See and Smell When a Tank Overflows

An overfill rarely looks subtle. Depending on where it happens, expect one or more of these:

- Oil spraying or dripping from the vent pipe outlet, sometimes for a while after the driver stops
- A dark, oily stain spreading across a driveway, lawn, or the snowbank under an outdoor tank
- A strong fuel oil smell that shows up outdoors within minutes, or indoors if the tank is in a basement
- Oil backing up and running out around the fill pipe cap itself, rather than the vent
- On an indoor tank, oil pooling on the basement floor near the tank's base or fittings

Cold weather changes what you notice first. In freezing temperatures, spilled oil sometimes sits on top of snow or ice rather than soaking straight in, which can make a spill look smaller than it is while it works its way toward a drain or the ground underneath. A sudden, strong oil smell during or right after a delivery is worth walking outside for, even if nothing looks obviously wrong yet.

## The First Steps If Oil Spills During Your Delivery

What you do in the first half hour has more effect on the outcome than almost anything that follows.

1. **Get the driver to stop the pump immediately** if the delivery is still in progress. Most drivers will already be reacting to the same signs you are.
2. **Keep people and pets away** from the spill and any fumes, and get windows open if oil has reached indoor air.
3. **Skip anything with a flame or a spark** near the area. Furnace oil is not easy to ignite, but there is no reason to test that.
4. **Contain what you can, if it is safe to do so.** Kitty litter, sand, or absorbent pads around the edge of a puddle keep it from spreading toward a drain, a garden bed, or a ditch.
5. **Never hose it down.** Rinsing a spill toward a drain, the lawn, or the street turns a contained problem into a spreading one.
6. **Note the basics** while they are fresh: roughly how much oil is involved, where it went, and the delivery company and driver, if you know them. You will want this for your own records regardless of who else is taking notes.
7. **Make your calls**, covered in the next two sections.

If oil has reached a floor drain, a sump pit, bare soil, or shows any sign of moving toward a well or ditch, stop and let professionals take it from there. ## Who Is Generally Responsible for a Delivery Spill

This is the question everyone asks first, and the honest answer is that it depends on the details of your delivery, your insurance policy, and the terms of service with your fuel supplier. A spill that happens because the driver kept pumping past a working whistle sits differently than one caused by a tank fitting that failed on its own. In practice, both the delivery company and the homeowner tend to end up involved, one way or another, and untangling exactly who pays for what is a conversation for your fuel supplier and your insurer, not something we can rule on from the outside.

What we can tell you is what to do about it: report the spill (next section), call your fuel supplier that day and ask how they document delivery incidents, and call your home insurer before the day is out as well. ## Reporting an Overfill or Delivery Spill in New Brunswick

Heating oil that ends up somewhere it should not be, whether it dripped from a failed seam or shot out a vent pipe mid-delivery, is a spill, and the safe assumption is that it gets reported. New Brunswick's page on [reporting a contaminant release](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/corporate/promo/contaminated-sites-program/reporting.html) says anyone who detects a petroleum product leak or spill should notify the Department of Environment and Local Government right away. We know of no exception for a small spill during a delivery.

During business hours, the province points you to the department's regional office nearest the spill. After hours, or if you can't reach the office, call **1-800-565-1633**. That is the 24-hour number the [federal report an environmental emergency page](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program/report-emergency.html) lists for New Brunswick, answered by the Canadian Coast Guard's Maritimes Regional Office. Storage and handling of petroleum products in the province falls under the [Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation](https://laws.gnb.ca/en/showdoc/cr/87-97). If the spill is a fire risk or a danger to people, 911 comes first.

Have a few basics ready when you call: your address, roughly how much oil is involved, where it went, and whether it is still moving. ## Why the Vent Whistle Is Your Best Protection Against an Overfill

Every overfill during a delivery traces back to the same weak point: the driver lost their one reliable signal for when to stop. That is the whole job of the vent whistle, and it is worth treating it that way rather than as background noise you tune out during a fill.

Be home for a delivery once in a while and listen. A steady hum that ends right before the pump shuts off is exactly what should happen. Keep the vent cap clear of snow, ice, and anything that could nest in it, especially heading into winter. If a driver has ever tagged your tank and refused to fill it because the whistle would not sound, treat that as the warning it is rather than an inconvenience, because it means the one thing standing between a normal delivery and an overfill was not working that day.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should I do the moment I notice oil overflowing during a delivery?

Get the driver to stop pumping right away if they have not already, keep everyone clear of the area and any fumes, and avoid anything with a flame or spark nearby. Contain what you safely can with sand or absorbent pads rather than hosing it toward a drain or the lawn. Once it is contained, call the provincial spill line and your fuel supplier.

### Is a small overfill during delivery still something I have to report?

Treat it as reportable. New Brunswick's reporting guidance asks anyone who detects a petroleum leak or spill to notify the environment department right away, and we know of no exception for a few litres spilled during a delivery. Reporting promptly also gives you a record that matters later, whether that is with your insurer, the delivery company, or a future buyer.

### Who pays for the cleanup after a delivery overfill?

That depends on the circumstances of the delivery, your policy, and your supplier's terms, so it is not something we can answer in general terms. Call your fuel supplier and your home insurer right away, keep photos and notes from when it happened, and lean on the paper trail from reporting the spill. Our guide on insurance and oil tank leaks covers how coverage decisions typically get made.

### Can an overfill damage my tank even if the oil stays inside it?

Yes. A tank filled right to the top leaves no room for the oil to expand as it warms, and that pressure can push oil out through the vent, stress a fill pipe fitting, or strain a seam that was already weak. An overfill that seems to have caused no visible mess outside can still be a sign the tank was pushed past a safe limit.

### How do I know if my vent whistle is working before my next delivery?

Be home for one delivery and listen for a steady hum that stops just before the driver shuts off the pump. If you hear nothing at all, or the sound cuts out very early with the tank still mostly empty, that points to a blocked or failed vent alarm rather than a full tank. A quick check before your next fill is far cheaper than dealing with an overfill during one.

### What is different about an outdoor tank overfill in winter?

Snow and ice around the vent cap are a common reason the whistle goes silent in the first place, and spilled oil can sit on top of snow rather than soaking in right away, which makes a spill easy to underestimate at a glance. Clear snow and ice from the vent and fill pipes after storms, and treat any oil smell near an outdoor tank in winter as worth checking immediately rather than waiting for a thaw.
