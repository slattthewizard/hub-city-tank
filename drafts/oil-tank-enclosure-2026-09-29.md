# Building an Oil Tank Enclosure That Works in Southeast New Brunswick

A steel tank sitting in plain view on the side of a bungalow is not anyone's idea of a nice yard. So people build a screen, a little shed, or a decorative box around it, and most of the time that's a fine idea. The problem shows up when the box is built to hide the tank rather than to work with it.

An outdoor oil tank has parts that have to stay reachable and a shell that has to stay dry, and a poorly planned enclosure can undo both. This guide covers what a cover or enclosure needs to get right, what it needs to leave alone, and the designs to avoid if you're building one around a tank in Moncton, Dieppe, Riverview or anywhere nearby.

## Why People Cover a Tank in the First Place

There are really only two honest reasons to build around an outdoor tank: looks, and weather protection.

On the looks side, a lattice screen or a small painted surround softens what is otherwise a grey steel drum next to the driveway. That's a reasonable want, and it's compatible with a healthy tank as long as the build respects a few rules below.

On the weather side, a cover can genuinely help. Breaking direct wind and spray can slow the salt air corrosion that works on coastal tanks around Shediac and Pointe-du-Chêne faster than on an inland tank in Salisbury or Moncton, something we go into in [salt air oil tank corrosion](/blog/salt-air-oil-tank-corrosion/). A roof over the tank can also keep falling roof ice off the fittings, which matters most for tanks tucked under an eave, covered in [protecting your oil tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/).

What a cover cannot do is stop a tank from aging, hide a problem that's already there, or replace a proper base. If your tank is old, leaning or rusting through, an enclosure just makes a bad tank harder to inspect. We'll come back to that.

## What Has to Stay Reachable, No Matter What You Build

This is the part that matters most, because it's tempting to build the neatest possible box and forget that four things on that tank need someone's hands on them, sometimes on short notice.

**The fill pipe.** Your oil delivery driver needs to find it, reach it, and connect a hose to it without moving anything first. A door, a hinged panel, or an open side facing the driveway all work. A latched gate the driver has to hunt for in the dark during a February storm does not.

**The vent pipe and cap.** The vent lets air out of the tank as oil goes in, and on most home tanks it carries the whistle that tells the driver when to stop filling. It has to stay open to the outside air, and the driver has to be able to hear it. CSA B139, the installation code for oil-burning equipment, has addressed vents directly: [CSA Group's summary of the code's 10th edition](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/) says a vent pipe whose opening faces downward without a vent cap is not considered enough to keep out foreign objects and blockage by ice build-up. Build your roof or panel so the vent still terminates cleanly outward, not tucked under a shelf where meltwater and ice can collect on it. Our guide to the [oil tank vent whistle](/blog/oil-tank-vent-whistle/) covers what that part does and why a blocked one can lead to an overfill.

**The gauge.** You, or whoever checks your oil level, need to see it without climbing over anything.

**The filter, valve and oil line.** A technician needs room to kneel at the base of the tank, and the panel over that side should come off easily, not require tools you don't own.

And one more thing that isn't a fitting: the tank's whole outside surface. Granby Industries' [installation and maintenance guidelines](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0007_Na-ULC-S602-INSTALLATION-AND-MAINTENANCE-GUIDELINES.pdf) for its steel tanks tell owners to visually inspect the tank at least once a year, because careful inspection will uncover traces of oil on the tank surface, and to touch up rusted spots on the outside yearly. The same CSA summary says the 10th edition added an annual visual inspection for leakage in fuel oil tanks, tubing, piping and filters. You can't do either through a solid wall.

A good rule of thumb: if a task takes longer with the enclosure on than it did with the bare tank, the enclosure is built wrong.

## Ventilation Is Not Optional

The instinct to build a tight, weathertight box around a fuel tank is exactly backwards, and it's worth understanding why.

The main reason is moisture, with vapour and smell close behind. A tank's vapour is meant to leave through the vent pipe, which is why the vent has to end in open air, outside any structure you build, and never inside it. But fittings weep, fills splash, and a closed box holds whatever gets out. CSA B139 takes the same view in a related case: the CSA summary says the 10th edition allows fill or vent pipes to be recessed into a building's facade with a ventilated architectural cover, while limiting the risk of combustible vapour migrating into the building. A box around the whole tank deserves at least the same care.

A sealed enclosure also traps moisture against the steel instead of letting it dry off, and damp steel that never dries is steel that rusts. You'd be building a box that makes your tank rust faster while making it harder to see happening.

The fix is simple: leave real gaps, not decorative slots. Open the top and bottom of any solid-sided structure so air moves through it, the way a louvred vent works. A lattice or slatted screen does this by design. A solid shed needs vents built in on purpose, not an afterthought cut in after the fact.

## Snow Load Is a Design Problem, Not a Shovelling Problem

Southeast New Brunswick gets real snow load, and anything you build with a roof over a tank has to be built to carry it, not just look like it can.

A flimsy roof over the tank does two things wrong. First, it can collapse under a heavy wet snowfall directly onto the gauge, vent and fill pipe, which is the exact failure mode we describe for roof avalanches in [protecting your oil tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/). Second, even a roof that holds can dump everything it collects straight down onto the tank's fittings in one thaw if it isn't pitched to shed snow away from the tank.

If you're adding a roof, pitch it so snow slides clear of the fill pipe, the vent and the gauge, not toward them, and build it to the same standard you'd expect from a small shed roof, not a garden trellis. If you're not confident in the structure, a simple three-sided windbreak with no roof at all avoids the problem entirely, the same approach we describe for coastal screening earlier.

## What Not to Do

A few designs to avoid.

**Don't build something that hides a leak.** A wet spot, a sheen, or an oil smell near the tank is easy to spot on a bare tank and easy to miss inside a closed structure. If you already suspect a leak, deal with that first and skip the enclosure. New Brunswick expects a release of oil to be reported: call 911 first if there's any fire risk, the regional Department of Environment and Local Government office during business hours, or the province's 24-hour environmental emergency line, 1-800-565-1633, which is listed on Environment and Climate Change Canada's [environmental emergency contacts page](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program/report-emergency.html). Our [guide to reporting an oil spill in New Brunswick](/blog/report-oil-spill-nb/) walks through when that applies.

**Don't block the vent, even partly.** A shelf, a hook, a coiled hose, anything hung over the vent cap defeats the whistle and raises the odds of an overfill nobody catches in time.

**Don't skip the base just because there's now a pretty box on top of it.** Whatever you build should leave the tank's own base alone and in plain view. The same Granby guidelines say the tank base shall be level, rigid and non-combustible, and they warn that new outdoor installs on recently disturbed ground are at particular risk of base movement. An enclosure built around a tank that's leaning or sinking just makes the problem harder to see, and a structure should never rest on or brace against the tank or its legs. Our guide to [oil tank stands and pads](/blog/oil-tank-stand-and-pad/) covers what that base should look like.

**Don't assume every town treats a small structure the same way.** Rules on accessory structures, setbacks and whether a permit is needed vary by municipality across Westmorland, Albert and Kent counties, so check with the town or city before you build anything permanent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I fully enclose my outdoor oil tank in a solid box?

You can, but only if the box is ventilated top and bottom, has a door or removable panel that gives the delivery driver and a technician real access to the fill pipe, vent, gauge and filter, and doesn't trap moisture against the steel. A fully sealed box with no vents and no door is the one design to avoid outright.

### Will an enclosure void my tank's warranty or my insurance?

We can't speak to specific warranty terms or insurance policies, since those vary by manufacturer and insurer. Check your tank's paperwork and your policy before you build, and if in doubt, ask your insurer directly whether a proposed cover changes anything about how they view the installation.

### Does a lattice screen count as an enclosure?

For the purposes of ventilation and access, yes, and it's usually the safest option because it's open by design. It won't block wind and salt spray as completely as a solid panel, but it also can't trap vapour or moisture against the tank the way a sealed structure can.

### Do I need a permit to build a cover or shed around my oil tank?

That depends on the size of the structure and the municipality. Moncton, Dieppe, Riverview and the other towns in Southeast New Brunswick each set their own rules for accessory structures, so check with the local building department before you start.

### My tank is already rusting. Should I build an enclosure to hide it or replace it?

Replace it, or at least have it looked at first. An enclosure makes a failing tank harder to inspect, not safer, and it does nothing to stop corrosion that's already underway. Call us for a free written estimate and we'll tell you honestly whether the tank is worth building around.

### What's the cheapest way to improve how my tank looks without risking anything?

A simple lattice or slatted screen set back from the tank, with room to walk all the way around it, gets most of the visual benefit with the least risk. It stays ventilated, keeps every part of the tank reachable, and doesn't require a roof strong enough to carry snow load.
