# Listing Says the Oil Tank Was "Filled in Place"? What a Buyer Should Ask For

When a listing, a seller or a lawyer says the old buried oil tank was "filled in place" or "decommissioned," it means the tank was supposed to be emptied, cleaned and filled with an inert material, then left in the ground. It does not mean the tank is gone, and it does not mean the soil around it was tested. Before you rely on those words, ask for the paperwork, ask what happened to the soil, and ask your insurer what it will accept. The answers tell you whether to accept the tank, ask for testing, or make removal a condition.

This guide is for a buyer looking at an older house around Moncton, Dieppe or Riverview with a tank still in the yard on paper. ## What "Filled in Place" Actually Means

Filling a tank in place, also called closure in place or abandonment in place, is a specific procedure. Our guide for owners, [can you abandon an oil tank in place](/blog/abandon-oil-tank-in-place/), covers the owner's side. For a buyer, the useful question is what a proper job leaves behind. In outline:

1. The top of the tank is uncovered so it can be opened.
2. Oil, water and sludge are pumped out and taken away.
3. The inside is cleaned.
4. The tank is filled with an inert material, most often clean sand, sometimes a flowable concrete mix, so the shell can't collapse or fill with water.
5. The fill and vent pipes and the oil line are disconnected and capped, or removed.
6. Samples of the soil around the tank are taken, ideally, to see whether it leaked before closure.
7. The work is written up.

That last step is where listings go quiet. "Decommissioned" can describe all seven steps done by a contractor with a report, or it can describe someone pumping out the oil in 1995 and putting a lid on it. Both can end up in the same sentence of a listing. You can't tell which you have from the sentence.

A Canadian local-government document gives the same outline. A [homeowner's guide from the Capital Regional District in British Columbia](https://www.crd.ca/media/file/wwrhome-heating-oil-tanks-oct3webpdf) describes decommissioning in place as uncovering the top of the tank, cutting it open, removing any oil or sludge, filling it with clean sand and reburying it. It's a BC document and New Brunswick's rules and insurers differ, so read it as background, not as the rule here.

## What Paperwork Should Exist

If a tank really was closed properly, someone should be able to hand you a record. Ask the seller, through your agent, for any of these:

- **A closure or decommissioning report or certificate** from the contractor, with the date and the method.
- **What the tank was filled with**, and roughly how much.
- **Soil sampling results,** with a lab report, if samples were taken.
- **Invoices or receipts** for the work, which at least show who did it and when.
- **Any correspondence with the insurer** at the time about the closure.
- **A sketch or description of where the tank sits,** since the next owner needs to know.

An estate seller or a seller who bought the house recently may have none of this. That alone isn't a reason to walk away. It does mean the words in the listing are a claim, not evidence, and you're buying the tank on what you can verify yourself.

Dates matter too. A tank "filled in place years ago" could have been closed under standards that insurers wouldn't accept today. Ask when, who did it, and whether anyone can speak to it.

## Why Some Insurers and Lenders Still Want It Out

A tank full of sand is far safer than a tank full of oil, but it's still a steel shell in the ground, and its history is still the question. The CRD guide says that in BC, even where the local fire department has signed off on a professional decommissioning, insurance companies are now requiring complete professional removal and surrounding soil testing, and that most financial institutions and insurers there require full removal and soil testing to give a mortgage or home insurance. That is a statement about BC. We can't tell you what a New Brunswick insurer will do with your file, and different insurers handle it differently. But it shows how the question tends to go.

The reasoning is fair enough. An insurer can't see what happened to the soil. If the tank leaked before it was closed, the oil is still in the ground, and the owner at the time it's found can be the one left paying for it. Our article on [soil remediation in New Brunswick](/blog/soil-remediation-nb/) explains what that process looks like and why it's the expensive end of an oil tank problem.

The insurance industry's standards body, CSIO, publishes a national [Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://www.csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire) for personal lines property insurance. If an insurer sends you something similar for a house with a closed-in-place tank, expect questions about the tank's status and what's been done, and have the documents ready.

So the practical advice is to ask your broker before you waive conditions. Say plainly: "The seller says a buried oil tank was filled in place in the yard. Will you insure the house with that, and what do you need to see?" Get the answer in writing. Lenders generally want proof of insurance before they advance funds, so an answer that comes late can threaten a closing date.

## The Open Question Is the Soil

The soil is what separates a closed file from a question mark. Filling a tank does nothing to oil that already escaped. A tank that was clean and dry at closure, with sampling to show it, is a different thing from a tank that was filled without anyone checking.

If there's no soil data, there are three ways to learn more:

- **Ask for any records.** Some are held by the contractor, the seller's lawyer or the previous owner.
- **Have the soil sampled.** That means a test pit or boreholes near the tank, with samples sent to a lab. Access needs the seller's permission, which takes time on an estate or power-of-sale listing. We're not giving a price for testing; ask for quotes from the people who do it.
- **Dig the tank out.** That answers the question directly, because the soil around and beneath the tank is visible and sampled as it comes out.

Also check that there's only one tank. Our guides on [finding a buried oil tank](/blog/find-buried-oil-tank/) and on [oil tank scans before buying](/blog/oil-tank-sweep-before-buying/) explain how a scan works, and a scan can confirm where a filled tank is and whether another is nearby. The CRD guide notes that if you find one tank you shouldn't stop scanning, because some properties have more than one.

## Three Ways to Handle It in a Purchase

### Accept it, with paperwork

This is reasonable when you have a dated closure report from a contractor, soil results that show clean samples, and an insurer who has told you in writing it will insure the house. In that case the tank is a documented, closed item, and you can keep a copy of everything for the day you sell. Make sure the documents are transferred to you and that your lawyer sees them.

### Ask for testing

If the paperwork is thin but not absent, or the insurer is open to the idea with proof, asking the seller to allow soil sampling before your conditions expire is a fair request. The results then decide the rest: clean results strengthen your position with the insurer, and bad results mean you can renegotiate or walk. If the seller won't allow it, you need to price in the uncertainty.

### Require removal

If there's no paperwork, the insurer says it wants the tank out, or the signs point to a leak, removal is the cleanest answer. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a tank filled in place the same as a tank removed?

No. A filled tank is still in the ground. Removal takes the tank out and lets the soil around and under it be seen and sampled. Filling in place can be done properly, but it leaves a steel shell behind, and some insurers and buyers prefer the tank gone.

### What should the seller give me if the tank was decommissioned?

A report or certificate from the contractor with the date and method, what it was filled with, any soil sampling results and the invoice. If none of that exists, treat the listing's wording as a claim to check, and ask your broker what the insurer will accept.

### Can I get home insurance with a filled-in-place tank?

Maybe, and it depends on the insurer. Some want paperwork, some want testing, some want the tank removed. Ask your broker before you waive conditions, tell them exactly what you know, and get the answer in writing.

### Does filling a tank stop it from contaminating the soil?

It stops the shell from collapsing and from holding oil, but it does nothing about oil that leaked before closure. Soil sampling is what shows whether that happened.

### How do I know whether there's another tank on the property?

You don't, without checking. A scan with a metal detector or ground-penetrating radar can show tanks that aren't mentioned in the listing. Our scan guide, linked above, explains what a scan can and can't tell you.
