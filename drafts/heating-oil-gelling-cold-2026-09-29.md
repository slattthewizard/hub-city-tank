# Heating Oil Gelling: Why Furnace Oil Thickens in the Cold and What Helps

Your furnace runs fine all fall, then on the first real cold snap it sputters, locks out, or won't fire, and the gauge shows plenty of oil. That combination points to heating oil gelling: the fuel itself thickening with wax as the temperature drops, usually in an exposed line, filter, or outdoor tank rather than deep inside a full tank of oil.

It is a real, physical process, not a myth or a sales pitch for an additive. It is also predictable. ## What Heating Oil Gelling Is

Furnace oil is a middle distillate, a close cousin of diesel fuel and heavier than kerosene. Distillates like this carry dissolved paraffin wax, and wax has a habit that plain fuel doesn't: it stays dissolved when the oil is warm and comes out of solution as the fuel cools.

The temperature where the first, smallest wax crystals appear is called the cloud point. Cool the fuel further and more wax drops out, the crystals grow and link up, and the oil thickens until, at its pour point, it barely flows at all. Somewhere between those two temperatures, the crystals are big enough to plug a filter or a narrow line. That is what people mean by gelling.

There is no single gelling temperature for furnace oil, and anyone who quotes you one is quoting a typical batch. The 2020 edition of Canada's standard for this fuel, [CAN/CGSB-3.2, Heating Fuel Oil](https://publications.gc.ca/site/eng/9.884936/publication.html), does not set a fixed cloud point for Type 2, the grade used in most household oil burners. It lists the cloud point as a value to report, and says it may be specified to suit the conditions of storage and use. It does set a cloud point of minus 48 or lower for Type 0, a grade meant for domestic appliances with outside storage where temperatures that low can happen. And it says lighter Type 1 oil may be used in place of Type 2 in colder weather to reduce waxing.

The important distinction is between clouding and full gelling. Oil that has just started to cloud still flows and still burns. Gelled oil in a line or a filter does not move, and that is when the furnace locks out.

## Why Outdoor Tanks and Exposed Lines Fail First

The oil sitting in a large steel tank is slow to change temperature. A line or a filter is not.

The oil line from the tank to the burner is usually 3/8 inch copper, with a lot of surface area for the little oil it holds. Run that line along an outside wall, across an open crawl space entry, or through an unheated corner, and it tracks the outdoor air temperature within minutes, not hours. The oil inside that skinny run can be well past its cloud point while the several hundred litres back in the tank are still liquid and unbothered.

The filter is the second weak point, for a related reason. Wax crystals travel with the oil, and a filter's fine element is built to catch the smallest debris in the fuel. On a hard cold night, that same element catches wax crystals just as readily, and a filter that was already carrying some sludge or rust from an older tank clogs even faster once wax joins the mix. ## Winter Blends: Ask What Goes in Your Tank

Most homeowners never have to think about any of this, because the fuel is meant to be matched to the season. Natural Resources Canada's [Heating with Oil](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/sites/nrcan/files/energy/pdf/energystar/Heating-with-Oil_EN.pdf) guide notes that Number 2 fuel oil is the grade used for most home heating, and that in colder regions Number 1, a slightly lighter fuel, is used. Blending lighter Type 1 oil into Type 2 lowers the temperature the mix can handle before it clouds, and the national standard also allows pour-depressed and flow-improved oil designed for the conditions of storage and use.

That is also where the question of diesel and stove oil comes in. Our guide to [furnace oil vs diesel vs stove oil](/blog/furnace-oil-vs-diesel/) covers how those three fuels relate. Stove oil sits at the lighter, kerosene end of the same family.

If your tank or line sits somewhere exposed, tell your supplier and ask what they deliver to outdoor tanks in winter, and whether it already contains a cold-flow treatment. That conversation costs nothing, and for most homeowners it is a better first step than any additive bought off a shelf.

## Anti-Gel Additives: What They Can and Cannot Do

Anti-gel or cold-flow additives are real products with a real mechanism, and the national fuel standard lists additives that improve low-temperature flow among those a heating oil may contain. Most work as flow improvers or pour-point depressants, changing the shape of the wax crystals as they form so the crystals stay small and separate instead of linking into a solid mass. That keeps fuel moving through a filter and a line at a lower temperature than untreated oil would manage.

What they do not do is remove the wax or raise the temperature outside. An additive extends how cold the fuel can get before it gels. It does not turn furnace oil into something that never waxes, and dosing has to happen before the fuel gets cold, not after a line is already solid.

| Situation | What an additive can help with | What it cannot fix |
|---|---|---|
| Exposed line, occasional deep cold | Keeps the oil in that run flowing to a lower temperature | A line that is already gelled solid right now |
| Older filter, some sludge already present | Nothing directly; wax and sludge are separate problems | A filter clogging fast because of tank condition, not cold |
| Tank showing rust, water, or sludge | Nothing; additive treats the fuel, not the tank | Internal corrosion, water at the bottom, or a failing seam |
| One cold outdoor tank every winter | Can be a reasonable standing precaution | A tank or line placement that will keep causing this every year |

Ask your supplier before adding anything yourself. Some winter blends already include a cold-flow package, and an aftermarket additive on top of that rarely helps. If a line is gelled right now, do not try to force it open with an open flame, a torch, or a substitute fuel poured in at the wrong point. That turns a cold-weather inconvenience into a safety call. Clearing a gelled line and bleeding the burner afterward is a job for an oil burner technician.

## When It Is Not the Cold, It Is the Tank

Gelling is seasonal and temporary. It shows up on the coldest nights and eases once the line and filter warm back up. If your fuel trouble does not follow that pattern, the cold is probably not the real cause.

A few signs point somewhere else entirely. If the filter clogs at temperatures that never bothered it in past winters, or clogs more than once in a season, [sludge building up in the tank](/blog/oil-tank-sludge/) is a more likely explanation than wax. Sludge is years of rust flakes, condensation, and broken-down oil settling on the tank bottom, and it gets pulled into the line right along with any wax that forms. The two problems compound each other. With a sound tank, a filter change and a talk with your supplier are often enough. With an older, corroding tank, the filter keeps plugging because the tank keeps feeding it.

[Water sitting in the tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/) is the other common culprit that gets blamed on the cold. Water is heavier than oil and settles at the bottom, which on a bottom-outlet tank is exactly where the line draws from. It can freeze in an outdoor line or foul the filter well before the oil itself is anywhere near its cloud point. If your furnace struggles specifically after a delivery, or a filter that was clean two weeks ago is full of dark paste, water and sludge deserve a look before you assume it was just a cold night.

None of this means a gelled line calls for a new tank. Often the fix is a rerouted or protected line, a filter changed more often through winter, or a word to your supplier about the blend. But if the tank is old, has been patched or weeping, or shows rust at a seam, treating the fuel with an additive is a bandage on the wrong problem. A tank that age and condition should be assessed on its own terms, not diagnosed by guesswork through a cold January.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### At what temperature does heating oil freeze or gel?

There is no single number. Wax starts to form at the oil's cloud point, and the fuel stops flowing at its lower pour point, but both vary by batch and blend. Canada's heating fuel standard does not fix a cloud point for Type 2 furnace oil; it is reported and can be specified to suit storage conditions. Winter blends and additives push both colder, but an exposed line or filter still gets there before the oil in the tank.

### My oil line is frozen. Is that the same as gelling?

Not quite, though the symptom looks identical from the furnace's point of view. A gelled line is blocked by wax that formed in the fuel itself. A frozen line usually means water, either condensation or water that got into the fuel, has frozen solid inside the copper. Both stop oil from reaching the burner, and both need a technician to clear safely rather than heat or force applied at home.

### Will an anti-gel additive completely stop my oil from gelling?

No additive makes furnace oil immune to cold. A cold-flow additive keeps the wax crystals small and stops them linking into a solid mass, which buys some margin on an exposed line. It will not help oil that has already gelled solid, and it will not compensate for sludge, water, or a tank that is corroding from the inside.

### Why does this only happen to my outdoor tank and not my neighbour's basement tank?

An outdoor tank and its line track the outside air temperature directly, with nothing insulating them overnight. A basement or indoor tank sits in heated space and almost never gets close to its cloud point. Location matters as much as the fuel, which is why our winter preparation checklist spends time on protecting exposed lines.

### Can I pour gasoline, windshield washer fluid, or a diesel additive into my oil tank to unclog a gelled line?

No. Gasoline in an oil burner system is a fire hazard, and washer fluid is not a fuel at all. Even a proper cold-flow additive has to be in the oil before it gets cold and has to be labelled for heating oil; poured into the tank, it will not clear a line that has already gelled. The safe fix is an oil burner technician and, if it happens every winter, protecting or rerouting that section of line.

### Does keeping my tank full over winter help with gelling too?

Indirectly. A full tank has less air space for humid air to condense in, which limits the water that ends up at the bottom feeding sludge and corrosion. It does not change the cloud point of the oil, but a tank with less water and sludge is less likely to have a filter or line problem that gets blamed on the cold.
