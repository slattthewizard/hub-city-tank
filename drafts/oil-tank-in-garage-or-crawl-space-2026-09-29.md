# Oil Tank in a Garage or Crawl Space: What Changes About the Rules

Plenty of Southeast New Brunswick homes don't have a clean basement spot or a flat patch of yard for an oil tank, so the tank ends up in the attached garage or tucked into a crawl space under the house. Both spots can work for a residential tank, but neither one is quite the same as a basement or an open yard, and the difference matters for safety, inspection, and cost.

The short version: a garage tank needs protection from vehicles unless its spot already keeps cars away, a crawl space tank needs a stable base and real access for anyone servicing it, and pulling a tank out of either spot usually costs more than a wide-open basement removal. ## Garage and Crawl Space Aren't Quite Indoor or Outdoor

Our guide to [indoor vs outdoor oil tanks](/blog/indoor-vs-outdoor-oil-tank/) covers the two classic locations: a heated basement, or a tank standing outside in the weather. A garage or crawl space tank sits in between the two.

An attached but unheated garage swings closer to outdoor temperatures in a Moncton winter than a basement does, so a tank there sees more of the condensation cycle that ages outdoor tanks, just without direct snow and rain landing on the shell. A crawl space is usually a little more sheltered and closer to basement temperatures, but it brings a different problem: low clearance, dirt or gravel underfoot, and a lot less room to look the tank over than an open basement gives you.

Both locations share one real advantage over a tank standing outside: no roof snow sliding onto the shell and no ice building up around the fittings. That's one less seasonal check on your list, which matters in a region with as many freeze-thaw cycles as ours.

## The Vehicle Protection Rule for a Garage Tank

This is the part most homeowners have never heard of. CSA B139, the national installation code for oil-burning equipment covered in our [CSA B139 guide](/blog/csa-b139-oil-tank-code/), made vehicle protection a mandatory clause in its 2009 edition. Nova Scotia's fuel safety office, which adopts the code into provincial law, walked technicians through that change in a [fuel safety bulletin on CSA B139-09](https://novascotia.ca/lae/fuelsafety/docs/FSB0906.pdf). Under clause 7.4.10 of that edition, unless the tank is protected by its location, the tank and anything attached to it have to be protected from vehicle movement in line with good engineering practice. The bulletin notes the idea sat in a non-mandatory annex in earlier editions, and it reads good engineering practice as protection that can stop a moving vehicle, not just mark the spot.

The same bulletin answers the garage question head-on: if a car can hit the tank, it needs protection. Prince Edward Island's [home heat tanks FAQ](https://www.princeedwardisland.ca/sites/default/files/publications/home_heat_tanks_-_frequently_asked_questions.pdf) says the same thing for that province. Tanks are allowed in residential garages there, the tank, its equipment and its piping must be protected from damage if the location doesn't already do it, and posts and guardrails are recommended, set at least 4 inches from every side of the tank.

In practice, that usually means steel posts or bollards set where a vehicle backing in or pulling forward can't clip the tank shell, the fill pipe, or the vent. A tank tucked into a corner a vehicle can't reach may not need posts at all. Clause numbers shift between editions, so ask your installer how the current CSA B139 handles it for your layout. Keep in mind that a tank installed under an earlier edition of the code went in under older rules, so an older garage install may have no posts at all.

The 2009 edition also widened protection for the piping itself. The Nova Scotia bulletin describes clause 9.3.1.5 as covering piping and tubing protection from damage such as foot and vehicle traffic, grass cutting and snow. In a garage, that means the oil line running along a floor or wall near where you park deserves as much thought as the tank body.

## New Brunswick's Own Rules Sit on Top of the Code

New Brunswick also has its own petroleum storage rules under the Clean Environment Act. The province's [petroleum storage tanks page](https://www.gnb.ca/en/topic/environment-resources/waste-management/petroleum-storage-tanks.html) explains that under the Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation (Reg 87-97), systems holding 2,000 litres or more need the minister's approval before installation and a petroleum storage site licence, and that only licensed installers may work on systems of that size. A single residential tank is usually well under that threshold on its own. Granby's installation guidelines for its steel tanks, for example, cover obround models from 455 to 1,135 litres.

So for a typical one-tank house, the 2,000 litre licensing rules aren't the ones doing the work. What still matters, in a garage, a crawl space, or anywhere else, is a proper installation to the CSA B139 code and the tank maker's instructions, and whatever your insurer asks for.

## Clearances, the Base, and Getting an Inspector In

For an indoor tank, PEI's FAQ gives the separations that province checks: at least 2 feet between the tank and the closest part of any fuel-fired appliance, at least 18 inches of clearance along one end and one side, and at least 2 inches between the tank and the wall. Those are PEI's published figures, not a New Brunswick table, but they show what an inspector is looking for: room between the tank and the burner, and room for a person to work.

The base matters most in a crawl space. Granby's [installation guidelines for its steel tanks](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0007_Na-ULC-S602-INSTALLATION-AND-MAINTENANCE-GUIDELINES.pdf) say the tank base shall be level, rigid and non-combustible, with a poured concrete floor as the best option. An older crawl space tank sitting straight on dirt or loose gravel doesn't meet that, and getting a proper pad in after the fact is hard work in a space you can't stand up in.

Access matters just as much as the base. The same Granby guidelines say a tank should sit where it can be visually inspected from all sides, with its label visible, and where normal household activity is unlikely to bother it. Whoever looks at the tank each year, for an insurer, a home sale, or routine upkeep, needs to read that label, check the legs and base, and get eyes on the vent, fill, and filter. A garage usually gives that access without much trouble. A crawl space with two or three feet of headroom does not, and a tank nobody can comfortably inspect is a tank nobody can vouch for when your insurer asks about it. ## Venting and the Fill Pipe Still Have to Reach Outside

Wherever the tank sits, the fill point and the vent work the same way they would anywhere else. The delivery driver fills from outside, so the fill pipe has to reach a point the truck can access, and the vent needs its whistle where the driver can hear it stop. That means running pipework from a garage or crawl space out through a wall.

The Nova Scotia bulletin describes two 2009 clauses for those wall crossings. Under 9.3.1.9, piping or tubing that passes through an exterior wall must be watertight and either sleeved or double wrapped with a waterproof wrap, and 9.3.1.10 applies the same rule to interior masonry or concrete walls. PEI's FAQ states the same requirement in plain words. Either way, an open hole cut around the pipe isn't good enough. Our breakdown of [oil tank parts](/blog/oil-tank-parts-explained/) covers what the fill pipe, vent, gauge, and valve each do if you want the fuller picture.

A loose or unsleeved wall penetration is an easy spot for water to track in over a winter, and it's the kind of thing that goes unnoticed for months in a space nobody walks through every day.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does an oil tank in a garage need vehicle protection posts?

If a vehicle can reach the tank, yes. The 2009 edition of CSA B139, as Nova Scotia's fuel safety bulletin explains it, requires the tank and anything attached to it to be protected from vehicles unless the location already does that, and the bulletin reads that as protection able to stop a moving vehicle. A tank in a corner no vehicle can reach may not need posts, but most garage tanks sit close enough to the parking spot to need them.

### Can a furnace oil tank go in a crawl space?

It can, if it sits on a level, rigid, non-combustible base rather than bare dirt or loose gravel, keeps its clearance from the furnace or boiler, and stays accessible enough for someone to read the label and check the tank each year. A crawl space too tight to inspect properly is a real problem regardless of what the code allows on paper, so have an installer look at the space before you commit to it.

### Why does removing a tank from a crawl space cost more than from a garage?

Low headroom is the main reason. A garage tank on an open floor usually comes out in one piece through a wide door. A crawl space tank often has to be pumped out, cleaned and taken apart by the crew so it fits through a small hatch or low door, which takes more time and more hands. That pushes the price toward the higher end of the indoor removal range on our [oil tank replacement cost](/oil-tank-replacement-cost/) page.

### Does the fill pipe still have to reach outside if the tank is in the garage?

Yes. The fill point and the vent, whistle included, end outdoors no matter where the tank itself sits, so the delivery driver can fill from outside and hear the whistle stop. That means at least one wall crossing between the tank and the outside of the house, and it should be watertight and sleeved or wrapped, as the code clauses described above require.

### Will my insurer treat a garage tank the same as an indoor basement tank?

That depends on the insurer, so ask directly rather than assuming. The insurance industry's national [Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://www.csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire), published by CSIO, asks whether the tank, supply line and valve are protected from impact, naming vehicles and ice as examples. Expect that question about a garage tank, and keep your installation paperwork, since that's usually what settles it at renewal.
