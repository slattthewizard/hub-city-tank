# Furnace Oil vs Diesel vs Stove Oil: What's the Difference?

It's 11:30 on a Friday night in February, the furnace just sputtered out, and your gauge reads empty. Could a jerry can of diesel from the Irving up the road keep the heat on until Monday? In most homes, yes, it could.

That surprise is at the heart of the furnace oil vs diesel question, and it comes up every single winter around Moncton. The two fuels are so similar that one can stand in for the other in a pinch. But they're not identical, and the differences show up in your wallet, your burner, and especially your oil tank. Add stove oil to the mix, which plenty of older homes in Southeast New Brunswick still use, and things get confusing fast.

Here's the plain-spoken breakdown, with real numbers in Canadian dollars.

## The Short Answer: Three Cousins From the Same Barrel

Furnace oil, stove oil, and diesel all come from the same part of the oil refining process. A refinery splits crude oil into cuts, and the middle cut, called middle distillate, becomes all three of these fuels. The U.S. Energy Information Administration puts it plainly: heating oil and diesel are nearly the same product, separated mostly by taxes and dye. You can read their overview at [eia.gov](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/heating-oil/).

The real differences boil down to three things:

- **The weight of the cut.** Stove oil is a lighter No. 1 oil, close to kerosene. Furnace oil and diesel are both heavier No. 2 oils.
- **Dye and taxes.** Fuel sold for home heating is dyed red and skips road taxes. Diesel at the pumps is clear and carries full road taxes.
- **Engine specs.** Diesel has cetane and lubricity requirements because it runs engines. Your oil burner doesn't care about either.

So when people ask about furnace oil vs diesel, they're mostly asking about colour, tax, and a few small specs, not two completely different fuels.

## What Is Furnace Oil, Exactly?

Furnace oil is the everyday name for No. 2 heating oil, the stuff the delivery truck pumps into most oil-heated homes from Moncton to Sackville. If you have an oil furnace or boiler, this is almost certainly what you're burning.

A few things worth knowing about it:

**It's dyed red.** The red dye marks it as tax-exempt heating fuel. That's why it's often called marked fuel. Running marked fuel in a road vehicle is illegal, and enforcement officers check for it, so don't get cute with the lawn tractor's big brother.

**It's cheaper than pump diesel.** Because it skips road taxes, furnace oil typically runs 15 to 25 cents per litre less than clear diesel at the pumps. In recent New Brunswick winters, delivered furnace oil has mostly floated between about $1.30 and $1.55 per litre.

**You burn a lot of it.** A typical oil-heated home in our region goes through roughly 2,000 to 2,500 litres a year. At $1.40 per litre, that's $2,800 to $3,500 a season, which is why even a few cents per litre matters.

**It can thicken in deep cold.** Straight No. 2 oil starts to wax up when an outdoor tank sits through a stretch of minus 20 nights. That's why suppliers blend in lighter No. 1 oil during winter for outdoor tanks. If your tank lives outside, tell your supplier, and they'll adjust the blend.

## Stove Oil: The Old-Timer That's Getting Harder to Find

Stove oil is the lighter No. 1 cut, sitting in the kerosene family. It burns cleaner and flows better in the cold, which made it the go-to fuel for oil ranges, pot burner stoves, and space heaters in older homes and cottages across Kent and Westmorland counties.

The catch is supply. Fewer and fewer trucks carry it, and you pay for that scarcity.

Diane Cormier heats her cottage outside Shediac with an oil range her parents installed decades ago. Last winter, only one supplier in her area would still deliver stove oil, and it ran 22 cents per litre more than furnace oil. On a 500 litre top-up, that was about $110 extra, every single fill. She's now pricing out a conversion, because the fuel her stove needs is slowly disappearing from the market.

If you're in the same boat, don't just switch fuels and hope. Pot burner stoves are designed for the lighter oil, and heavier furnace oil can smoke, soot, and burn poorly in them. ## Furnace Oil vs Diesel: The Real Differences

Now the matchup everyone actually searches for. Here's how the three fuels compare side by side:

| | Furnace oil | Stove oil | Road diesel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Also called | No. 2 heating oil | No. 1 oil, kerosene range | Clear diesel, ULSD |
| Colour | Dyed red | Dyed red | Clear to pale yellow |
| Taxes | Heating fuel, no road taxes | Heating fuel, no road taxes | Full road taxes included |
| Cold weather | Can wax in deep cold unless winter-blended | Best cold-weather flow | Winter blends flow well |
| Typical NB price | About $1.30 to $1.55 per litre | Often $0.10 to $0.25 more than furnace oil | About $1.60 to $1.85 per litre |
| Best use | Furnaces and boilers | Oil stoves, cold outdoor tanks | Short-term emergency top-up |

Prices bounce around with the market, so treat those ranges as ballpark figures from recent New Brunswick winters, not quotes.

Chemically, modern road diesel and furnace oil are closer than they've ever been. Diesel is ultra-low sulphur by law, and most heating oil sold in Canada now comes from the same low-sulphur stream. Diesel also carries additives for engines, like cetane improvers and lubricity agents. Your burner doesn't need them, but they don't hurt it either. The EIA has a good plain-language explainer on diesel itself at [eia.gov](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/diesel-fuel/).

The one-way rule is worth repeating. Diesel in your oil tank is legal, you're just donating road tax you didn't owe. Marked furnace oil in your truck is illegal, full stop.

## Can You Burn Diesel in Your Oil Furnace in an Emergency?

Yes, and for a lot of Moncton families, it has saved a frozen weekend.

Marc LeBlanc in Riverview found his tank empty during a cold snap last January, at suppertime on a Friday. His supplier's next regular delivery was Monday, and a weekend emergency run carried about a $200 premium. Instead, he bought two 20 litre jerry cans of clear winter diesel at roughly $1.80 per litre, about $72 total, poured them into the fill pipe, and kept the house warm until the truck arrived.

If you try the same thing, do it the safe way:

1. **Buy winter diesel from the pumps**, in proper fuel containers. Two or three 20 litre cans is plenty to bridge a weekend.
2. **Pour it into the outside fill pipe**, never into the tank's top fittings indoors, and wipe up any drips.
3. **Wait 15 to 30 minutes** so any stirred-up sediment can settle before the burner draws fuel.
4. **Press the burner reset button once.** Once only. Repeated resets can flood the chamber with unburned oil and create a dangerous restart.

If the furnace won't fire after one reset, if you smell oil strongly, or if you see any wet spots near the tank or lines, stop there and call a professional. ## What Fuel Switching Does to an Older Tank

Here's the part most fuel articles skip, and the part we see up close every week.

Any time you dump fuel into a tank through jerry cans, you churn up what's sitting on the bottom. In an older tank, that bottom layer is sludge: years of rust flakes, condensation water, and broken-down oil. Stir it up and it heads straight for your filter and nozzle.

Kevin Melanson in Salisbury learned that the expensive way. He topped up his 24-year-old tank with diesel during a cold stretch, and within a day the burner starved and locked out. The service call to swap the clogged filter and nozzle ran $190. While he was under there, the technician spotted weeping rust along the tank's bottom seam. The tank was rotting from the inside out, and the stirred-up sludge was the tip-off. A new tank, installed and inspected, came to about $3,100. Not fun, but a fraction of what an oil spill cleanup would have cost him.

Two things feed that bottom layer. The first is [sludge buildup](/blog/oil-tank-sludge/), which every steel tank grows with age. The second is [water in the tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/), mostly from condensation, which sits under the oil and quietly rusts the steel where you can't see it. Fuel type matters far less than tank condition. A clean, sound tank shrugs off a weekend of diesel. A corroded 25-year-old tank can be pushed over the edge by any disturbance.

## Which Fuel Should You Actually Buy?

For almost everyone reading this, the answer is boring: keep buying furnace oil from your regular supplier. It's the right spec for your furnace, it's the cheapest of the three, and delivery beats hauling jerry cans in a snowstorm every time.

Use the others for what they're good at. Stove oil belongs in the older stoves and cold outdoor setups it was made for, as long as you can still get it. Diesel is your emergency bridge fuel, nothing more. Buy what gets you to the next delivery, then go back to furnace oil.

And whichever fuel you burn, remember that the tank is the part that can actually hurt you financially. Fuel mistakes cost hundreds. Tank failures cost tens of thousands once oil hits a basement floor or the ground outside. Keep the tank young, keep it inspected, and the fuel question stays a small one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I put diesel in my oil tank in an emergency?

Yes. Clear diesel from any gas station will run a standard oil furnace or boiler safely for days or even weeks. Buy winter diesel in proper fuel containers, pour it into the outside fill pipe, wait 15 to 30 minutes for sediment to settle, and press the burner reset once. If it won't fire after one reset, call a technician instead of hitting reset again.

### Is furnace oil just dyed diesel?

Very nearly. Both are No. 2 middle distillates, and in much of Canada they now come from the same low-sulphur supply. The main differences are the red dye, the tax treatment, and engine-focused additives in diesel that a furnace doesn't need. That's why diesel works fine in a furnace, while dyed furnace oil is illegal on the road.

### Why does stove oil cost more than furnace oil?

Stove oil is a lighter No. 1 cut in the kerosene family, and demand for it has shrunk to a niche, so fewer suppliers carry it and margins are higher. Around Southeast New Brunswick it commonly runs 10 to 25 cents per litre above furnace oil. If only one supplier in your area still delivers it, expect to pay for that scarcity.

### Will burning diesel damage my furnace or my tank?

The fuel itself won't harm a properly working burner over a short stretch. The risk is in the tank: pouring cans of fuel into an older tank stirs up sludge and water that can clog your filter and nozzle, and heavy sediment is often a sign the tank itself is corroding. If a top-up suddenly leads to burner lockouts, get the filter changed and have the tank inspected while the technician is there.
