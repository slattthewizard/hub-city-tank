# Oil Tank Fill Pipe and Vent Pipe: What the Sizes and Rules Mean

Every residential oil tank has two pipes sticking out of the top: a fill pipe the delivery driver connects to, and a vent pipe that lets air escape while oil goes in. They look like simple plumbing, but both have set materials, minimum sizes, and a slope they have to hold, and both get replaced more often at a tank swap than most homeowners expect.

## What the Fill Pipe and Vent Pipe Do

The fill pipe is the capped pipe the driver hooks the delivery hose to. Oil is pumped down it from the truck, so it has to be sound, well supported and pitched toward the tank.

The vent pipe runs from the top of the tank to the outside and does the opposite job: it lets the air the incoming oil is displacing get out. Without it, the tank would behave like a sealed jug and the driver couldn't fill it at all. The vent also carries the vent alarm (the whistle that tells the driver when the tank is nearly full), which we cover in detail in our [oil tank vent whistle guide](/blog/oil-tank-vent-whistle/), and it's the part most likely to ice over in a Southeast New Brunswick winter. This article is about the pipes themselves: what they're made of, how big they need to be, and why they usually come out with the old tank.

## Fill and Vent Pipe Size: What the Code and the Manufacturers Require

There's a floor set by code, and then there's what an individual tank maker requires for its own tank, and the two aren't always the same number.

For the vent pipe, the [installation manual for the Granby 2-in-1 fuel oil storage tank](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0018_Ga-Granby-2-in-1-Installer-Instructions-1.pdf), sold in Canada and installed to CSA B139, tells the installer to connect the vent pipe in accordance with local codes and "do not reduce vent capacity below 1-1/4 in NPS." That 1-1/4 inch figure is also the minimum vent diameter NFPA 31 allows, according to the [installation manual for Roth's EcoDWT tank](https://www.roth-america.com/app/uploads/2021/02/DWT_Installation_Manual_2023.pdf), which says the tank meets both NFPA 31 and CSA B139. NFPA 31 is the American standard; in Canada the code is CSA B139. Roth goes further for its own tank, requiring a 1-1/2 inch minimum vent pipe for its complete length to the outside termination, and it requires the vent pipe to be the same diameter as the fill pipe or larger. That last rule is Roth's, for its tank, though it's a sensible thing to ask about on any install.

Fill pipe diameter is looser at the code level, at least in NFPA 31. Roth's manual notes that NFPA 31 doesn't specifically address fill pipe diameter for above-ground tanks under 660 US gallons, so Roth sets its own floor of 1-1/2 inch NPT for its tank, and says using anything smaller for the fill or vent voids its warranty. The manual also flags something homeowners rarely think about: there are 4.35 US gallons (about 16 litres) of oil in 25 feet of 2-inch fill pipe, and that oil still has to drain into the tank after the vent whistle stops. Roth says its tank should have no more than 25 feet of fill piping, because longer runs raise the chance of an overfill.

The takeaway for a Moncton-area homeowner isn't the exact number so much as the principle: a fill or vent pipe that's been narrowed down with reducing fittings, or pieced together from whatever was on the truck, can be technically undersized even if it looks fine. That's a detail worth asking about on any written estimate.

## Material and Slope: The Rules Behind Every Legal Fill and Vent Line

Two rules matter as much as diameter.

The first is material. Roth's installation manual, citing NFPA 31 and CSA B139, says all fill and vent piping for its tank shall be wrought (black) iron or steel, that copper piping is not permitted, and that copper fill or vent piping on a tank being replaced has to be replaced too. A [Nova Scotia fuel safety bulletin on the 2009 edition of CSA B139](https://novascotia.ca/lae/fuelsafety/docs/FSB0906.pdf) says the same for technicians in that province: fill and vent pipes are part of the tank storage system and must meet the code of the day, fill pipes must be steel (black iron) or galvanized, and no cast 90-degree elbows are allowed. Prince Edward Island's [home heat tanks FAQ](https://www.princeedwardisland.ca/sites/default/files/publications/home_heat_tanks_-_frequently_asked_questions.pdf) is blunter still for PEI: vent and fill pipes must be steel or galvanized, and copper vent and fill pipes can't be used on any installation or replacement there after April 2000. If your house still has copper fill or vent lines, expect an installer to replace them with the tank.

The second rule is slope. Fill and vent piping has to drain back toward the tank rather than sitting flat or sagging, so oil (and any water that gets in) runs down into the tank instead of pooling in a low spot in the pipe. The Nova Scotia bulletin says the fill and vent must drain 1/4 inch per foot, without sags or traps where liquid can collect. It notes the rule had applied to vent pipes for some time and that the 2009 edition extended it to fill pipes. A pipe that dips even slightly between the tank and the outside wall becomes a place for water and sediment to sit, quietly feeding corrosion from the inside.

One more detail worth knowing if a quote is for a Roth tank: Roth requires swing joints (a pair of elbows that lets the pipe pivot slightly) on both the fill and vent pipes between the tank and the wall. Its tank's top drops a little as the sides expand during a fill, and a rigid run would open a gap at the fitting that lets oil fumes seep into the room. The swing joints also let the pipes keep their pitch back toward the tank.

## Why Fill and Vent Lines Almost Always Get Replaced With the Tank

If your estimate for a new tank includes new fill and vent piping, that isn't an upsell. The Nova Scotia bulletin says the chance of old fill and vent pipes meeting every current requirement is very slim and it's better to count on replacing them. There are three plain reasons.

The first is the material rule above. Copper fill and vent lines from a previous era don't meet the requirements described above, and Roth's manual, for one, says they have to be replaced along with the tank.

The second is fit and connection. A new tank comes with its own adaptors and gaskets, and makers like Granby and Roth spell out how the pipe must line up with them. Forcing old piping onto a new tank's ports risks a gasket that never seats properly, which is a slow, hard-to-find leak point rather than a dramatic one.

The third is condition. Old black iron pipe corrodes from the inside, especially at low spots where water has been sitting for years, which is exactly the kind of sag the slope rule exists to prevent. By the time a tank is old enough to replace, the piping attached to it has usually spent just as many winters outside.

## Signs of a Leaking Fill Pipe Joint

A fill pipe doesn't have to fail dramatically to be a problem. The joint where the pipe meets the tank, and the joint where the cap meets the pipe, are the two spots worth checking after every delivery.

Watch for a rust-coloured streak running down from the fill cap or the connection point, a persistent oil smell right at the fill pipe rather than elsewhere around the tank, or a damp or discoloured patch on the foundation or siding below where the pipe passes through the wall. None of these are urgent on their own, but they're worth a look before the next fill, not after. If the smell is strong, spreading, or you can see oil rather than just smell it, treat it as more than a maintenance item: our guide to [oil smell in the basement](/blog/oil-smell-in-basement/) walks through when a smell means stop and call rather than wait and watch.

## Ice, Snow, and a Blocked Vent

A vent pipe with the right diameter and slope still fails in a Maritime winter if it's buried in a snowbank or capped in ice. A blocked vent silences the whistle, which leaves the driver with no signal that the tank is nearly full. Granby's 2-in-1 instructions list a blocked vent among the things to check at least once a year, by looking at the pipe and cap outside. We've covered the winter side of this in detail in our [snow and ice protection guide](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/), so we won't repeat it here beyond the short version: brush snow off the vent cap by hand after storms, never chip at ice with a metal tool, and never pour hot water on a frozen pipe.

## What a Properly Piped Tank Looks Like When the Job Is Done

On a finished installation, the fill pipe is steel or galvanized, sized to at least the manufacturer's minimum for that tank, capped tight, and pitched back toward the tank the whole way. The vent pipe is at least the size the tank maker calls for, fitted with a screened cap and a working vent alarm, and the piping is held by hangers rather than resting its weight on the tank. Granby's 2-in-1 instructions say the tank isn't designed to carry the weight of the piping. Both pipes terminate outside with proper fittings, clear of snow slide zones and reachable without anyone wading through a snowbank.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What size should an oil tank fill pipe be?

There's no single number that applies everywhere. Roth's manual says the US NFPA 31 standard doesn't specifically address fill pipe size for above-ground tanks under 660 US gallons, but tank makers set their own: Roth requires 1-1/2 inch NPT pipe for its EcoDWT tank's full run. The safest approach is to match whatever the specific tank's installation instructions and the current CSA B139 call for, not a generic rule of thumb.

### What size should the vent pipe be?

Granby's installer instructions for its 2-in-1 tank say not to reduce vent capacity below 1-1/4 inch NPS, which matches the minimum Roth reports for the US NFPA 31 standard. Roth requires 1-1/2 inch for its own tank, and requires the vent to be the same diameter as the fill pipe or larger. Follow the instructions for the tank being installed.

### Can fill and vent pipes be copper?

Not on a new installation, going by every source we checked. Roth's manual, citing NFPA 31 and CSA B139, says copper fill and vent piping is not permitted. Nova Scotia's bulletin on the 2009 code says fill pipes must be black iron or galvanized, and PEI bans copper vent and fill pipes on installations or replacements after April 2000. If your tank has copper fill or vent lines, plan on replacing them with the tank.

### Do fill and vent pipes need to be replaced when the tank is?

Usually, yes. Nova Scotia's fuel safety bulletin says old fill and vent pipes are very unlikely to meet every current requirement and it's better to count on replacing them. Add fittings matched to the new tank and piping as weathered as the tank it served, and new piping is the normal choice. Ask that it be listed on your written estimate.

### Why does the area around my fill pipe smell like oil after a delivery?

A splash at the cap during filling is common and usually fades within a day. A smell that lingers, gets stronger, or comes with a visible stain or wet patch points to a joint that isn't sealing, whether at the cap, the tank connection, or a low spot in the pipe holding water and residue. That's worth a call rather than a wait, especially if you can see oil and not just smell it.
