# Dual Fuel Heat Pump and Oil Furnace Backup: What It Means for Your Tank

A dual fuel heat pump setup is an air-source heat pump, often a cold climate model, paired with your existing oil furnace, with controls that switch between the two depending on outdoor temperature and, on some systems, cost. The heat pump carries most of the season. The furnace, and the tank behind it, only wake up for the coldest stretches of a Southeast New Brunswick winter.

That sounds like the furnace side of the house gets easier. In some ways it does. But an oil tank that only feeds the burner a few weeks a year does not stop being a tank. It still sits outside or in the basement, it still ages by the calendar, and it still needs to pass muster if your insurer or a home inspector ever looks at it. ## How a Dual Fuel System Decides When to Switch

A dual fuel, or hybrid, heating system runs the heat pump and the furnace one at a time. Natural Resources Canada's guide to [heating and cooling with a heat pump](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/energy-star/heating-cooling-heat-pump) is blunt about it: with a furnace or boiler as the backup, either the heat pump runs or the oil or gas furnace runs, never both together.

The same guide lists three temperatures at which an air-source heat pump can hand off, and says your installation contractor sets which one your system uses:

- **Thermal balance point**: the outdoor temperature below which the heat pump does not have enough capacity to meet the house's heating needs on its own.
- **Economic balance point**: the temperature below which, given the price of electricity against the other fuel, running the backup system is the cheaper choice.
- **Cut-off temperature**: the heat pump's minimum operating temperature, below which it stops heating at all.

Hybrid systems, the guide says, switch between the heat pump and the backup according to the thermal or economic balance point. So the changeover is a setting, not a fixed number, and two houses on the same street can switch at different temperatures.

Natural Resources Canada's research team has also written about [hybrid home heating systems](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/stories/simply-science/future-home-heating-hybrid-home-heating-systems-offer-energy-savings-reduce-ghg-emissions), noting that air-source heat pumps "can be retrofit and work with any fossil fuel furnace" running on natural gas, propane or oil. The test homes in that article used smart controls that switch to whichever mode is cheapest at the time, weighing fuel and electricity prices, time of use and outdoor temperature.

## Cold Climate Heat Pumps: How Far They Carry the Load

"Cold climate heat pump" describes a real design, not just a sales line. Natural Resources Canada's guide says these units combine variable capacity compressors with improved heat exchangers and controls to get more heating capacity out of colder air while staying efficient in milder weather.

Cold still costs them. The same guide gives the coefficient of performance (units of heat delivered per unit of electricity) of air-source heat pumps as typically 2.0 to 5.4 at 8 degrees, falling to 1.1 to 3.7 at minus 8. It also says the vast majority of air-source heat pumps have a minimum operating temperature, which for newer models ranges from minus 15 to minus 25. Below that, a supplemental system has to carry the heat.

That is the gap a dual fuel setup is built for. Through a Southeast New Brunswick winter, the heat pump can do most of the work in the milder months and on the shoulder days, and the oil furnace takes the house through the coldest stretches, when the outdoor temperature drops below the balance point or cut-off your contractor set.

Where a home lands on that spectrum depends on insulation, how the heat pump was sized, and how the house is laid out, which is why a proper heat load calculation from your HVAC contractor matters more than the number on the brochure. ## What Backup Duty Does to Your Oil Tank

Here is the part that gets missed. When a furnace goes from running most of a five-month heating season to running for a few cold snaps, the tank behind it changes too, just not in a good way on every count.

**Oil moves through the tank much more slowly.** A tank that used to draw down and refill several times a winter might now sit at the same level for months. That is good for your fuel bill and bad for the inside of the tank. A half-full tank that never gets used or topped up gives humid air more room to condense on the bare steel above the oil line, and that water sinks to the bottom, feeds corrosion, and builds sludge over time. Our post on [keeping an oil tank full over the summer](/blog/keep-oil-tank-full-summer/) covers the same mechanism, and it applies just as much to a backup tank sitting mostly idle through a mild winter as it does to a tank sitting empty all summer. Our guide to [water in your oil tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/) covers what that water does once it is in there and how to know if it has become a problem.

**The tank still ages on the calendar, not on the fuel gauge.** Steel corrodes from the inside whether the furnace runs once a week or once a winter. Our guide to [how long oil tanks last](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) has the typical service life ranges by type and setting, and none of those ranges assume light backup use buys you extra years. If anything, a tank that sits mostly full and mostly still can hide a slow problem longer, since nobody is watching the gauge move or listening for the vent whistle during a fill.

**Your insurer does not care that the furnace barely runs.** A 22-year-old steel tank in a basement is a 22-year-old steel tank in a basement, whether it burns 2,000 litres a year or 300. Insurers ask about tank age and condition, not about how many cold days the oil side covered. Our post on [oil tank age and insurance limits](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) breaks down what insurers typically look for. If your tank is already near the end of its useful life, adding a heat pump does not change that clock, and it does not make sense to lock in a shaky tank behind a new heat pump installation just because the furnace will barely run.

The short version: dual fuel heating is genuinely good for your oil bill and your comfort on the coldest nights. It is not a reason to stop checking on the tank.

## Full-Time Oil vs Dual Fuel Backup: What Changes for the Tank

| | Full-time oil furnace | Dual fuel backup |
|---|---|---|
| How often the tank draws down | Several times over a heating season | Occasionally, on the coldest stretches |
| Condensation and water risk | Present, especially in summer | Present, and can build up over a longer idle stretch |
| Tank aging | By calendar year | Still by calendar year |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a dual fuel heat pump?

It is an air-source heat pump, often a cold climate model, installed alongside an existing furnace, with controls that switch the house between the two rather than running them together. The heat pump handles most of the heating season, and the furnace takes over below a set outdoor temperature or when running it costs less than running the heat pump. Only one system heats the house at a time.

### Does adding a heat pump mean I can get rid of my oil tank?

Not automatically. If you are keeping the furnace as backup, the tank has to stay in place and stay insurable, since it may only be a few weeks of hard use away at any point. Some homeowners do remove oil entirely and go all electric, but that is a separate decision from adding a heat pump for most of the season.

### Will my oil tank last longer if a heat pump is doing most of the work?

Not in any way you can count on. A tank corrodes from the inside based on age, moisture and how it sits, not strictly on how many litres pass through it in a season. A tank that runs light duty for years can still reach the end of its service life on schedule, sometimes with less warning because nobody is watching it as closely.

### What outdoor temperature makes the system switch to the oil furnace?

There is no single number. Natural Resources Canada says the switch can be set at the thermal balance point (where the heat pump can no longer keep up), the economic balance point (where the backup becomes cheaper to run) or the heat pump's cut-off temperature, which for newer models ranges from minus 15 to minus 25. Your HVAC contractor sets it during installation, based on the unit and the house.

### Do insurers care about an oil tank that is only used a few weeks a year?

Insurers generally ask about the tank's age, type and condition rather than how much oil moved through it that year. A backup tank gets the same scrutiny at renewal as a full-time one. If your tank is old enough to draw questions, light use is unlikely to change the answer.

### Can I add a heat pump if my oil tank is already old?

Yes, but it is worth having the tank checked first rather than building a heat pump installation around a tank that might need replacing soon anyway. A free written estimate tells you where the tank stands, so you can decide whether to replace it before, during or shortly after the heat pump goes in.
