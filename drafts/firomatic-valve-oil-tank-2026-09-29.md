# Firomatic Valve, Tank Valve, and Oil Safety Valve: What Each One Protects You From

Firomatic is a brand name. It's Beckett Corporation's line of fusible fire safety valves: brass valves in the oil line that you can open and close by hand, but that also close themselves if a fire heats them past a set temperature, cutting off the oil before it can feed the flames. On a lot of oil systems, the valve you turn at the bottom of the tank is one of these. Other makers sell fusible valves too, and plenty of older tanks have a plain manual valve instead.

There's also a third device that gets mixed up with both: the oil safety valve (often shortened to OSV), which stops a tank from draining through a broken line. Knowing which one you have, and where it sits, matters the day you need to shut the oil off in a hurry, or when an insurer's form asks about valves.

If you're not sure what's on your tank, or one of your valves is weeping, call [(506) 806-0900](tel:+15068060900) to have someone look at it. This guide walks through each valve in plain terms.

## Three Kinds of Valve, Three Jobs

Not every system has all of these. Here's the short version before the detail:

| Valve | Where it usually sits | How it closes |
|---|---|---|
| Fusible tank valve (for example a Firomatic) | At the tank outlet, where the oil line leaves the tank | By hand with the handwheel, or on its own when heat melts the fusible element |
| Plain manual shut-off valve | At the tank outlet on some older systems | By hand only |
| Fusible burner or inline valve | In the line, at or near the burner inlet | On its own in heat, and by hand |
| Oil safety valve (OSV) | In the supply line, placed per the maker's instructions | Only opens while the burner's pump is pulling oil |

The first three do the same basic job, stopping oil at a point in the line. The difference is whether they can react to a fire when nobody's home. The OSV does a different job entirely.

## The Valve at the Tank Outlet: Your First Move by Hand

Whatever kind it is, the valve right where the line leaves the tank is the one you'll reach for in an emergency. It's usually a small round handwheel or a lever. Beckett describes its Firomatic [fusible tank valves](https://www.beckettcorp.com/product/firomatic-fire-safety-fusible-tank-valves/) as designed to be installed at the outlet of most oil tanks, in straight and 90 degree versions, so if your tank has a brass valve with a round handwheel at the outlet, there's a fair chance it's a fusible valve rather than a plain tap.

If you ever spot a drip downstream of the tank, at the filter, a fitting or the line, closing this valve is usually the first thing to try. Most turn clockwise to shut, but look at the handle rather than assuming, and never force one that won't budge. A valve that has sat untouched for fifteen years can seize, and cranking on a seized fitting with a wrench is how a slow drip becomes a flood. If yours won't turn, stop there and get help rather than fighting it.

A plain manual valve only works when a person turns it. It does nothing during a fire or an unattended leak. That's the gap the other two devices fill.

## How a Fusible Valve Works

Firomatic valves are, in Beckett's words, designed to automatically close and shut off fuel flow in a fire or other high-temperature condition. Inside, a heat-sensitive element holds the valve open while it's intact. When it reaches its rated temperature it gives way and the valve closes. Beckett lists its valves at two ratings, 165°F (about 74°C) and 200°F (about 93°C), and it also sells replacement handwheels at those same two ratings, which tells you where the heat-sensitive part lives on these valves.

Firomatic valves come in several forms. Beckett's range includes the tank valves above, inline valves that fit into the run of the oil line, and burner (angle) valves that it describes as additional protection at the burner inlet. A system can have a fusible valve at the tank and another at the burner.

Beckett's product pages say these valves are required in residential and commercial installations covered by CSA B139 Series:19 in Canada (and by NFPA 31 in the United States). CSA B139 is the national installation code for oil-burning equipment. Series:19 was its 10th edition; the current edition is CSA B139 Series:24, so check what applies to your installation with the technician who services it. [CSA Group's summary of the 10th edition changes](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/) also notes that the external parts of the body of any fuel-containing device, component or accessory installed in an oil line should have a melting point of no less than 538°C. That's a separate point from the fusible element: the element is meant to give way at a low temperature, while the valve body itself isn't.

Once a fusible valve has closed from heat, whether from a real fire or from something else nearby getting too hot, it stays closed. The heat-sensitive part has done its job and has to be replaced, or the whole valve does, before the system runs again. That's a job for an oil burner technician, not a homeowner, since it means working on the fuel line. It's also worth finding out why it tripped before anyone puts a new one on.

## The Oil Safety Valve: Stopping a Line From Draining Itself

Heating oil doesn't need a pump running to move. If your tank sits higher than the point where a line breaks, gravity alone will push oil out of a cracked fitting for as long as the tank has fuel in it. An outdoor tank at ground level feeding a basement burner is the classic case, since the whole tank sits above the line.

An oil safety valve is built to stop that. The common type is vacuum-operated. Beckett's page for the [Suntec PRV oil line safety valve](https://www.beckettcorp.com/product/suntec-prv-oil-line-safety-valve/) says it isolates the burner components from fuel supply line pressure and requires pump vacuum to draw oil from the tank. In plain terms, oil only gets through while the burner's pump is actively pulling it. If the line downstream breaks, there's no vacuum, the valve stays shut, and the tank's oil stays in the tank instead of running out by gravity or siphon.

An OSV isn't a fire valve and it isn't something you turn by hand in an emergency. Where it goes in the line depends on the model and the maker's instructions, which is the installer's call. A system can have an OSV and a fusible valve at the tank, and many do.

## What an Insurer's Form Asks About Valves

If your insurer or broker has sent you a tank questionnaire, the national [Fuel Oil Tank Questionnaire](https://csio.com/fuel-oil-tank-questionnaire) published by CSIO is the one many use. It asks whether there's a shut-off valve on the fuel supply line at the furnace burner, whether the tank, fuel line and valve are protected from impact (it gives vehicles and ice as examples), and it lists an anti-siphon device among the safety features to tick. It also asks where the outlet is on the tank and where the filter sits.

The form doesn't set the rules. Your insurer does. But if you can't answer the valve questions, that's a good reason to have someone look at the setup and tell you what's there before you fill it in.

## Signs a Valve Is Leaking or Failing

A valve problem rarely announces itself with a flood. It usually shows up as one of these, and any of them is worth a call rather than a wait:

- **Weeping at the stem or body.** A small wet ring where the handle passes into the valve usually means a worn stem seal, not a failing tank.
- **A valve that won't fully close.** If oil keeps trickling past a shut valve, the seat inside is worn or something is caught in it.
- **A valve that won't turn at all.** Years of sitting untouched can seize the stem solid, which is a problem the day you need it.
- **Corrosion on the brass body or fittings.** Salt air along the Northumberland Strait is hard on exposed metal, and outdoor tanks around [Shediac](/oil-tank-service-shediac/) tend to show it sooner than tanks inland.
- **A fusible valve that has closed with no obvious fire.** A space heater parked too close or another hot spot nearby can be enough. Don't just have the part swapped and move on without finding out what got it that hot.

A weeping or worn valve is usually its own small repair, like the other [oil tank parts](/blog/oil-tank-parts-explained/) around the tank and line. It's a different problem from a leak in the tank body or in the [oil line running to the burner](/blog/oil-line-replacement/), so have someone confirm which part is at fault rather than guessing.

## What to Do If a Valve Is Actively Leaking

If oil is dripping from a valve or fitting right now, close the valve at the tank outlet if it turns freely, put a container under the drip, and switch the furnace off at its emergency switch. If oil has reached the ground, a drain or water, or you have reason to believe it has, New Brunswick expects it to be reported. Call 911 first if there's any fire or explosion risk. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a Firomatic valve?

Firomatic is Beckett Corporation's brand of fusible fire safety valve for oil lines. You can open and close it by hand, and it also closes on its own when a heat-sensitive element reaches its rated temperature, 165°F or 200°F depending on the model. Beckett makes tank, inline and burner versions. Other manufacturers make fusible valves that do the same job.

### Where is the fusible valve on an oil tank system?

Often right at the tank outlet, where the oil line leaves the tank: Beckett designs its Firomatic tank valves to be installed there. Some systems also have a fusible valve at or near the burner inlet, which Beckett describes as additional protection. Older systems may have only a plain manual valve at the tank.

### What is the difference between a fusible valve and an oil safety valve?

A fusible valve stops oil when heat melts its fusible element, and you can also close it by hand. An oil safety valve is usually vacuum-operated: it only lets oil through while the burner's pump is pulling, so a broken line downstream can't drain the tank by gravity. They protect against different failures, and a system can have both.

### How do I know if a valve on my oil tank is leaking?

Look for a wet ring or drip at the stem or body, a valve that won't fully close, or a handle that won't turn at all. Brass and steel fittings on outdoor tanks near the coast also corrode sooner than inland ones. Any of these is worth having looked at rather than guessed at.

### Can I close the tank valve myself in an emergency?

Yes, if it turns freely. Most close clockwise, but check the handle rather than assuming. Don't force a seized valve or put a wrench on it, since an old fitting can snap. If it won't move, catch the drip, switch the furnace off at its emergency switch and get help.

### Does New Brunswick require a fusible valve on an oil heating system?

Beckett states that its fusible valves are required in installations covered by CSA B139 Series:19, the 10th edition of the national installation code for oil-burning equipment. The current edition is Series:24. What an existing system needs depends on when it was installed and what your insurer asks, so have an oil burner technician check yours.
