# Do You Need a Permit to Remove an Oil Tank in New Brunswick?

Short answer: we couldn't find a municipal permit in Moncton, Dieppe or Riverview that names oil tank removal on its own, and New Brunswick's provincial approval and licensing rules for petroleum storage start at a total of 2,000 litres on a property, well above a single typical home tank. That is not the same as "no permit is needed." Permit lists give examples, not every case, and a job that involves digging, plumbing changes or two tanks can land in a different category. The only safe answer for your house comes from your municipality, before the work starts.

This article lays out exactly which sources we checked, what each one says, and the questions worth asking. If you'd rather talk it through for your own tank, call [(506) 806-0900](tel:+15068060900).

## The Sources We Checked

For a question like this, the honest approach is to say where we looked rather than guess from the absence of a form. We checked four places in September 2026:

1. **The province.** The Government of New Brunswick's reference page on [petroleum storage tanks](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg/environment/content/land_waste/content/reference_manual/petroleum_storagetanks.html), and the text of the Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation (NB Reg 87-97) under the Clean Environment Act.
2. **Moncton.** The City of Moncton's [building and development permits page](https://www.moncton.ca/en/building-permits).
3. **Dieppe.** The City of Dieppe's [building permits page](https://www.dieppe.ca/en/affaires-et-developpement/amenagement-et-construction/permis-de-construction/).
4. **Riverview.** The Town of Riverview's [building permits page](https://www.townofriverview.ca/town-hall/permits-licenses-and-service-requests/building-permits).

One note on the provincial page: gnb.ca blocked our fetch tools, so what we say about it comes from the search listing that shows that exact page and its text, checked against the regulation itself. Rules and web pages change, so treat everything below as a starting point for your own call to the city or town, not a final ruling.

## What Moncton, Dieppe and Riverview Say

**Moncton** lists the projects that need a building or development permit: new construction, additions, changes in building use, renovations, plumbing work, fences, pools and swim spas, accessory buildings like sheds and garages, and some decks. Oil tanks and heating equipment aren't named either way. The page also says, in effect, that if you're not sure whether your project needs a permit, you should contact the office. That's the right move. The number listed there is 506-856-4375.

**Dieppe** gives a list of examples of work that needs a permit from its Planning and Development Department. Two items on it matter for tank jobs: "excavation or backfill work" and "new plumbing systems or changes to existing ones." Demolition and new or changed electrical systems are on the list too. A plain swap of an above ground tank in a basement doesn't obviously fit any of those. A buried tank dig, on the other hand, is excavation and backfill by any plain reading, so if you're in Dieppe and the tank is underground, call the department before anyone brings a machine.

**Riverview** says permits are needed for construction, renovation and demolition activity, including new buildings, significant changes of use and interior alterations, and it lists separate plumbing permits. Riverview's page routes permit questions to Plan360. Again, tanks aren't named either way.

So across all three, nobody lists "oil tank removal" as a stand-alone permit. What they do list (plumbing changes, excavation, renovation) can overlap with a tank job depending on how it's done. A straight removal of an above ground tank, with the oil line capped at the tank location, is the simplest case. Moving the tank to a new spot, running new line through a wall or slab, or digging one up is where you should ask first.

## The Province: 2,000 Litres Is the Line

New Brunswick's rules for petroleum storage sit in NB Reg 87-97. As we read it, the regulation applies broadly to storage tank systems, including their removal and disposal. The paperwork parts, though, are tied to size:

- **Site and environmental approvals.** Section 23 says no one may install, alter, excavate, disassemble, destroy or dispose of a system with a total capacity of 2,000 litres or more (the threshold is lower for marinas) without a site approval and an environmental approval from the Minister.
- **Who does the work.** For that same approval-level work, the owner has to make sure it's done or supervised by an employee of a registered business who holds a provincial installer's licence.
- **Site licences.** Owners of systems at 2,000 litres or more need a provincial licence, and the regulation includes a residential licence class for heating systems in one- or two-unit homes at that size.

The province's reference page describes the same structure: a storage site of 2,000 litres or more has to be registered, and approval is needed before a system that size is installed.

Here's the detail that trips people up. "Total capacity" is not one tank. The regulation adds up all the storage tanks on the same parcel of land. Most single home tanks in this area are the 910 litre size, with 1,000 and 1,150 litre tanks also common, so one tank on its own is well under 2,000 litres. Two 1,150 litre tanks on one lot, though, add up to 2,300 litres. If your property has a second tank, a spare nobody ever pulled, or a buried tank from an older setup, add them together before assuming the small-system rules apply, and ask the province if you're close.

For a single tank under 2,000 litres, we did not find a requirement in the regulation that the removal be done by a provincially licensed installer. We also did not find anything saying it doesn't need one, and the province isn't the only party with a say: your insurer and your buyer's lawyer have their own expectations. Hiring someone who can show you a provincial installer's licence and give you proper paperwork is the sensible choice either way. If you want a firm answer on licensing for your tank, the Department of Environment and Local Government is the body to ask.

## When the Code Pushes a Tank Out

Separate from permits, there's the national code. CSA B139 is the installation code for oil-burning equipment. According to [CSA Group's summary of the 10th edition](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/) (the 2019 code; a newer edition has since been published), that edition added "new requirements for the removal of an aboveground or underground tank system that has not been used for more than three years."

The summary doesn't spell out every detail, and how the code applies to an old tank in an existing house depends on which edition your jurisdiction enforces and on who is asking (an inspector, an insurer, a buyer's home inspector). What it tells you in plain terms is that the code's direction is clear: a tank system that has sat unused for more than three years is one the code expects to see dealt with, not left in place indefinitely.

That fits a common local situation. A house switched to a heat pump or electric heat years ago and the old tank is still sitting in the basement or beside the house, disconnected and half full. If that's yours, it's worth planning its removal rather than waiting for a sale or an insurer to force the question. Buried tanks sometimes have a different route, a documented closure in place, and we cover when that is and isn't an option in our post on [abandoning an oil tank in place](/blog/abandon-oil-tank-in-place/).

## The Paperwork That Matters in Practice

Whatever the permit answer turns out to be, the documents you keep after a removal matter more to most homeowners than a permit would.

**A disposal receipt.** This is the most useful piece of paper you can hold onto. It shows a buyer's lawyer, a future insurer or your own broker that the tank was emptied, disconnected and taken away by someone accountable for it. Our post on [oil tank disposal](/blog/oil-tank-disposal/) walks through what happens to the tank, the leftover oil and the sludge, none of which should ever go in the garbage or down a drain.

**Your insurer's letter, if there was one.** If you're removing a tank because your insurer asked, keep a copy of the letter with the receipt, and tell your insurer in writing when the tank is gone.

**Anything that shows a buried tank's condition.** For a buried removal, keep any soil test results and photos. They answer the first question a buyer's side will ask.

**Answers for a sale.** A buyer's inspector who spots a capped fill pipe on the siding or a stand mark on the basement floor will ask what happened to the tank. The receipt answers that in one line.

If the tank has already leaked, the permit question stops being the priority. New Brunswick expects a release of a contaminant to be reported, and we cover who to call and in what order in our post on [reporting an oil spill in New Brunswick](/blog/report-oil-spill-nb/).

## Before You Book the Removal

A short checklist for Greater Moncton homeowners:

- Count every tank on the property, above ground and buried, and add up their sizes.
- If anything goes beyond pulling one above ground tank and capping the line (digging, moving, new line through a wall), call your municipality first. In Dieppe, excavation and backfill are on the permit list.
- Ask whoever does the job what paperwork you'll get at the end, and make sure a disposal receipt is part of it.
- If you're on a deadline from an insurer or a sale, start early.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a permit to remove an above ground oil tank in Moncton, Dieppe or Riverview?

We didn't find tank removal named as a permit on any of the three municipal permit pages. That doesn't settle it for your house. Those pages list examples, and plumbing changes, renovations and excavation can each need a permit. Call your city or town before the work starts and describe the job exactly. Moncton's permit page lists 506-856-4375.

### Does New Brunswick require a licence to remove an oil tank?

For systems with a total capacity of 2,000 litres or more on a property, the regulation requires provincial approvals and work done or supervised by a licensed installer working for a registered business. For a single home tank under that size, we didn't find a licensing requirement in the regulation either way, so ask the Department of Environment and Local Government if it matters for your job.

### My tank hasn't been used in years. Does it have to come out?

CSA Group's summary of the 10th edition of CSA B139 says it added requirements for removing tank systems that haven't been used for more than three years. How that is applied to your house depends on which code edition is enforced and on your insurer, but a tank idle that long is one to plan to remove.

### What paperwork should I keep after a tank is removed?

Keep the disposal receipt, any insurer letter that prompted the removal, and for a buried tank, any soil test results and photos. Those are what an insurer, a buyer's lawyer or a home inspector will ask for to confirm the tank was emptied, disconnected and disposed of properly rather than left behind or hauled off with no record.

### Is a buried tank handled differently for permits?

It can be. Digging it up is excavation and backfill, which is on Dieppe's permit list, and any municipality may treat the dig differently from an indoor swap. A buried tank also brings soil questions if contamination is found. Call your municipality before the work, and see our post on abandoning a tank in place for the closure option.
