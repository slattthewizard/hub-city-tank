# Protecting an Outdoor Oil Tank From Cars and Snowplows: Posts, Barriers, or a Better Spot

If your outdoor oil tank sits next to a driveway, a parking pad or the corner where the plow turns, it needs protection from vehicles, or it needs to move. A steel tank takes a scrape better than most people expect. A bent leg, a sheared gauge or a snapped fill pipe is another story, and a fibreglass tank can crack from a hard point impact. Posts or a barrier set in front of the tank cover most of that risk. If the spot is the problem, the cheapest time to fix it is when the tank is being replaced anyway.

This guide covers where the hits come from, what manufacturers and government guides say about protection, how to set up a barrier that doesn't get in the way of the oil truck, and how to decide between protecting the tank where it sits and putting the new one somewhere safer. ## Where the hits actually come from

It is rarely a dramatic collision. In Southeast New Brunswick, the usual threats to an outdoor tank near a driveway are ordinary winter and yard-work habits:

- **The plow or snowblower.** A blade pushes a ridge of packed snow and ice against whatever is at the edge of the lane. A snowblower chute can throw ice chunks sideways at the tank. Neither driver may see a tank that is buried in a drift.
- **Reversing.** A bumper backing into a narrow parking spot is enough to bend a leg or crush a gauge.
- **Lawn tractors and trailers.** Summer isn't risk-free, especially with a tank beside a shed or a gate.
- **Falling roof snow.** Not a vehicle, but it often arrives at the same time and place. Our guide to [protecting your oil tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/) covers that side.

What breaks first is usually not the tank shell. It is the small parts: the gauge, the filter, the fill and vent pipes, and the oil line where it leaves the tank. A tank that gets shoved a few centimetres can pull on a rigid oil line until a fitting weeps, and that kind of leak can sit unnoticed under a snowbank.

## What the manufacturer and government guides say

CSA B139, the installation code, is a paid standard, so rather than guess at a clause, here's what two documents you can read for yourself say.

Granby's installer instructions for its 2 in 1 fibreglass tanks say that tanks installed outdoors "should be protected from damage with proper protective barriers, including collision with motor vehicles." The same section says to avoid placing the tank directly under house eaves, where falling snow, ice or dripping water can reach it, and to leave about 2 inches (50 mm) of clearance from walls or other obstructions for normal expansion and visual inspection. You can read the full [Granby installer instructions (PDF)](https://www.granbyindustries.com/app/uploads/2026/04/SI0018_Ga-Granby-2-in-1-Installer-Instructions-1.pdf) for the context. That is one maker's rule for one product line, and your tank may come with different wording.

The Government of the Northwest Territories publishes a [Homeowner's Guide to Oil Tanks](https://www.gov.nt.ca/sites/ecc/files/brochures/homeowners_guide_oil_tanks.pdf). It says a heating oil tank should sit where it is safe from vehicular impact and other physical hazards, that a tank in an unsafe location should be moved, and that if relocation isn't an option it should be protected with "a solid, immovable barrier such as steel-concrete posts anchored into the ground." It also says fuel lines below the slope of a roof can be broken by falling or accumulating ice, and that a gauge should have a heavy steel protector. That is a northern territory's guidance, not a New Brunswick rule, but the logic carries over to a Moncton driveway.

For what your own lot needs, ask the installer who sets the tank. Placement depends on the code edition in force, the tank maker's instructions, and your property. Our guide to [outdoor oil tank clearances](/blog/outdoor-oil-tank-clearances/) sorts out which distances come from which source.

## Building protection that works

### Posts and bollards

Steel pipe posts filled with concrete and set into the ground are the usual choice, and the NWT guide names the same idea. A few practical points:

- **Set them deep enough for frost.** Freeze-thaw winters heave shallow footings. A post that leans after two winters protects nothing.
- **Keep them off the tank.** Leave room to walk around the tank, read the gauge and see the legs. Granby's 2 inch expansion clearance is a floor, not a target, and a post that touches the shell defeats the purpose.
- **Think about the hose.** The oil truck has to reach the fill pipe. Put the posts on the vehicle side of the tank and keep the fill pipe, the vent pipe and the path to them open. If a post blocks the driver's access, it won't stay in use.
- **Make them visible.** A plow driver can't avoid what they can't see. A reflective wrap or a bright cap on each post helps, and so does telling whoever plows your driveway exactly where the tank is.

Large boulders and concrete blocks are other forms of protection in principle, but they need to be heavy enough to stop whatever vehicle normally uses the area. A decorative planter won't do the job.

### Distance and layout

Often the best protection is distance. A tank set a few metres back from the lane, behind a fence line or tucked against the side of the house away from the driveway, may not need a barrier at all. A tank right at the edge of the pavement is the case where posts matter most. If you are rethinking the layout, our guide to [moving an oil tank](/blog/moving-an-oil-tank/) covers what a relocation involves.

### The oil line, fill pipe and vent

Protecting the tank and leaving the pipes exposed solves half the problem. Look at:

- **The oil line.** If it runs along the ground or crosses the path of a plow, it can be sheared or pinched. Our guide to [copper oil lines and when to sleeve or replace them](/blog/oil-line-replacement/) explains the options.
- **The fill and vent pipes.** These stick out of the wall or tank top and are easy to bend with a ladder, a snowblower or a bumper. See [fill and vent pipe sizes and rules](/blog/oil-tank-fill-and-vent-pipes/) before you change anything, because the rules on how they terminate aren't something to improvise.
- **The gauge.** A heavy steel gauge protector, as the NWT guide recommends, costs little compared with a snapped sight glass.

## If something already hit your tank

Go out and look, in daylight if you can. Check the tank for oil on the shell, the ground or the snow below it. Check each leg and the pad, look for new leaning, and look at the gauge, filter and oil line. A dent in a steel shell with no weeping and no wet spot is often cosmetic, though a dent near a seam or fitting deserves a closer look. Our [guide to bulging, dented or cracked tanks](/blog/oil-tank-bulging-dented/) walks through what each kind of damage means. A cracked fibreglass tank isn't a repair job.

If you see oil, shut the supply valve if it is safe to reach and call us. If oil has reached the ground, report it to your regional Department of Environment and Local Government office, or the 24-hour line at 1-800-565-1633 after hours. Don't wait for the next delivery to find out how bad it is. Whether your home insurance responds to impact damage depends on your policy, so read it or ask your broker before you assume anything.

## Protect it where it is, or move it?

The real decision comes down to the spot. Here is a plain way to sort it:

| Your situation | Sensible move |
|---|---|
| Tank is a few metres from the lane, sound, and never been touched | Keep it. A reflective marker for the plow may be enough. |
| Tank is at the pavement edge, old but sound | Add posts on the vehicle side, with the fill path open. Plan replacement for when it ages out. |
| Tank is old, rusty or dented and sits where vehicles pass | Replace it, and place the new one somewhere safer. |
| Tank has been hit and the legs or fittings are damaged | Have it looked at before the next delivery. Replace if the damage is structural. |
| No safe spot outdoors on the lot | Ask about an indoor tank. |

Replacing is the best time to fix placement, because the old tank is coming off its pad anyway. Steel versus fibreglass is its own topic. One point matters here: steel tolerates a dent better, and fibreglass resists rust better but can crack from a sharp hit. Neither is a reason to leave a tank where a bumper can reach it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does an outdoor oil tank have to be protected from vehicles?

Manufacturer instructions say it should be. Granby's installer instructions for its outdoor fibreglass tanks call for protective barriers, including against collision with motor vehicles. For the exact code requirement on your tank, ask your installer or insurer.

### What kind of barrier is best for an oil tank?

A set of steel pipe posts filled with concrete and anchored into the ground is the usual choice, and it is what the Northwest Territories homeowner's guide describes. Set them below frost depth, on the vehicle side, with room left for the oil truck to reach the fill pipe.

### Will my insurance cover a tank hit by a plow or car?

It depends on your policy. Sudden impact damage is treated differently from gradual wear in many policies, but wording varies, so ask your broker before you need to make a claim. Photograph the tank and the damage right away.

### Can I move my oil tank away from the driveway myself?

No. A tank holding oil is heavy, and the oil line, fill pipe, vent and pad all have to be redone correctly. Moving it is a job for someone who sets tanks, and the cheapest time to do it is during a replacement.

### My tank was dented by a plow. Do I need to replace it?

Not always. A dent in a steel shell with no weeping, no wet spot and straight legs is often cosmetic. A dent near a seam or fitting, a leaning tank, or any oil on the shell or ground is a reason to have it looked at.
