# The Oil Tank Vent Whistle: Why Silence During a Fill Is Bad News

Every oil delivery driver in Greater Moncton fills your tank by ear. Not by the gauge, and not by the metre on the truck. The whole time oil is flowing, there's a steady hum coming from the vent pipe, and the moment it stops, the driver shuts off the nozzle.

That hum is your oil tank vent whistle doing its job. Most homeowners have never heard of it, it costs less than a family dinner out, and it's the only thing standing between a normal delivery and a driveway full of heating oil. When it goes silent at the wrong time, good drivers walk away. Careless fills turn into five-figure cleanups.

Here's how the whistle works, what silence really means, and what it's cost real homeowners around Southeast New Brunswick when nobody was listening.

## How the Oil Tank Vent Whistle Works

Your oil tank has two pipes at the top. One is the fill pipe, where the driver connects the hose. The other is the vent pipe, which lets air escape as oil comes in. Without a vent you couldn't fill the tank at all. It'd be like pouring milk into a sealed jug.

The vent whistle, called a vent alarm in the trade, sits inside that vent line at the top of the tank. It's a simple fitting with a short tube hanging down into the tank and a small air whistle built into the body. The most common one in New Brunswick is the Scully Ventalarm, a part that costs around $30 to $40 before labour.

When the driver starts pumping, incoming oil pushes air out. All that air has one way to go: up the vent, straight through the whistle. That's the hum you hear, and it means air is moving and oil is flowing where it should.

As the oil level rises, it reaches the bottom of the whistle's tube and blocks it. Air stops moving, the sound dies, and the driver knows the tank is nearly full, with roughly 10 centimetres of air space left at the top. That space matters. Oil expands as it warms, and a tank filled to the brim on a cold morning can push oil back out by afternoon.

## Why Drivers Won't Fill a Silent Tank

Canada's installation code for oil-burning equipment, CSA B139, calls for a vent alarm wherever the driver can't see the oil level while filling. That covers basically every residential tank in Westmorland, Albert, and Kent counties. Most fuel companies around Moncton back it up with a plain policy: no whistle, no fill.

That feels harsh when you're low on oil in January, but it's the driver protecting you. With a tight connection on the fill pipe, oil moves at well over 100 litres a minute, and the driver can't see inside your tank. If the whistle never sounds, they've got no way of knowing whether the tank is empty, half full, or already topped up with a stuck gauge lying about it. Pumping blind into a full tank forces oil up and out the vent, over your siding, and into the ground in under a minute.

## What Makes an Oil Tank Whistle Go Quiet

A vent whistle has no motor, no wiring, and no moving parts beyond air itself. When it goes silent, something is physically in the way. Around Southeast New Brunswick, these are the usual suspects:

- **Snow and ice over the vent cap.** The top cause from December through March. Freezing rain glazes the screen shut, or drifting snow buries a low vent on an outdoor tank.
- **Insect nests.** Paper wasps and mud daubers treat an open vent screen like a starter home. We pull nests out of vents every fall.
- **Rust flakes and sludge.** Inside an aging steel tank, rust scales off the walls and sediment builds on the bottom. Splashing during a fill can plug the whistle's tube, and it's often the first hint the tank itself is corroding.
- **A kinked, crushed, or corroded vent line.** Ladders, snow sliding off the roof, and plain rust can all choke the pipe.
- **A tank that's already nearly full.** Sometimes silence means exactly what it's supposed to mean, even when the gauge disagrees.

That last one trips up more people than you'd think. Heather in Salisbury called her fuel company certain something was broken. The driver had stopped after barely 40 litres because the whistle cut out, yet her gauge showed just over a quarter of a tank. She wanted him to keep pumping. He refused, and good on him.

We checked the tank with a dipstick and found it sitting at about 90 percent full. The gauge float had seized months earlier and slowly drifted down, and the whistle was the only honest instrument left on the tank. Replacing the gauge and confirming the vent alarm cost Heather $210. If the driver had trusted the gauge over the silence, she'd have been paying for a cleanup instead. ## How to Check Your Vent Whistle Without Getting Into Trouble

There's a safe amount of DIY here, and then there's a line you shouldn't cross.

What you can do yourself:

- **Be home for one delivery a year and listen.** Stand well back and pay attention. A steady hum that stops just before the driver shuts off is exactly right.
- **Eyeball the vent cap from the ground.** The cap should have an intact screen with nothing packed into it. Binoculars work fine if the vent runs up high.
- **Keep it clear in winter.** Gently brush snow away from the vent and fill pipes after storms, and knock down icicles forming above them. Never hammer on the pipes themselves.
- **Ask your fuel company.** Delivery slips often note vent alarm problems long before anyone mentions them to you.

Where you stop is the moment a wrench gets involved. Vent alarms thread into the top of the tank, and on a 20-year-old steel tank those fittings can be brittle and rust-welded in place. Force one and you can crack a fitting or twist the whole vent assembly, turning a $200 fix into a leaking tank. Testing a new alarm properly also means being there under real fill conditions, which is our job, not yours.

And if you smell oil or spot a wet patch while you're looking around up there, stop entirely. That's not a whistle problem anymore. Treat it as an [emergency tank situation](/emergency-oil-tank-service-moncton/) and get a professional on site before the next delivery, not after.

One more habit worth building: the [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/furnaces-and-boilers) recommends annual professional service for oil-fired heating systems, and a vent alarm check adds about five minutes to that visit. Cheap insurance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does my oil tank whistle during a fill?

The whistle is your vent alarm working normally. As oil flows in, it pushes air out through the vent pipe, and that moving air makes the alarm hum. The sound stops once oil reaches the alarm's tube near the top of the tank, which tells the driver to shut off. A steady hum that ends right before the pump stops is exactly what you want to hear.

### Is it bad if my oil tank never whistles?

Yes, treat it as a real problem rather than a quirk. A silent vent usually means a blocked cap, a plugged or failed alarm, or a damaged vent line, and most fuel companies around Moncton won't deliver until it's fixed. It also removes the only overfill protection your tank has. A service call to sort it out typically runs $150 to $350.

### Can I replace an oil tank vent whistle myself?

We don't recommend it. The alarm threads into the top of the tank, and on older steel tanks those fittings are often brittle or seized, so forcing them can crack the tank or the piping. A proper job also includes checking the vent line and confirming the alarm sounds under real fill conditions. It's a quick, inexpensive visit for a technician and a risky afternoon for a homeowner.

### What happens if a driver overfills a tank with no working whistle?

Oil gets forced out the vent pipe or backs up the fill pipe, and even 20 litres on the ground is a reportable spill in New Brunswick. Outdoor cleanups commonly start around $5,000, and spills that reach a basement, well, or watercourse can run into six figures. That's why the no whistle, no fill policy exists. It protects you far more than it inconveniences you.
