# Storm Damaged Oil Tank: What to Check, When to Shut the Valve, and When to Replace

After a big wind or ice storm, an outdoor oil tank can be hit by a falling limb, pushed off its pad, buried in slumping snow, or have its pipes bent. Often the tank is fine and a pipe or fitting took the hit. Sometimes the tank itself is damaged, and then the question is how fast you need to act. The short version: stay clear of anything dangerous first, look at the tank from a distance, shut the valve if it is leaking and you can reach it safely, and don't fire the furnace until someone has looked at the whole setup.

This guide covers a safe order for checking the tank after a storm, what each kind of damage means, how insurers generally treat sudden storm damage, and how to decide between fixing a fitting, re-setting the tank, or replacing it. ## Safety Before the Tank

Check the surroundings before you check the tank.

- **Downed power lines.** If a line is down or hanging near the tank, stay well away and treat it as live. Report it to the utility. Don't approach the tank until it is safe.
- **Hanging limbs and leaning trees.** A cracked branch over the tank can come down in the next gust. Don't stand under it.
- **Ice on the roof above the tank.** A roof that is shedding snow and ice can drop it on the tank, or on you. Our guide to [protecting an oil tank from snow and ice](/blog/protect-oil-tank-snow-ice/) explains why roof slide is a recurring problem for outdoor tanks.
- **Smell.** If you smell oil strongly near the house or in the basement, treat it as a leak and skip ahead to the leak section below.

If there is fire risk, such as a smell of oil with a sparking line or an open flame nearby, call 911 first.

## A Safe Order for Checking an Outdoor Tank

Do this from a distance first, then up close only if it is safe. Take photos as you go, because you may need them later.

1. **Look at the tank from where you stand.** Is it where it was? Does it lean? Does it sit flat on its pad, or has a leg slid off, sunk or tipped? A tank that has shifted is under stress at every fitting. Our guide to [an oil tank leaning or sinking](/blog/oil-tank-leaning-sinking/) explains why that can't wait.
2. **Look for oil on the ground, on the snow, or on the tank.** Staining, a sheen, or dead patches of grass or discoloured snow under the tank are warning signs. Snow makes oil easy to spot.
3. **Check the shell for impact marks.** A dent, a crease, a scrape that has gone through the paint, or a bulge means something struck it. See [oil tank bulging, dented or cracked](/blog/oil-tank-bulging-dented/) for what each of those can mean.
4. **Check the fill pipe and vent pipe.** A bent, pulled or crushed pipe is common after wind or a slide of snow. A bent vent can restrict airflow, and a damaged fill pipe can be a spill risk at the next delivery.
5. **Check the oil line.** Follow the copper line from the tank to the house. A kink, a pulled fitting, or a line dragged tight is a possible leak and a furnace problem.
6. **Check the valve, filter and gauge.** Look for drips or cracks. A gauge dome knocked sideways can let rain into the tank.
7. **Look at what is on top.** Heavy wet snow, ice or a fallen branch sitting on the tank adds weight and can hide damage underneath. Don't climb on it and don't hammer at ice.

If anything on that list is wrong, don't try to straighten the tank, jack up a leg or tug at a bent pipe. A full tank is heavy, and a damaged fitting can let go when you disturb it.

## When to Shut the Valve

If you can see oil dripping from the oil line, the filter, a fitting or the valve, and you can reach the tank safely, close the shut-off valve at the tank outlet. It is usually a small lever or wheel where the line leaves the tank, and closing it stops oil feeding the leak. Put a container under the drip if that is safe. Turn the furnace off at its switch so the burner isn't pulling on a damaged line.

If the leak is coming through the wall of the tank itself, the valve won't stop it. Catch what you can, keep people and pets away, and call. Our guide to [what to do about a leaking oil tank](/blog/oil-tank-leaking-what-to-do/) walks through the first steps.

## If Oil Has Escaped

If oil has reached soil, snow melt, a drain, a ditch or a well, treat it as a spill. New Brunswick asks you to report when you know or have reason to believe a contaminant has been released. Call 911 first if there is fire or explosion risk, then call the regional Department of Environment and Local Government office during business hours. ## What the Damage Usually Means

| What you found | What it usually means | Likely decision |
|---|---|---|
| Bent or crushed vent pipe | Snow slide or limb strike, tank may be fine | Repair or replace the pipe |
| Bent fill pipe | Same, plus spill risk at the next fill | Repair the pipe before any delivery |
| Kinked or pulled oil line | Possible leak at the fitting, furnace may lose fuel | Replace the line section, check the tank |
| Cracked gauge or valve | Fitting damage | Replace the fitting |
| Tank shifted but level and dry | Pad or base problem | Re-set the tank on a solid base |
| Tank leaning, leg sunk or off the pad | Load on fittings, risk to the shell | Re-set, then inspect; replace if damaged |
| Dent, crease or bulge in the shell | Impact or internal pressure | Have it assessed, replacement is likely |
| Oil on the tank, legs or ground | Leak | Valve, report, replace |
| Tank toppled | Stress on every joint | Replace |

The pattern is that fittings and pipes can be repaired, but the tank shell can't. A dent or crease from an impact is a reason to be careful, since it may sit on a seam or a weld and may not leak right away. If the tank shell has been struck hard by a tree or a roof avalanche, don't count on it.

## Insurance and Sudden Storm Damage

Insurance wording varies, so read your policy. The Insurance Bureau of Canada's page on [wind protection for your home](https://www.ibc.ca/news-insights/in-focus/good-better-best-wind-protection-learn-the-most-effective-resilience-strategies-for-your-home) says most standard home policies cover losses from flying debris, fallen branches or trees, and sudden water entering through openings caused by a windstorm, and that coverage depends on whether the cause of loss is one the policy covers. That page doesn't mention fuel tanks, so it tells you nothing about whether yours is covered. Ask your insurer about the tank, the oil that escapes, and the cleanup. These can be treated differently.

Some practical points:

- **Photograph everything before you move anything.** Take pictures of the tree, the dent, the bent pipe and the ground.
- **Call your insurer before you get rid of damaged parts.** Ask what they need to see.
- **Expect sudden damage to be treated differently from wear.** Storm damage to a tank is a different kind of claim from a tank that corroded over twenty years. Don't assume the second kind is covered because the first might be.
- **Get the repair or replacement estimate in writing.** A written estimate is something an adjuster can work from. We provide free written estimates.

## Repair, Re-Set or Replace

**Repair a fitting** when the damage is a pipe, a valve, a gauge or a section of oil line, and the tank shell is clean, dry and undented. This is the cheap outcome.

**Re-set the tank** when it has shifted off level or off its pad, with no dent, no oil and no damaged fittings. That means a solid base, a level tank and a check of every connection afterward. Don't try to lever a tank with oil in it. The base matters as much as the tank.

**Replace the tank** when the shell is dented, creased or bulged, when it has leaked, when it toppled, or when an old tank has been knocked about and you don't trust it. If the tank was already old, a storm often just brings the decision forward. Say your tank is a 25-year-old steel tank behind a Dieppe garage and a limb has dented the end cap. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should I do first if a tree fell on my oil tank?

Stay clear of power lines and hanging limbs, then look from a distance for oil, a leaning tank and dents. If oil is escaping, close the valve at the tank only if you can reach it safely, keep people away and call. Take photos and call your insurer.

### Can I still use my furnace after a storm?

Not until the tank, pipes and oil line have been checked. A bent vent, a pulled line or a leaking fitting can turn a working system into a spill. If you see any damage, leave the furnace off and have someone look at it.

### Is a dented oil tank safe to keep using?

Not necessarily. A dent or crease can sit on a seam, and the damage may not leak right away. Have it assessed in person. If the dent is on the shell rather than a fitting, replacement is the likely answer.

### Does home insurance cover a storm-damaged oil tank?

It depends on your policy. The Insurance Bureau of Canada says most standard policies cover damage from flying debris and fallen branches or trees, but its page doesn't address fuel tanks. Ask your insurer in writing about the tank and any spill cleanup.

### Do I have to report a small leak after a storm?

New Brunswick asks you to report when you know or have reason to believe oil has been released. If it has reached soil, a drain or water, report it. For anything small and fully caught, call the regional environment office or the 24-hour line at 1-800-565-1633 and ask.
