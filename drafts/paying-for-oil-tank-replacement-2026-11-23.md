# How to Pay for an Oil Tank Replacement When There's No Grant for It

If you need a new oil tank and you're hoping a grant will cover it, the short answer is that no program we can find pays to replace an oil tank with another oil tank. The federal Oil to Heat Pump Affordability program (OHPA) is closed to new applicants, and the programs that remain are built around efficiency upgrades or leaving oil, not a like-for-like tank swap. For most households the money comes from savings, a line of credit, or a payment plan you arrange yourself. What you control is the timing and the size of the job.

This guide is for the homeowner holding an insurance letter, a rusty tank, or a quote that came in higher than expected. It covers what is and isn't on offer, how to plan around a deadline, what a sensible budget looks like using the ranges we publish, and the cheap shortcuts that cost more later. ## Is There a Grant to Replace an Oil Tank?

Not that we can find. Here is where the main programs stand.

**OHPA is closed.** New Brunswick's page on saveenergynb.ca says registration closed on June 30, 2026, and the federal page lists a different last day to apply. Our article on [the OHPA closure](/blog/ohpa-program-closed-what-now/) sorts out the two sets of dates. Either way, nobody is taking new applications, and OHPA was about switching to a heat pump, not about buying a new oil tank.

**The Enhanced Energy Savings Program** is the no-cost route for lower-income homeowners. Its [Save Energy NB page](https://www.saveenergynb.ca/en/for-home/enhanced-energy-savings-program/) sets an income limit on combined household income, and the upgrades it describes are insulation, ventilation and heat pumps. For an oil-heated home, the program's direction is toward a heat pump and away from oil. That's a switch, not money for a new tank. Program terms and funding change, so read the current page or call NB Power before you count on anything.

Save Energy NB's other home programs are rebates on efficiency upgrades. None of the ones we looked at lists an oil tank.

If you're weighing a heat pump anyway, that can change the math, because a tank you remove doesn't need to be replaced. We'll come back to that near the end. If you're staying on oil, plan on paying for the tank yourself.

## Plan Around the Insurance Deadline

For many households the money question starts with a letter. The insurer says the tank is past its age limit and must be replaced by a date. That deadline shapes everything, so work backward from it.

The Insurance Bureau of Canada's [Code of Consumer Rights and Responsibilities](https://www.ibc.ca/insurance-basics/how-insurance-works/code-of-consumer-rights-and-responsibilities) says insurers provide renewal terms at least 30 days before a policy expires. That's the floor. It means a letter can leave you a month or less, and the first week usually goes to getting your head around it, so start the day the letter lands. Our guide to [replacing an oil tank before an insurance deadline](/blog/insurance-deadline-replace-oil-tank/) walks through the first calls.

For the money side, a few steps help.

1. **Get a written estimate first.** Before you ask the insurer for anything, have a document that shows a price and a proposed install date. It makes every later conversation easier.
2. **Ask your broker about an extension, in writing.** Brokers can only pass along what the underwriter allows, so ask the specific question: if the tank is booked for replacement by a certain date, will the insurer hold the policy until then? Get the answer by email or letter, not by phone alone. We can't promise an insurer will agree, and it isn't something we arrange for you.
3. **Book before the deadline, not on it.** An installation date inside the window is a stronger position than a plan to "get to it." Winter weather can move outdoor work around, so leave room.
4. **Ask what proof the insurer wants after the work.** Common asks are a photo of the new tank's data plate and a written record of the install, but your insurer decides. Knowing in advance means you pay for the paperwork once.

If you can't make the date, tell the broker before the deadline passes, not after. A broker who hears early can sometimes look at other options. A broker who hears late has only one.

## Ways Households Usually Cover the Cost

We won't name lenders or quote rates here. These are the general routes, with the trade-offs of each.

**Savings.** The simplest option, and the one with no interest. If you're in the middle of a deadline, it's also the fastest.

**A line of credit.** A personal or home equity line lets you borrow what you need and pay it back over time. The Government of Canada's [page on home equity lines of credit](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/mortgages/home-equity-line-credit.html) explains that a HELOC is secured against your home, that most carry a variable rate tied to prime, and that failing to repay could cost you the house. It's a flexible tool for a one-time job, and a risky one if you treat it like spare cash. If you do borrow, it helps to pick a repayment period up front and stick to it.

**A payment plan or credit card.** Some households split a bill across a few months. Whatever you use, ask for the total cost including interest before you agree, not just the monthly figure.

**Family help.** Common, and often the cheapest. Put the terms in writing, even between relatives.

**Timing the spend.** If your insurer's date allows it, you can plan the replacement for a month when you have the money on hand rather than borrowing for it.

## What Not to Do to Save Money

When a bill feels too big, the temptation is to shave it. A few shortcuts cost far more later than they save now.

**Don't buy a cheap secondhand tank.** A used tank has an unknown history, a data plate that may be unreadable, and no way to check what is going on inside the steel. Many insurers also look at tank age, so a used tank can leave you right back where the letter started. See our piece on [why a secondhand tank is the wrong fix](/blog/used-oil-tank-for-sale/).

**Don't do it yourself.** The savings look good on paper, but a tank install isn't a weekend job. There are code requirements for the base, the fill and vent piping, the fire-safety valve and the oil line, and a mistake can leave you with a spill, an uninsurable tank, or both. Our [honest answer on removing your own oil tank](/blog/diy-oil-tank-removal/) explains where the limits are.

**Don't wait for a leak.** A leaking-tank call often carries the emergency premium we publish, $200 to $400 extra for nights, weekends and leaking-tank calls, and it adds cleanup on top. Soil work is a different scale of cost entirely. Replacing a tank on your own schedule is almost always the cheaper day.

**Don't pick on the lowest number alone.** Two quotes for the same tank can differ because one leaves out the stand, the filter, the valve or the disposal. Compare what each one lists before you compare the totals.

## When Paying for a New Tank Isn't the Right Move

Replacement is the right answer for a household staying on oil, but it isn't the only answer. If a heat pump or another heat source is already on your list, the tank may not need replacing at all. Removing it instead costs $500 to $900 for an outdoor steel tank or $700 to $1,100 for an indoor one, and that is a lot less than a full swap.

That's a bigger decision than a tank, and it shouldn't be rushed because of one insurance date. If you need coverage now and a conversion later, a replacement tank on a plan you can afford may still be the practical choice. A written estimate for each route lets you compare them with real numbers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there a government grant to replace an oil tank in New Brunswick?

Not one we can find. OHPA is closed to new applicants, and the Save Energy NB programs described on their pages cover insulation, ventilation and heat pumps, not a new oil tank. If you can't find a program that lists tank replacement, assume you'll pay for it yourself and plan from a written estimate.

### Can I ask my insurer for more time to replace the tank?

You can ask, through your broker, and it's worth asking in writing. The answer depends on the insurer's own rules, and a booked installation date with a written estimate makes the request stronger. Don't assume an extension will be granted, and don't leave it until the final days to ask.

### Does a home equity line of credit make sense for a tank?

It can, if you'd otherwise pay a higher rate elsewhere and you have a repayment plan. The Government of Canada notes that a HELOC is secured against your home and usually carries a variable rate, so the cost can change. Borrow only what the written estimate says, and ask the lender for the full terms.

### Is a used oil tank a safe way to save money?

Rarely. A secondhand tank has an unknown history and may not satisfy your insurer's age or condition rules. The saving at purchase can disappear if the tank has to be replaced again, or if a leak follows. A new tank installed to code is the safer base for a policy.

### What if I'm switching to a heat pump instead of replacing the tank?

Then the tank may only need removal, which costs less than a full swap. Check the current terms of any heat pump program you're counting on before you commit, because dates and funding change, and ask the program about tank removal costs separately. Call us for a removal estimate if you need one.
