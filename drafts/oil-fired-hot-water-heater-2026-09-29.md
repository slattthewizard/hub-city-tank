# Oil-Fired Hot Water Heater: What Happens to Your Tank After a Heat Pump Goes In

Plenty of Greater Moncton homes have added a heat pump and still have an oil tank sitting in the basement or out back. That isn't always an oversight. The ductless and ducted air-source heat pumps going into most homes heat and cool the air in your house. They don't make your hot water. If your hot water comes from an oil-fired boiler through an indirect tank or a coil, or from a standalone oil-fired water heater, that equipment is still drawing from the same tank the furnace used to.

Which means the tank's job did not end when the heat pump showed up. It changed.

## How Homes Around Here Actually Make Hot Water on Oil

Many oil-heated homes in Southeast New Brunswick have a forced-air oil furnace and a plain electric water heater sitting next to it. If that describes your house, your hot water was never on oil, a heat pump conversion doesn't change it, and most of this article isn't about you.

Some homes, especially older farmhouses and houses with baseboard or radiator heat rather than forced air, run on an oil-fired boiler instead of a furnace. Boilers heat water for the house, not air directly, and a fair number of them are also set up to produce your household hot water off the same burner. There are two common ways that is done:

- **A tankless coil.** A heat exchanger sits inside the boiler itself. Household water is heated as it passes through the coil, on demand, with no separate storage tank, which means the boiler has to keep its own water hot, or fire when a tap opens, all year round.
- **An indirect tank.** A separate insulated storage tank sits beside the boiler, with boiler water running through a coil inside it. The boiler heats the stored water in batches and the tank holds it, so the boiler isn't keeping itself hot all day and night just to be ready for a tap.

Less often, a home has a standalone oil-fired water heater: a self-contained unit with its own burner, not tied to a space-heating boiler at all. Whichever setup you have, the fuel comes from the same oil tank, on the same delivery schedule, subject to the same age and condition rules as before.

## Why the Heat Pump Doesn't Take Over Hot Water

This trips people up because a heat pump feels like it should replace everything oil ever did. It does not, and this is not a gap in New Brunswick's heat pump programs. It is how the equipment works.

A ductless mini-split or a ducted air-source system moves heat between the outdoor air and the air inside your house. [Natural Resources Canada's guide to heating and cooling with a heat pump](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/energy-star/heating-cooling-heat-pump) explains how air-source units use the outdoor air as their heat source, and notes that heat pumps can supply hot water only "in some cases": ground-source systems with a domestic hot water option, for example, or air-to-water heat pumps built for homes with hydronic (water-based) heating. Neither is the air-to-air unit on the wall of most converted homes. For those homes, domestic hot water is a separate category of equipment. A dedicated heat pump water heater pulls heat out of room air and puts it into a water tank instead, and [NRCan's page on heat pump water heaters](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/products/water-heaters/heat-pump-water-heaters/14556) describes it as a water heater that takes heat from the air in a basement or utility room and moves it into a tank of water. It's bought and installed as water heating equipment, not as part of your space-heating heat pump. If you have an oil boiler and radiators, it's worth asking whoever quotes your heat pump whether an air-to-water system was considered, because that changes the hot water question.

So a heat pump conversion answers the furnace question. It does not answer the hot water question unless somebody also swaps out the water heater, the coil, or the boiler that makes it.

## What This Means for the Tank

If your hot water still runs on oil after the heat pump goes in, your tank is not retired. It has one job instead of two, but it is still a live, fuelled, code-covered piece of equipment, and it still needs the same attention it always did.

A few things that do not change:

- **Age and insurance.** Your insurer's questions about tank age, wall type, and condition don't usually depend on whether the tank feeds a furnace, a boiler coil, or an indirect tank. If your renewal has ever asked about the tank, see our guide on [oil tank age and insurance limits](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) for what that usually looks like.
- **Code coverage.** The tank, the lines, the vent and fill, and the appliance it feeds are all still covered by [CSA B139](/blog/csa-b139-oil-tank-code/), the installation code for oil-burning equipment, whether that equipment is a furnace, a boiler, or a water heater.
- **Winter and weather.** Freeze-thaw cycles, ice sliding off a roof onto an outdoor tank, and salt air along the Northumberland Strait near Shediac keep working on the tank exactly as before.

One thing does change, and it is worth planning for: how much oil moves through the tank. Space heating was the big load. What's left is hot water, which is a much smaller one. Oil now sits in the tank for months instead of weeks, and if you let the level run down between deliveries, the empty space above the oil is where condensation forms and water collects on the bottom of a steel tank. Our guide on [keeping an oil tank full over summer](/blog/keep-oil-tank-full-summer/) covers why a low level is the problem, and the same logic applies once hot water is the only load left on the tank.

Say your farmhouse near Salisbury runs an oil boiler with an indirect tank for hot water, and you've just added a ducted heat pump for the house itself. Your oil use could drop to a fraction of what it was. That's good news for your fuel bill. It is a reason to keep an eye on the tank's fill level and get it looked at at the usual intervals, not a reason to forget about it.

## Tankless Coil, Indirect Tank, or Standalone Oil Water Heater: What It Means for the Tank

| Setup | How it draws from the tank | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Tankless coil in an oil boiler | Boiler keeps its water hot or fires on demand, so it burns some oil every day of the year | Oil burned through the summer; the boiler can't be shut off for the season without losing hot water |
| Indirect tank off an oil boiler | Boiler heats the storage tank in batches, then sits off between draws | Fewer, larger draws on the oil tank; steadier fill level |
| Standalone oil-fired water heater | Its own burner draws directly on the tank, independent of any furnace or boiler | Same tank, same lines, now feeding only this one appliance |

None of these change what the tank itself needs. They change how steadily oil moves through it, which is useful to know when you are deciding what to do next.

## If You Decide to Move Hot Water Off Oil Too

Some homeowners keep oil-fired hot water going for years after a heat pump conversion, because the boiler and coil or indirect tank still work fine and there is no reason to touch them. Others decide to finish the job and take hot water off oil entirely, usually with an electric storage tank or a heat pump water heater, which pulls heat from room air the way [NRCan describes](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/products/water-heaters/heat-pump-water-heaters/14556) rather than burning fuel. This is the point where the tank question gets decided. Once nothing in the house burns oil, an idle tank has no job left, and idle tanks do not age gracefully. You have two honest choices:

1. **Keep the tank in reserve.** This is rarely worth it once both the furnace and the water heater are off oil. An unused tank still corrodes, your insurer may still ask about it, and it sits there as a question mark for the next buyer's home inspector. The national installation code also has a view on idle tanks: [CSA Group's summary of the 10th edition of CSA B139](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/) says it added requirements for removing tank systems that haven't been used for more than three years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a heat pump make hot water too?

A standard air-source heat pump, ductless or ducted, only heats and cools the air in your home. It does not produce domestic hot water. A separate heat pump water heater does that job by pulling heat from room air into a water tank, but it is different equipment from the unit heating your house, and you would need to add or buy one on purpose.

### If my furnace is gone but my hot water is still oil, is my tank still an active tank?

Yes. As long as any appliance draws fuel from it, whether that's a boiler with a coil or an indirect tank, or a standalone oil-fired water heater, the tank is in service, and you should expect your insurer to ask about it the same way it did when it fed the furnace too.

### Will my oil tank last longer now that it only feeds hot water?

Not automatically. Age and wall condition matter more than how much oil moves through it. Oil that sits for months, especially in a tank left half empty, gives condensation more time to collect on the bottom, where steel tanks rust from the inside. Your insurer's age limits and the tank's own condition still decide how long it stays.

### Can I switch my hot water off oil without touching my heating system?

Generally yes, if you have an oil boiler with a coil or an indirect tank, or a standalone oil-fired water heater. Swapping that one appliance for an electric tank or a heat pump water heater does not require changing your space heating. Confirm the details with an HVAC contractor and, if you want to pursue a rebate, with NB Power's Save Energy NB program.

### If I take hot water off oil too, do I have to remove the tank right away?

Not right away, but don't leave it for years. CSA Group's summary of the 10th edition of CSA B139 says it added requirements for removing tank systems unused for more than three years, and an idle tank keeps corroding and can still come up at insurance renewal. Removing it once nothing in the house burns oil closes the question for the next inspection or buyer.

### Does a tankless coil or indirect tank change what my insurer asks about my oil tank?

Usually not. Insurer questions focus on the tank itself: its age, its wall construction, its condition, and often its location. What it feeds, a furnace, a boiler coil, an indirect tank, or a standalone water heater, doesn't normally change those questions, though your own insurer's form is the one that counts.
