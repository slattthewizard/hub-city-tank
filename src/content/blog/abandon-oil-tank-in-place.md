## What Abandoning an Oil Tank in Place Actually Means

Abandoning a tank in place, sometimes called closure in place, is not the same as ignoring it. Ignoring it means a rusting steel shell full of oily sludge sits under your lawn, quietly thinning out year after year until it leaks or collapses.

A proper abandonment is a controlled shutdown. Here's what the process looks like when it's done right:

1. **Locate and expose the tank.** The top of the tank is uncovered so it can be opened safely.
2. **Pump out all liquids and sludge.** Every litre of oil, water, and sediment comes out and gets disposed of at a licensed facility.
3. **Clean the tank interior.** The inside is cleaned so no oily residue is left to migrate later.
4. **Test the surrounding soil.** Samples are taken around and beneath the tank to confirm nothing has leaked. This step is the whole ballgame.
5. **Fill the tank with an inert material.** Clean sand, gravel, or a flowable concrete slurry fills the empty shell so it can never collapse and leave a sinkhole.
6. **Cap and disconnect the lines.** Fill pipes and vent pipes are cut and capped so nobody can ever accidentally pump oil into a dead tank.
7. **Document everything.** You get a written record of the closure, the soil results, and the fill material used.

That last step matters more than most homeowners realize. A tank abandoned in place without paperwork is, in the eyes of a future buyer's lawyer or insurer, just a buried tank. The documents are what turn it from a liability into a closed file.

## When You Can Leave a Buried Tank in the Ground

The main reason to abandon a tank in place instead of removing it is access. Some tanks simply cannot come out without wrecking something expensive above them.

We see this constantly in older Moncton neighbourhoods. Houses from the 1950s and 60s often had underground tanks installed close to the foundation. Then decades of renovations happened on top of them. Sunrooms, garages, concrete patios, decks, even additions with full footings.

Denise in Riverview is a good example. Her bungalow had a 900 litre steel tank buried tight against the foundation, and a previous owner had built a sunroom directly over most of it. Pulling the tank meant cutting the sunroom slab, shoring the footings, and rebuilding afterward. The excavation contractor's rough number for all that was about $9,500 before any landscaping.

Instead, her soil samples came back clean, the tank was pumped, cleaned, filled with sand, and capped, and she got a closure report for her records. Total cost: about $2,600. The sunroom never moved an inch.

Closure in place tends to make sense when:

- The tank sits under a structure, slab, deck, or addition
- Removing it would damage septic lines, wells, or mature trees
- Soil testing confirms the tank has not leaked
- Excavation access for machinery is impossible or wildly expensive
- You're keeping the property long term and just want the risk shut down properly

Notice the common thread: the soil has to be clean. If testing finds contamination, abandonment in place is off the table. A leaking tank has to come out, and the affected soil has to be dealt with, full stop.

## When Full Removal Is the Smarter Call

Here's the uncomfortable truth about abandonment: even done perfectly, it leaves a steel object in the ground, and some people will always ask questions about it.

The people asking are usually buyers, their lawyers, their home inspectors, and their insurance companies. Only complete removal lets everyone see the actual tank, confirm its condition, and inspect the native soil underneath it. That certainty has real value the day you sell.

Marc in Dieppe learned this during a sale. His split-level had a buried tank that had been out of service for years. The buyer's insurer flatly refused to write a policy on a property with an unaccounted-for underground tank, and the deal stalled two weeks before closing. Marc had the tank removed for $3,900, the soil under it checked out clean, and the sale closed on schedule. His agent told him afterward that abandonment paperwork might have satisfied the insurer, but might is a rough word to gamble a closing date on.

Full removal usually wins when:

- You're selling within the next few years
- You're refinancing and the lender asks about the tank
- The tank is reasonably accessible with an excavator
- There's any sign of a past leak, oily odours, or staining
- You want the issue permanently and provably gone

## What the Rules and Your Insurer Say in New Brunswick

New Brunswick's petroleum storage regulations focus most heavily on larger and commercial tank systems, and the rules for a single residential tank are less prescriptive than many homeowners expect. That doesn't mean you're free to do whatever you like.

Two things are firmly true. First, if oil has escaped from any tank, you have a duty to report the spill to the province. The [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg.html) handles environmental emergencies and spill reporting, and getting ahead of a leak is always cheaper than hiding one. Second, whatever the regulations technically require, your insurance company sets its own bar, and it's usually higher.

Most insurers writing policies in Southeast New Brunswick want underground residential tanks gone or professionally closed with documentation. Some will accept a properly abandoned tank with clean soil results. Others want removal, period. If you've had one of those unsettling insurance letters about your oil tank, you already know how much weight their opinion carries.

For broader background on why underground tanks worry regulators everywhere, the U.S. EPA's [underground storage tank program](https://www.epa.gov/ust) is a solid plain-language resource. The chemistry of rusting steel and leaking oil doesn't care which side of the border it's on.

An old buried tank never gets safer with time. Steel only rusts in one direction. Every year you wait, three risks grow:

- **Leak risk.** Residual oil and sludge sit against thinning steel.
- **Collapse risk.** An empty rusted tank can cave in and open a sinkhole under a lawn, driveway, or play area.
- **Paper risk.** Every year that passes makes the tank harder to explain to a buyer, a lawyer, or an insurer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it legal to abandon an oil tank in place in New Brunswick?

Yes, when it's done properly. A legitimate closure means the tank is pumped out, cleaned, confirmed leak-free through soil testing, filled with inert material, and documented. Simply leaving an old tank in the ground untouched is a different thing entirely, and if that tank leaks you're legally required to report the spill to the province.

### How much does it cost to abandon an oil tank in place?

In the Greater Moncton area, most residential closures in place run between $1,500 and $3,500, depending on tank size, depth, access, and whether sand or concrete slurry is used as fill. That assumes soil testing comes back clean. If contamination turns up, the project becomes a removal and cleanup instead, and costs rise sharply from there.

### Will my insurance company accept a tank that was abandoned in place?

Some will, some won't, and the paperwork usually decides it. Insurers that accept closure in place want to see documentation showing the tank was emptied, cleaned, tested, and filled by a professional. If your closure has no records, most insurers will treat it as an unknown buried tank. Call your broker before you choose between closure and removal, because their answer should shape your decision.

### What happens if contamination is found during the abandonment?

The closure stops and the job changes. A tank that has leaked can't be abandoned in place, so it has to be excavated, and the contaminated soil has to be assessed and remediated. The spill also needs to be reported to the New Brunswick Department of Environment and Local Government. It's not the news anyone wants, but finding a leak during a controlled closure is far better than finding it during a home sale.
