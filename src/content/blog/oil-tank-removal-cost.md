## What Changes Your Oil Tank Removal Price

Two neighbours on the same street in Moncton can get quotes hundreds of dollars apart, and both quotes can be fair. Here's what moves the number.

**How much oil is left in the tank.** A near-empty tank is quick work. A tank with 400 litres still sloshing around needs to be pumped out first, and that fuel has to be handled and transported properly. If the oil is still clean, some of it may be transferable to a new tank, which softens the cost.

**Where the tank sits.** Outdoor tanks with clear access are the cheapest to remove. Basement tanks that have to be drained, cut into sections, and carried up a narrow staircase take more time and more hands. If your tank went into the basement before the rec room was finished around it, expect the higher end of the range.

**The tank's condition.** A solid tank can be moved in one piece. A badly rusted one has to be handled carefully so it doesn't fail mid-move. If you've spotted flaking rust or a damp patch underneath, mention it when you call so the crew comes prepared.

**Sludge at the bottom.** Decades of condensation and settling leave a layer of water and sludge in most old tanks. That material can't go down a drain or into the garbage. Proper disposal is part of an honest quote, and it's one of the line items lowball quotes tend to leave out.

**Season and urgency.** A planned removal in June is straightforward. An emergency removal in a February cold snap, with a leaking tank and a furnace that still needs to run, involves more urgency and sometimes temporary fuel arrangements. ## Why Buried Tank Removal Costs So Much More

If your tank is underground, everything gets more involved. The crew has to locate the tank precisely, dig down to it without damaging gas lines, water lines, or your foundation, lift a heavy steel tank out of the hole, check the surrounding soil, and then backfill and grade the site. That's excavation equipment, more people, and more hours, which is why buried jobs start around $1,500 and climb from there.

The bigger financial risk is what's in the soil. Buried steel tanks rust from the outside in, and many were abandoned decades ago with oil still inside. If the soil around the tank shows contamination, New Brunswick's [Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation](https://laws.gnb.ca/en/showdoc/cr/87-97) comes into play, and the province's [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg.html) may require testing, remediation, and formal reporting. Remediation jobs routinely pass $10,000, and severe ones go well beyond that.

Here's the reassuring part: most buried tanks we dig up in Westmorland, Albert, and Kent counties come out with clean soil. The horror stories are real but they're not the norm, and finding a tank early, before it fails, is exactly how you stay out of them. ## How to Avoid Surprise Fees and Spot a Lowball Quote

The cheapest quote in your inbox isn't always the cheapest job on your final invoice. Here's how to protect yourself.

**Get it in writing.** A verbal ballpark isn't a price. A written estimate should spell out draining, disconnection, removal, disposal, and fuel handling. If a quote is silent on disposal or leftover oil, assume those show up later as extras.

**Ask what happens if there's oil left.** Some outfits quote an empty tank and charge steeply for pumping. Ask for the pump-out cost up front so it's not a surprise on removal day.

**Confirm licensing and insurance.** Handling fuel oil isn't a job for an unlicensed hauler with a trailer. If a leak happens during an amateur removal, you own that problem, and it can cost more than a hundred proper removals. This is also where DIY ends. Draining lines, cutting steel near fuel residue, and transporting oil are professional work, and the vapours in an "empty" tank can ignite around a grinder or torch. Disconnecting your own tank to save $400 risks a spill or fire that costs thousands.

**Ask about insurance paperwork.** If your removal is driven by an insurer's letter, you'll want documentation that the work was done properly. A legitimate contractor provides it as a matter of course. And if a leak is part of your story, it's worth reading up on [whether insurance covers oil tank leaks](/blog/does-insurance-cover-oil-tank-leaks/) before you assume anything about coverage.

**Beware the too-good number.** A $200 removal quote usually means the sludge, the fuel, or the disposal fee is missing from it, or the tank is headed somewhere it shouldn't go. In a province where improper disposal can land back on the homeowner, that's no bargain at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to remove an oil tank in New Brunswick?

Most above-ground tank removals in the Moncton area cost $300 to $800, depending on access, tank condition, and how much oil is left inside. Buried tanks run $2,000 to $4,000 or more because of the excavation involved. If contaminated soil turns up, remediation can push a buried job past $10,000, though clean removals are far more common.

### Does home insurance pay for oil tank removal?

Routine removal of an aging tank is considered maintenance, so insurers generally won't pay for it. In fact, it's usually the insurer demanding the removal as a condition of keeping your coverage. Sudden leak damage may be covered depending on your policy, but oil spill coverage varies a lot between insurers, so read your policy and ask your broker directly.

### Can I remove an oil tank myself to save money?

You can clear access to the tank and burn the oil level down before removal day, and both genuinely help lower the cost. But draining, disconnecting, cutting, and hauling the tank should be left to licensed professionals. One spilled pail of furnace oil can contaminate soil and trigger cleanup costs that dwarf what you saved, and improper disposal can leave you legally on the hook.

### What happens to the old tank and the leftover oil?

Clean oil can often be transferred into your new tank or removed for proper handling, while the sludge and water at the bottom go to an approved disposal facility. The empty tank is drained, cleaned, and sent for scrap metal recycling. You should never see it rusting in a ditch, and a reputable contractor can tell you exactly where yours is going.
