Half a cup of water sitting in the bottom of your oil tank can shut your furnace down on the coldest night of February. Most homeowners who call us about a dead furnace in winter had no idea water was the problem, because water in oil tanks hides where you can't see it, right at the bottom, underneath hundreds of litres of fuel.

Water is heavier than heating oil, so it sinks. It pools on the tank floor, quietly rusting the steel from the inside, feeding sludge, and waiting for a cold snap to freeze your fuel line solid. The good news? Checking for it takes about ten minutes, and getting it out usually costs a couple hundred dollars, not a couple thousand.

Here's how water gets in, how to find it, and how to get rid of it before it ruins your tank or your winter.

## How Water Gets Into an Oil Tank

There's no single culprit. In Southeast New Brunswick we see four main sources, and some tanks have more than one going at once.

**Condensation is the big one.** Every oil tank has air space above the fuel, and that air carries moisture. When the temperature swings, and around here it swings hard, that moisture condenses on the cold steel walls and dribbles down into the oil. The emptier the tank, the more air space, the more condensation. A tank left a quarter full through a humid Maritime summer is basically a water-making machine.

Outdoor tanks get it worst. They bake in the July sun, cool off overnight, and repeat that cycle for months. Coastal properties add fog and salt air on top of it.

Marc in Shediac learned this the expensive way. His outdoor 910-litre tank sat about a quarter full from April to October. When his burner started sputtering in the fall, we dipped the tank and the water-finding paste showed almost 4 centimetres of water on the bottom. The pump-out and sludge treatment ran him $240, but the damage was already done. The tank floor was pitted with internal rust, and he ended up booking a full replacement at $3,400 before the snow flew. Six years of summer condensation did that, one drip at a time.

**Rain and snowmelt get in through bad caps.** A cracked fill cap gasket, a missing vent cap, or a loose fitting gives rainwater a straight path into the tank. It doesn't take much. One driving rainstorm off the Northumberland Strait can push a surprising amount of water through a gap the width of a toonie.

**Deliveries can carry a little water in.** It's rare with reputable fuel suppliers, but condensation in delivery equipment does happen from time to time.

**Groundwater leaks into buried tanks.** If your tank is underground and water is showing up inside it, that's serious, because a hole that lets water in also lets oil out. The U.S. EPA identifies corrosion and water intrusion as leading causes of underground tank failure, and the science is the same on our side of the border. You can read their guidance at the [EPA underground storage tank program](https://www.epa.gov/ust). A buried tank taking on water isn't a DIY situation, and the sooner a professional looks at it, the cheaper it tends to be.

## Signs There's Water in Your Oil Tank

Water doesn't announce itself. It builds up for months or years, then shows up as one of these problems.

**Your furnace sputters, short cycles, or locks out.** When the water level rises high enough to reach the fuel pickup, it gets pulled toward the burner. Burners don't burn water. You'll get rough starts, weak flames, and eventually a reset button that won't stay reset.

**Trouble right after a fuel delivery.** A fresh delivery stirs up everything sitting on the tank floor. If your furnace acts up within a day or two of a fill, suspect that water and sludge got churned into the pickup.

**Rust streaks or weeping at the bottom seams.** Tanks rust from the inside out, and water sitting on the floor is why. If you're seeing orange streaks or damp spots low on the tank, read our guide on [rust on your oil tank](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) and take it seriously.

**A frozen fuel line in winter.** Heating oil doesn't freeze at any temperature New Brunswick can throw at it. Water does. If your furnace dies on a bitter night and the line is blocked, there's a decent chance you're looking at an ice plug.

That's exactly what happened to Diane in Riverview last January. It was minus 18, her furnace locked out at 5 a.m., and the house was down to 14 degrees by the time she called. ## How to Check for Water in Your Oil Tank

This is one of the few oil tank jobs a homeowner can safely do, and it's cheap.

You'll need water-finding paste, which costs about $15 to $25 at a plumbing supply store, and a clean wooden dowel or stick long enough to reach the bottom of the tank through the fill pipe.

Here's the process:

1. Remove the fill cap on top of the tank.
2. Coat the bottom 10 centimetres of the stick with the paste.
3. Lower the stick straight down until it touches the tank floor, hold it for about 30 seconds, then pull it out.
4. Read the colour. The paste changes colour, usually to red or purple, wherever water touched it. The height of the colour change tells you how deep the water is.

A trace, meaning a few millimetres, is common and worth rechecking in a month. Anything over a centimetre needs to come out.

A few safety rules. Don't open any fitting other than the fill cap. Don't touch the drain valve or the fuel line connections, because a stuck or corroded valve can turn a check-up into a spill. Don't smoke or use open flame anywhere near the tank. And if you smell oil strongly, see wet staining, or find sludge on the stick along with the water, stop there and get a professional in.

## Getting Water Out of an Oil Tank

Once you know water's in there, you've got a few options, and the right one depends on how much water you found and what shape the tank is in.

**Professional pump-out.** A technician pumps the water and loose sludge off the tank floor, either through the fill pipe with a suction wand or through the bottom drain if it's in safe condition. This is the proper fix for anything more than a trace. The water comes out, gets disposed of legally, and the tech can tell you what the tank floor looks like while they're at it.

**Hand pumping small amounts.** For a trace of water in an accessible tank, a small hand pump with tubing down the fill pipe can lift water off the bottom. It works, but it's slow, messy, and easy to do badly. Remember that whatever you pull out is an oil and water mix, and you can't pour it down a drain or onto the ground. It has to go to a hazardous waste depot.

**Fuel treatments.** Additive treatments disperse small amounts of water so it passes through the burner harmlessly. They're fine as a follow-up after a pump-out, or for trace moisture. They are not a fix for a centimetre or more of standing water, no matter what the bottle says.

**What never works: draining onto the ground.** Opening the drain valve and letting it run out contaminates your own property, breaks environmental rules, and even a few litres of oily water can turn into a five-figure cleanup.

## What Happens If You Leave the Water In

Steel tanks almost never rust through from the outside. They fail from the inside, at the bottom, in the one spot nobody can inspect. Water sitting on the tank floor is what does it.

It gets worse. The boundary layer where water meets oil is a perfect home for microbes that feed on the fuel. They multiply, die, and settle into a black acidic muck that speeds up the corrosion and clogs filters and nozzles. If your tank is more than a few years old and has ever held water, there's a good chance that layer is already forming. Our post on [oil tank sludge](/blog/oil-tank-sludge/) covers what that muck does to a heating system.

Left long enough, pitting eats through the floor and you've got a leak, with cleanup costs that regularly run into the tens of thousands of dollars, and insurance coverage that's shaky at best on older tanks. That's the point where a $240 pump-out you skipped becomes a full [oil tank replacement in Moncton](/oil-tank-replacement-moncton/), plus whatever the spilled oil took with it.

The math is never close. Water removal is a service call. A rotted tank is a project.

## Keeping Water Out of Your Tank for Good

Prevention here is genuinely easy, and most of it costs nothing.

**Keep the tank full through the off-season.** A full tank has almost no air space, which means almost no condensation. Topping up in spring instead of letting the tank sit low all summer is the single best habit an oil-heat household can build. We wrote a full piece on why you should [keep your oil tank full in summer](/blog/keep-oil-tank-full-summer/).

**Check your caps and gaskets twice a year.** Make sure the fill cap seats tight, the gasket isn't cracked or missing, and the vent cap is in place with its screen intact. This is a thirty-second job.

It's the check Paul in Memramcook wishes he'd done sooner. His fill cap gasket had split, probably years earlier, and every rainstorm sent a trickle straight into the tank. He caught it because he happened to test with paste after reading about it online. The fix was a $200 pump-out and about $35 in parts for a new cap and gasket. The same problem ignored for another two or three winters would likely have cost him the whole tank.

**Test with paste every fall.** Ten minutes in October beats an emergency call in January. It pairs well with the rest of a pre-winter routine, and the [U.S. Department of Energy's home heating guidance](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/home-heating-systems) backs up what every local tech will tell you: regular maintenance is the cheapest heat you'll ever buy.

**Get eyes on an aging tank.** If your tank is past 15 years old, has visible rust at the seams, or has failed a paste test more than once, have it assessed before winter, not during it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if there's water in my oil tank?

The reliable test is water-finding paste on a stick, lowered through the fill pipe to the tank floor. The paste changes colour where water touches it and shows you exactly how deep the layer is. Warning signs that should prompt a test include a sputtering burner, trouble after a delivery, rust at the bottom seams, or a frozen fuel line in winter.

### Is a small amount of water in the tank normal?

A trace of a few millimetres is common, especially in outdoor tanks, and it isn't an emergency. It should still be dealt with, because water never leaves on its own and it builds up season after season. Recheck monthly, keep the tank full to limit condensation, and book a pump-out once it climbs past a centimetre.

### How much does it cost to get water out of an oil tank?

In the Greater Moncton area, a professional water and sludge pump-out typically runs $150 to $300, with a fuel treatment and line bleed adding $100 to $250 if needed. Emergency frozen line calls in winter run $250 to $450. If water has sat long enough to pit the tank floor, replacement runs roughly $2,800 to $4,200.

### Can water in the tank damage my furnace?

Yes. Water pulled into the fuel line corrodes the pump, fouls the filter, and clogs the nozzle, which causes rough starts, lockouts, and no-heat calls. In winter it can freeze inside the line and block the fuel entirely. Getting the water out protects the burner just as much as it protects the tank.
