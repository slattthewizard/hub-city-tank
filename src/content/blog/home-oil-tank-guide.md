A home oil tank is the tank that stores heating oil for your furnace or boiler, usually holding somewhere between 620 and 1,150 litres, sitting in the basement, on a stand outside, or, in older properties, buried in the yard. Most are steel. A growing number are fibreglass or a double-wall design with a plastic inner tank inside a steel shell. Which one you have, and how old it is, decides almost everything else: what your insurer will say at renewal, how long you have before it needs to come out, and what a buyer's lawyer will ask about if you sell.

## The types of home oil tanks you'll find in a Southeast NB house

Walk into basements and backyards from Moncton to Sackville and you'll see four tank designs, over and over.

Single-wall steel is the old standard: an oval tank on four legs. It's the cheapest tank to buy and the easiest to install, and it's what most houses built before 2000 still have. Its weak point is condensation collecting at the bottom of the tank, which rusts the steel from the inside out for years before anything shows on the outside.

Double-bottom steel puts a second layer of steel under the oil, where internal corrosion starts, so a pinhole in the inner floor doesn't reach the basement floor straight away. It costs more than plain single-wall and gives insurers something they're more comfortable with.

Double-wall tanks with a plastic inner tank (Roth is the best-known name) use a polyethylene inner tank inside a galvanized steel outer shell. The inner tank can't rust, which removes steel's main failure mode. Many of these stand upright rather than lying on their side, so they often fit tight basements that a long steel oval can't.

Fibreglass tanks are moulded from resin and glass fibre and don't rust either. They cost the most up front, and they make the most sense for coastal properties near Shediac, where salt air eats at bare steel from the outside.

| Tank type | Typical lifespan | Rusts? | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-wall steel | 10 to 20 years | Yes, from the inside out | Tight budgets, dry indoor spots |
| Double-bottom steel | 15 to 25 years | Yes, but a protected second layer | Most Moncton area homes |
| Double-wall (Roth style) | 25 to 30+ years | No | Tight basements, insurance-friendly |
| Fibreglass | 25 to 30+ years | No | Coastal properties, long-term owners |

## Where it sits: indoor, outdoor, and buried

Two identical steel tanks age very differently depending on where they sit. An indoor tank enjoys stable temperatures and never sees rain, snow load, or direct sun, so it typically outlasts an outdoor tank of the same material by five to ten years.

Outdoor tanks in our climate earn every grey hair. They swing from deep winter cold to summer heat, and every swing pulls more moist air in through the vent. Snow sliding off a roof can bend a fill pipe or a leg, and soft spring ground around Salisbury and Memramcook lets legs sink or tilt over time. Along the Northumberland Strait near Shediac, salt air speeds up exterior corrosion on top of everything else.

Buried tanks are their own category. They turn up at older properties anywhere in the region, often where a house switched from an underground tank to an above-ground one decades ago and the old one never came out. Steel underground corrodes from the soil side as well as the inside, out of sight the entire time, which is why a buried tank is the highest-stakes tank on this whole list. ## How long a home oil tank really lasts

There's no single number, but there is a reliable pattern. Single-wall steel typically runs 10 to 20 years, shorter outdoors and longer indoors. Double-bottom steel usually reaches 15 to 25 years. Fibreglass and double-wall tanks routinely go 25 to 30 years or more.

What surprises a lot of homeowners is that many tanks get retired because an insurer sets an age limit, not because the tank is leaking. That means the honest question isn't "how long can this tank physically last," it's "how long will anyone let me keep it." ## Sizing: what size tank does your home need

Four sizes cover almost every home in our service area. The 620 litre compact tank suits cottages, mini-homes, and small bungalows. The 910 litre steel oval, often called the 200 gallon tank (that's imperial gallons, about 240 US gallons), has been the standard install for decades. The 1,000 litre upright double-wall tank has become the common replacement choice because it fits tighter basements. And the 1,150 litre tank serves larger or draftier homes that burn through oil fast in a cold snap.

The right size depends on your coldest month, not your average one. Size for how many litres a day the house burns in a February cold snap and how often you're willing to book a delivery, not for the yearly total. ## The rules that apply to a home oil tank in New Brunswick

Three different sets of rules touch a home oil tank, and they carry very different weight.

The province regulates petroleum storage under the Clean Environment Act and its Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation, which is mainly aimed at larger systems. The province's [Petroleum Storage Tanks](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg/environment/content/land_waste/content/reference_manual/petroleum_storagetanks.html) page explains that storage sites with a total capacity of 2,000 litres or more must be registered and hold a petroleum storage site licence. A single house tank in the 620 to 1,150 litre range is below that line. A spill is a separate matter at any size: New Brunswick's 24-hour environmental emergency line is 1-800-565-1633.

New installations are supposed to follow CSA B139, the national installation code for oil-burning equipment, which covers where a tank sits, what it sits on, and how the vent, fill and oil lines are run. [CSA Group's summary of the current edition](https://www.csagroup.org/article/top-changes-in-csa-b139-installation-code-for-oil-burning-equipment/) lists changes that matter to homeowners: an annual visual inspection for leaks in fuel oil tanks, tubing, piping and filters is now required, and there are new requirements for removing a tank system that hasn't been used for more than three years.

Then there's your insurance company, which isn't a regulator at all but is, in practice, the strictest rule-setter you'll deal with. The age limit that decides your renewal comes from your insurer's own underwriting rules, and each company sets its own. Our post on [New Brunswick oil tank regulations](/blog/nb-oil-tank-regulations/) sorts out which rules come from where, including what to do if a spill happens.

## What your insurer will ask about your tank

Sooner or later, most home oil tanks get replaced because of a letter, not a leak. Insurers can't see inside a tank, so they lean on age and construction as a stand-in for risk: how old the tank is, whether it's single-wall or double-wall, and whether it was installed properly with paperwork to show for it.

Every company sets its own cutoff, but the pattern is similar: questions start around 15 years for single-wall steel, and many insurers want steel tanks replaced somewhere between 15 and 25 years, depending on the tank type. Fibreglass and double-wall tanks generally buy more room before that conversation starts.
