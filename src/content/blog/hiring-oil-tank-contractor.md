## Why Choosing an Oil Tank Removal Company Carefully Matters

An oil tank isn't a fence or a deck. If a fence job goes sideways, you're annoyed. If a tank job goes sideways, you can end up with heating oil in your soil, a claim your insurer won't touch, and a cleanup bill that dwarfs the original job.

Home heating oil is a regulated substance in New Brunswick. Spills have to be reported, contaminated soil has to be handled properly, and old tanks can't just go to the curb. The [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg.html) oversees petroleum storage rules in this province, and a legitimate contractor works inside those rules every day.

That's the real difference between companies. Anyone with a truck and a reciprocating saw can cut up an empty tank. What you're actually paying for is the judgment to drain it safely, spot a problem before it becomes a spill, and leave you with the paperwork that protects you later. When you sell your house, your buyer's lawyer may ask for proof the old tank was removed and disposed of properly. A receipt from a licensed company answers that question. A cash deal with no paper trail does not.

So treat the first phone call like a job interview. You're hiring someone to handle a small environmental risk on your property. It's worth ten minutes of questions.

## Licensing and Insurance: Ask These Questions First

Start here, because a wrong answer to any of these ends the conversation.

**Are you licensed to do this work in New Brunswick?** Oil burner and tank work in this province requires proper licensing. Ask who holds the licence and confirm it's current. A vague answer like "we've been doing this forever" is not a licence.

**Do you carry liability insurance, and does it cover environmental damage?** This is the big one. General liability insurance often excludes pollution. If a contractor punctures a tank line and oil hits your soil, you want their insurance responding, not your lawyer chasing a numbered company. Ask directly: "If oil spills during the job, whose insurance pays?" Watch how quickly they answer.

**Are your workers covered by WorkSafeNB?** If an uninsured worker gets hurt on your property, you can be exposed. A legitimate company will confirm coverage without blinking.

Marc in Shediac learned why this matters. He had a buried tank near his cottage and got a cash offer to dig it out for $1,800, no paperwork. He almost took it. Then he asked the insurance question and got a shrug. He hired an insured contractor instead for $3,600, and the soil test during excavation found a small leak that was caught and handled early. A full spill cleanup in a coastal area like Shediac can run past $50,000 once contaminated soil starts getting trucked away. His extra $1,800 bought him a working safety net, and he needed it.

The pattern is simple. The companies that cost a bit more usually cost more because they're carrying real coverage. The bargain quotes are often bargains because someone else, meaning you, is carrying the risk.

## How a Good Oil Tank Removal Contractor Handles the Job Itself

Once the paperwork questions check out, ask about the actual work. The answers tell you whether you're talking to a specialist or a general handyman who does tanks on the side.

**Walk me through the process.** For a standard removal, you should hear something like: pump out remaining oil, disconnect and cap the lines, remove the fill and vent pipes, drain and purge the tank, remove it, and haul it to a proper recycling facility. For a replacement, the sequence includes setting the new tank on a proper stand or pad and pressure-testing the new lines before the first fill. If the answer is "we just cut it up and take it away," keep shopping. Our [oil tank replacement service in Moncton](/oil-tank-replacement-moncton/) page describes what a full job looks like.

**What do you do if you find a leak?** This is the question that separates pros from pretenders. The right answer involves stopping, containing, assessing, and being straight with you about reporting obligations. In New Brunswick, spills must be reported, and federal environmental rules can apply too. [Environment and Climate Change Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change.html) and the province both take petroleum releases seriously. A contractor who says "we'd just bury it and keep going" is inviting you into a legal problem.

**Have you done buried tanks?** Underground tanks are a different animal. They involve excavation, soil assessment, and sometimes an environmental consultant. Peter in Salisbury had his above-ground tank replaced for $3,400 all-in, a new double-bottom tank on a fresh pad with new lines, done in one day. His brother-in-law in Memramcook had a buried tank, and that job took two days, an excavator, and soil samples. Both jobs went fine, but only because the contractor knew which job was which. If you have an underground tank, ask specifically about that experience, and read up on the process before you call anyone. Our [oil tank removal service](/oil-tank-removal-moncton/) covers both.

**Who does the work?** Some companies sell the job and subcontract it to whoever's available. That's not automatically bad, but you deserve to know whose boots will be on your property and whether they're covered by the same insurance you just asked about.

## Red Flags That Should Send You Somewhere Else

Some warning signs are worth ending the call over. Watch for these:

- **Cash only, no paperwork.** You lose the receipt, the warranty, and any proof the tank was disposed of legally. If something surfaces during a home sale years later, you have nothing.
- **Pressure to sign today.** Real companies in this region are busy, especially in fall. Anyone pushing a "today only" price on a tank job is selling urgency, not service.
- **No mention of your insurance situation.** A good contractor asks why you're removing the tank, because the answer changes the job. If your insurer wants proof of removal or a new tank installed to code, the contractor should know exactly what documentation your insurer expects. If they've never heard of an insurance letter, they haven't done many of these. Our post on [whether insurance covers oil tank leaks](/blog/does-insurance-cover-oil-tank-leaks/) explains why insurers care so much about tank age.
- **They badmouth the rules.** A contractor who calls provincial requirements "overkill" is telling you how they'll behave when nobody's watching. New Brunswick's tank rules exist because spills here have wrecked basements and wells. You can read more about what the province requires in our plain-language guide to [NB oil tank regulations](/blog/nb-oil-tank-regulations/).
- **No physical address or local track record.** Storm-chaser style operators show up in every trade. Ask where they're based and which neighbourhoods they've worked in. A local company can name streets.

None of these alone proves someone's a crook. Two or three together is a pattern, and you don't need to be the person who finds out for sure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many quotes should I get before choosing an oil tank company?

Two or three written quotes is plenty. More than that and you're burning weeks while your insurance deadline gets closer. Focus less on collecting numbers and more on comparing what each quote actually includes, especially disposal, oil pump-out, and paperwork.

### Do oil tank companies in New Brunswick need to be licensed?

Yes, oil burner and tank work in New Brunswick falls under provincial licensing and petroleum storage rules. Ask who on the crew holds the licence and confirm it's current. Also confirm liability insurance that covers environmental damage, because a licence without insurance still leaves you exposed.

### Can I remove an old oil tank myself to save money?

Draining, cutting, and disposing of an oil tank yourself is a bad bet. Even an "empty" tank holds sludge and vapours, disposal facilities often won't accept tanks from homeowners, and if oil hits the ground you own the cleanup. The few hundred dollars saved isn't worth a five-figure spill risk, so leave this one to a licensed pro.

### What paperwork should I get when the job is done?

At minimum, get an itemized invoice and a disposal receipt showing where the tank went. If you had a new tank installed, keep the tank's specifications and installation record too. Your insurance company and any future buyer of your home may ask for all of it, so keep the folder somewhere safe.
