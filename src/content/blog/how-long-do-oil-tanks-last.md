## The Short Answer: Oil Tank Lifespan at a Glance

If you just want the numbers, here they are. These ranges reflect what we actually see in basements and backyards across Westmorland, Albert and Kent counties, not what the sales sheet promises.

| Tank type and location | Typical lifespan | What usually ends it |
|---|---|---|
| Steel single-wall, outdoors | 10 to 15 years | Internal corrosion plus weather and salt air |
| Steel single-wall, indoors | 15 to 20 years | Internal corrosion from condensation |
| Steel double-bottom (Granby style) | 15 to 25 years | Insurance age limits, fittings and lines |
| Fibreglass or polyethylene double-wall (Roth style), indoors | 25 to 30+ years | Hardware wears out before the tank does |
| Double-wall, outdoors | 20 to 30 years | Weathered components, insurer limits |
| Buried steel tank | 10 to 20 years, often unknown | Soil-side corrosion you can't see |

Two things jump out from that table. First, location matters almost as much as material. Second, most tanks don't die of old age. They get retired by an insurance company or a fuel supplier who won't touch a tank past a certain age. For steel tanks in New Brunswick, 15 years is the number that matters most, because that's where many insurers draw the line.

## How Long Do Steel Oil Tanks Last?

Steel is what most of us have. The standard setup around here for decades was a 910 litre single-wall steel tank, either in the basement or sitting on legs outside the house. A single-wall steel tank is good for roughly 10 to 20 years, with indoor tanks landing at the top of that range and outdoor tanks at the bottom.

The part that surprises people is that steel tanks rust from the inside out. Every time the temperature swings, moist air gets pulled in through the vent and condenses inside the tank. Water is heavier than oil, so it sinks to the bottom and sits there, quietly eating the steel at its lowest point. That's why a tank can look perfectly fine on the outside and be paper thin along the bottom seam. It's also why the first visible drip is often the last warning you get.

Newer double-bottom steel tanks last longer because the design puts a second layer of protection exactly where tanks fail first. Many carry warranties in the 25 to 30 year range. Just know that a warranty and an insurance policy are two different documents, and your insurer's age limit usually wins.

Denise LeBlanc in Riverview learned how that plays out. Her 1998 single-wall tank had never leaked a drop, but her renewal arrived with a letter: replace the tank within 30 days or lose coverage. ## How Long Do Fibreglass and Double-Wall Oil Tanks Last?

This is where the lifespan story gets a lot better. Fibreglass and double-wall tanks routinely last 25 to 30 years or more, and most of the ones we remove are being replaced for insurance paperwork or renovations, not failure.

A Roth style double-wall tank pairs a seam-free polyethylene inner tank with a galvanized steel outer shell that can hold 110 percent of the contents if the inner tank ever fails. The plastic inner tank can't rust, which removes the single biggest killer of steel tanks. Manufacturers typically back them for 30 years. Granby's double-bottom steel tanks take a different approach to the same problem and also carry long warranties.

You'll pay more upfront, usually $500 to $1,000 over a basic steel tank. In our experience it's money well spent if you plan to stay in the house, because you buy a longer service life, a built-in leak backstop, and an easier conversation with your insurance company at every renewal.

One caution: a 30 year tank doesn't mean 30 years of ignoring it. Oil lines, filters, gauges, vents and fittings all age on their own schedule, and a $40 fitting can cause the same mess as a rusted seam. An annual look-over still matters, whatever your tank is made of.

## Indoor vs Outdoor vs Buried: Location Changes Everything

Take two identical steel tanks, put one in a basement and one outside, and the indoor tank will typically outlive the outdoor one by five to ten years. Inside, the tank enjoys stable temperatures, which means less condensation, and it never sees rain, snow load or UV.

Outdoor tanks in our climate get worked hard. They cycle from minus 25 in January to plus 28 in July, and every swing pulls more moist air inside. Snow sliding off a roof can bend fill pipes and legs. Soft spring ground lets legs sink and tilt, which shifts the oil away from the outlet and stresses the seams. None of that is fatal on its own, but it all shortens the clock.

Buried tanks are their own category, and the honest answer on lifespan is that nobody knows, because nobody can see them. Steel in wet soil corrodes from the outside as well as the inside, and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's [underground storage tank program](https://www.epa.gov/ust) exists precisely because buried tanks fail quietly and contaminate soil and groundwater before anyone notices. If your house went up before the 1980s anywhere in Southeast New Brunswick, there's a real chance a steel tank is sleeping under the lawn.

Paulette Melanson found that out after buying a 1962 house in Memramcook. Her home inspector spotted an old fill pipe beside the foundation. The buried tank had supposedly been retired decades ago, but it still held about 200 litres of oil and sludge. The dig-out, disposal and soil testing came to roughly $6,800. That stung, but a buried tank that's actively leaking can cost several times that once contaminated soil starts leaving in dump trucks. ## Signs Your Oil Tank Is Near the End

Age is the biggest predictor, but tanks usually give warnings before they let go. Walk out and look for these:

- Rust blisters, flaking or bubbling paint, especially along the bottom seam
- Pitting deep enough to catch a fingernail
- A wet, oily sheen or dark staining on the tank bottom or the ground below it
- A persistent oil smell indoors or around the tank
- Legs that are leaning, sinking or rusted at the welds
- Dents or creases from ice, snow or impacts
- A gauge that sticks or readings that don't match your fuel deliveries

Any one of these on a tank past ten years is a reason to get a professional opinion. If you're not sure of the age, the manufacturer's plate usually tells you, and our guide on [how old your oil tank is](/blog/how-old-is-my-oil-tank/) shows exactly where to look. Here's the safety line: if a tank is weeping, dripping or smells strongly of oil, stop there. Don't sand it, patch it, or put a wrench on the fittings, because one wrong turn can open a seep into a steady stream. Put a pan under the drip, keep ignition sources away, and call a professional. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can an oil tank really last 30 years?

Yes, but usually only a fibreglass or double-wall tank installed indoors. Steel single-wall tanks almost never make it that far in our climate, and one that has is living on borrowed time. Keep in mind that even a healthy 30 year old tank can be refused by insurers or fuel suppliers based on age alone.

### Will my insurance company drop me if my tank is over 15 years old?

Many insurers in New Brunswick set age limits of 15 to 20 years for steel tanks and 25 to 30 years for double-wall or fibreglass models, though every company writes its own rules. Some ask for an inspection, others simply demand replacement before renewal. Replacing an aging tank before your renewal date keeps the decision in your hands instead of theirs.

### How do I find out how old my oil tank is?

Check the manufacturer's nameplate or stamped markings, usually on the top or upper end of the tank, which include the year it was built. If the plate is rusted or painted over, your fuel supplier's records, past home inspection reports, or installation permits can often pin down the age. If nothing turns up, treat the tank as old and have it inspected.

### Is it worth replacing a tank that isn't leaking yet?

Usually, yes, once a steel tank passes the 15 year mark. Replacing early costs about the same as replacing late, but you get to choose the day, avoid emergency rates, and skip the risk of a five-figure cleanup. A new tank also smooths out insurance renewals and adds a selling point if you ever list the house.
