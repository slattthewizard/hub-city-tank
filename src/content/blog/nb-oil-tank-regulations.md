## Who Sets New Brunswick Oil Tank Regulations?

Here's the thing that surprises most homeowners: there isn't one single rulebook. The rules that govern your heating oil tank come from three different places, and they don't all carry the same weight.

**The province.** New Brunswick regulates petroleum storage under the Clean Environment Act and its Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation. You can read the regulation itself on the [Government of New Brunswick's laws site](https://laws.gnb.ca/en/showdoc/cr/87-97). Most of it is aimed at large commercial storage systems, like the big tanks at fuel depots and gas stations. A typical 910 litre tank beside your house doesn't get registered with the province the way a commercial system does.

That said, two provincial rules absolutely do apply to you. First, if your tank leaks, you have a legal duty to report the spill. Second, you're responsible for the cleanup, and the [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg.html) can order remediation on your property. More on both of those below, because this is where the real money is.

**National installation codes.** Every new oil tank installation in Canada is supposed to follow CSA B139, the installation code for oil-burning equipment. New Brunswick adopts it through its building and fire codes, and licensed installers work to it every day. This is the code that decides where your tank can sit, what it sits on, and how the oil line runs to your furnace.

**Your insurance company.** Legally speaking, your insurer isn't a regulator at all. Practically speaking, it's the strictest one you'll ever deal with. No provincial inspector is going to knock on your door about a 20 year old tank. Your insurance company will, and they'll put a deadline on it.

## The Installation Rules: What CSA B139 Means at Your House

You don't need to read the code yourself. That's your installer's job. But it helps to know what a compliant installation looks like, because it's exactly what your insurance company's inspection form is checking for.

A residential oil tank installed to code in New Brunswick will generally have:

- A tank certified to a recognized standard, such as ULC S602 for steel tanks or ULC S615 for fibreglass. Look for the certification plate on the tank itself.
- A stable, level, non-combustible base. Patio stones sinking into soft lawn don't cut it. A leaning tank stresses every fitting on it.
- Proper clearances from windows, doors, air intakes, and property lines.
- A protected oil supply line, sleeved where it runs through or under concrete.
- A vent line with a whistle, so your delivery driver knows when the tank is full and can't accidentally overfill it.
- Installation by a licensed oil burner technician, with documentation you can hand to your insurer.

## NB Oil Tank Rules Your Insurer Adds on Top

This is the layer that generates most of the phone calls we get. Insurance companies across New Brunswick have tightened their oil heat requirements over the past decade, because a single oil spill claim can cost them more than a house fire.

The exact rules vary by company, but the common ones look like this:

- **Age limits.** Many insurers want outdoor single-wall steel tanks replaced somewhere between 15 and 20 years of age. Fibreglass and double-bottom tanks often get 20 to 25 years. If you don't know your tank's age, the date is usually stamped on the certification plate.
- **Proof of professional installation.** An invoice or inspection form from a licensed technician.
- **Photos and inspection forms.** Some companies send their own checklist and want photos of the tank, the legs, the fuel line, and the filter.
- **No visible rust, dents, or oil staining.** Surface rust on a steel tank is the single most common reason a renewal gets flagged.

## Leaks and Spills: What the Law Actually Requires

Here's where provincial regulation stops being background noise and becomes very real. If heating oil escapes from your tank or your fuel line, New Brunswick law requires the spill to be reported. In the Maritimes, the 24-hour environmental emergencies line is 1-800-565-1633. Reporting isn't optional, and it isn't something to put off until Monday.

Just as important: you, the property owner, are responsible for the cleanup. Not the tank manufacturer, not the fuel company, you. Contaminated soil has to be excavated and disposed of properly, and if oil reaches a basement sump, a well, or a neighbour's property, the scope grows fast. Minor residential cleanups in our area commonly run $15,000 to $50,000. Serious ones, where oil has travelled, can pass $100,000. Whether your insurance covers any of that depends heavily on your policy and on how the leak happened.

So what should you actually do if you smell oil or find a wet spot under the tank? Three things, in this order. Shut off the tank's supply valve if you can reach it safely. Report the spill. Then call a professional, because this is firmly past the DIY line. Soaking up a cup of oil with kitty litter is fine. Diagnosing why a tank is weeping through a rust blister is not. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there a legal age limit for oil tanks in New Brunswick?

No. The province doesn't set a law that says a tank must be replaced at a specific age. Age limits come from insurance companies, which commonly require replacement of steel tanks somewhere between 15 and 25 years depending on the tank type. In practice, the insurer's limit is the one that matters, because you can't keep a mortgage without coverage.

### Do I need a permit to replace a residential oil tank?

In most Greater Moncton installations, you won't be pulling paperwork yourself. Your licensed installer works to CSA B139 and handles any documentation your municipality or insurer requires. What you should insist on is a proper invoice and installation record with the technician's licence information, because that's what your insurance company will ask to see.

### What do I do if my oil tank is leaking right now?

Close the tank's supply valve if you can do it safely, then report the spill to the 24-hour environmental emergencies line at 1-800-565-1633. Don't try to patch, drain, or move the tank yourself. Call our 24/7 emergency line at (506) 802-3471 and we'll walk you through the next steps and get someone out to you.

### Can I remove my old oil tank myself?

We'd strongly advise against it. Even an empty tank holds residual oil and sludge that has to be pumped out and disposed of at an approved facility, and one dropped or punctured tank can turn into a reportable spill on your own lawn. A professional removal also gives you the paperwork trail that insurers and home buyers expect to see.
