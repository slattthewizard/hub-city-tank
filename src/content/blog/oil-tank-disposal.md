## First, the Tank Has to Come Out Safely

Before anyone talks about disposal, the tank has to be emptied and disconnected the right way. That's not a five-minute job, and it's where most DIY attempts go sideways.

A proper crew starts by pumping out whatever oil is left. Even a tank that reads empty on the gauge usually holds 20 to 80 litres of oil and sludge below the outlet. That liquid has to go into sealed containers, not a bucket from the garage. Then the fill pipe, vent pipe, and oil line get disconnected and capped so nothing drips into your basement or onto your lawn.

Only then does the tank actually move. Basement tanks often need to be tipped, drained again, and sometimes cut down to fit through a doorway or up a set of stairs. Outdoor tanks are simpler, but frozen ground, snowbanks, and tight side yards in older Moncton neighbourhoods keep things interesting.

## How Oil Tank Disposal Works, Step by Step

Once the tank is on the trailer, here's the road it travels.

**1. Leftover oil gets pumped and stored.** Usable heating oil can often be transferred into your new tank if you're replacing, so you don't pay twice for the same fuel. Oil that's too old or contaminated goes into sealed drums for proper processing.

**2. Sludge gets separated.** The bottom of an old tank holds a thick mix of rust, water, and degraded oil. That sludge is regulated waste in New Brunswick. It can't go in the garbage, down a drain, or onto the ground. It travels in sealed containers to a licensed waste oil handler.

**3. The tank gets cleaned and de-gassed.** Before anyone cuts steel, the tank has to be free of flammable vapours. Residual fumes inside an "empty" tank can ignite from a single spark, which is why professional facilities flush and ventilate tanks until they're safe to cut.

**4. The steel gets cut or crushed.** Once clean, the tank is cut open, flattened, or sheared into pieces. This also proves to the recycler that there's no liquid trapped inside.

**5. The steel gets recycled.** Clean tank steel goes into the scrap stream with car bodies and appliances, then off to a mill where it's melted down. Your old tank might come back as rebar, a beam, or honestly, part of somebody's new oil tank.

The whole chain exists for one reason: heating oil is a hydrocarbon, and hydrocarbons don't belong in soil or groundwater. The federal rules on [pollution and waste management](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/environment/pollution-waste-management.html) and New Brunswick's [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg/environment.html) both treat waste petroleum as a controlled substance, and the disposal chain is built to keep it contained at every step.

## What Happens to the Leftover Oil and Sludge?

This is the part people underestimate. The steel is easy. The liquid is the problem.

Take Monique in Shediac. She bought a summer place near Parlee Beach with an old 700-litre tank sitting in the garage, supposedly empty. When our crew tipped it, 60 litres of sour, watery oil sloshed toward the outlet. Left alone, that tank would have eventually rusted through and drained onto the garage slab and into the sandy soil below. Her removal and disposal, including pumping and handling that liquid, came to $475. A spill cleanup on the same property could easily have run into five figures.

Here's what happens to each type of liquid.

**Good oil** gets checked and, if it's clean, transferred to your new tank or credited. There's no sense paying to destroy fuel you already bought.

**Degraded oil** goes to licensed waste oil processors, the same system that handles used motor oil in the province. Much of it is re-refined or blended into industrial fuel. It doesn't just get burned in a barrel somewhere.

**Sludge and rinse water** are the nastiest part. They're handled as contaminated liquid and processed at facilities set up for petroleum waste. This is also why the "just pour a little diesel in and rinse it out" advice you'll hear at the hardware store is so dangerous. Every litre of that rinse water is now contaminated too.

## Why DIY Oil Tank Disposal Isn't Worth the Risk

Every spring, somebody in Greater Moncton loads an old tank onto a utility trailer, drives it to a scrap yard, and gets turned away at the scale. Most yards won't touch a residential oil tank unless it's been professionally cleaned and cut open, because they've been burned before by tanks with liquid still inside.

That leaves the DIYer with a worse problem than they started with: a heavy, half-full tank they still legally own, sitting on a borrowed trailer, often with oil weeping from a fitting.

The risks stack up fast:

- **Spills in transit.** If oil leaks from your trailer onto a road or driveway, the cleanup is your responsibility. In New Brunswick, petroleum spills also have to be reported to the province.
- **Fire and explosion.** Cutting into an uncleaned tank with a grinder or torch is genuinely dangerous. Oil vapour inside a confined steel shell is an explosion waiting for a spark.
- **Back injuries and property damage.** An empty 900-litre steel tank weighs roughly 100 to 150 kilograms. Add sludge and it's heavier, awkward, and top-heavy on stairs.
- **No paper trail.** When you sell your house, a buyer's inspector may ask what happened to the old tank. A disposal receipt from a licensed contractor answers that question. "My brother-in-law took it somewhere" does not.

Our honest advice: draining a filter or tightening a fitting is homeowner territory. The moment a job involves moving, cutting, or transporting a tank, stop and call a professional. The few hundred dollars you spend buys insured handling and a receipt that protects you for years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I sell my old oil tank for scrap?

Technically yes, but the scrap value of a residential tank is only about $20 to $50, and most yards won't accept one unless it's been professionally cleaned and cut open. By the time you pay to have that done, you're behind. Bundling disposal with removal or replacement is almost always the cheaper route.

### Do I need a permit to dispose of an oil tank in New Brunswick?

Homeowners don't typically need a permit to remove a standard above-ground residential tank, but the waste oil and sludge must go through licensed handlers, and spills have to be reported to the province. Buried tanks can involve extra requirements, especially if contamination is found. A licensed contractor handles that paperwork as part of the job.

### How long does oil tank removal and disposal take?

A straightforward outdoor tank usually takes one to two hours on site. Basement tanks run two to four hours depending on access, and buried tanks are typically a full day once excavation is involved. The disposal itself happens off site afterward, so your part is done when the trailer leaves.

### What happens to the heating oil left in my old tank?

If the oil is clean, it can usually be transferred into your new tank so you don't lose fuel you've paid for. Oil that's degraded or contaminated with water and rust goes to a licensed waste oil processor, where much of it is re-refined. Either way, it should never be dumped, burned in a barrel, or poured down a drain.
