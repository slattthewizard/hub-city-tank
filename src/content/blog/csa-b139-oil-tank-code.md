## What Is CSA B139 and Why Does Your Oil Tank Have to Follow It?

CSA B139 is the Installation Code for Oil-Burning Equipment, published by the CSA Group (formerly the Canadian Standards Association). It sets out how oil-fired furnaces, boilers, and their fuel storage tanks must be installed across Canada. Think of it the way you'd think of the electrical code. You wouldn't want a panel wired by guesswork, and you don't want 900 litres of furnace oil stored by guesswork either.

On its own, a CSA standard is just a document. What gives it teeth in New Brunswick is that provincial rules and industry practice adopt it. The province regulates petroleum storage under the [Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation](https://laws.gnb.ca/en/showdoc/cr/87-97) made under the Clean Environment Act, and the [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg/environment/content/land_waste/content/petroleum_storage.html) oversees petroleum storage in the province. Licensed installers, insurers, and fuel suppliers all treat B139 as the baseline for a legal residential install.

Here's the practical version. If your tank was installed by a licensed professional to CSA B139, you have a system that's safer, insurable, and sellable. If it wasn't, you may not find out until the worst possible moment: an insurance renewal, a home sale, or a leak.

## The CSA B139 Oil Tank Requirements That Matter Most at Home

The full code covers everything from commercial boiler rooms to piping design. For a house in Dieppe or Riverview, a handful of requirements do most of the work. Here's what a compliant residential setup looks like.

**An approved tank.** The tank itself has to be built and certified to a recognized standard, such as ULC standards for steel and fibreglass tanks. A homemade tank, a repurposed drum, or a tank with no legible certification label doesn't qualify. If the label has rusted off, expect questions from your insurer.

**A stable, level base.** The tank must sit level on a solid, stable foundation that won't shift, settle, or wash out. Outdoors in our climate, that usually means a proper concrete pad or engineered base, not a couple of patio stones dropped on topsoil. Frost heave is brutal in Southeast New Brunswick, and a leaning tank puts stress right where the oil line connects. We cover this in detail in our guide to the [oil tank stand and pad](/blog/oil-tank-stand-and-pad/).

**Proper fill and vent piping.** The fill pipe has to be sized and positioned so your delivery driver can fill the tank safely, and the vent has to let air escape as oil comes in. The vent needs a whistle, that alarm you hear during a delivery. When the whistle stops, the driver stops. No whistle means no warning, and overfills are one of the most common causes of residential spills.

**A protected oil supply line.** The line running from tank to burner is a known weak point, especially copper lines run under a concrete floor. Modern installs protect the line with a sleeve or run it overhead, and include a shut-off valve at the tank and a filter to keep sludge out of your burner.

**Clearances and location.** The code sets minimum distances between an indoor tank and the oil-fired appliance, keeps tanks away from ignition sources, and requires outdoor fills and vents to terminate away from windows, doors, and fresh-air intakes. Outdoor tanks also need to be protected from things like sliding roof snow and ice, a real issue here every winter.

None of this is exotic. It's the difference between a tank that quietly does its job for 20 years and one that fails early or fails dangerously.

## How to Tell If Your Current Tank Meets Code

You can't certify your own tank, but a ten-minute walk-around tells you a lot. Here's what to look for.

**Find the label.** Every approved tank has a certification plate or sticker showing the standard it was built to, the manufacturer, and usually a date. If you can read it, note the year. If it's rusted beyond reading, that's a flag on its own, and there are other ways to date a tank, like the serial number or your fuel supplier's records.

**Check the base and the lean.** Stand back and sight along the tank. It should look level and solid. Any visible lean, sinking leg, or wobble is a call-us-now item, because the oil line is under stress.

**Look at the vent and fill.** The vent should stand above typical snow depth and have a whistle inside. The fill should be accessible and capped, away from windows and intakes.

**Scan for rust, weeps, and wet spots.** Surface rust on top is common. Rust blistering along the bottom seam, a shiny or damp patch, or any oil smell means stop reading and call. Tanks corrode from the inside out, so the bottom is where trouble shows first.

**Check the paper trail.** If you have an invoice from a licensed installer, keep it with your insurance documents. If you have nothing, an inspection report from a licensed oil tank contractor is the next best thing, and insurers generally accept it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is CSA B139 actually the law in New Brunswick?

CSA B139 is a national installation code that becomes enforceable when provinces, insurers, and licensing bodies adopt it, and in New Brunswick it's the accepted standard for oil-burning equipment installs. The province also has its own petroleum storage regulation layered on top. In practice, any licensed installer working on your tank is required to follow it, and insurers treat it as the definition of a proper install.

### Can I install my own oil tank if I follow the code myself?

We don't recommend it, and your insurer almost certainly won't accept it. Even a technically tidy DIY install leaves you with no licensed installer's documentation, which is what insurance companies and home buyers actually ask for. If a DIY-installed tank ever leaks, you can expect a coverage fight at the worst possible time. Handle the visual checks yourself and leave the wrenches to a licensed pro.

### How do I prove my tank was installed to CSA B139?

The gold standard is the invoice or installation record from the licensed contractor who did the work, showing the date, tank details, and installer information. If you've lost it or never had it, a written inspection report from a licensed oil tank contractor documenting the tank's condition and setup is usually enough for insurers. Keep whichever document you have with your insurance papers, because you'll be asked for it again.

### My tank works fine but doesn't meet code. Do I really have to replace it?

Not always, and nobody can force you overnight, but the pressure tends to arrive on someone else's schedule: an insurance renewal, a home sale, or a delivery driver refusing to fill. Some issues, like a missing whistle or an exposed line, can be corrected without replacing the tank. Others, like an uncertified or badly deteriorated tank, only have one real fix.
