## What Oil Heat Costs to Run in Greater Moncton

Oil heat has three cost buckets: fuel, maintenance, and the equipment itself.

**Fuel is the big one.** Furnace oil prices in NB move with world markets, and they move fast. Over the past several years we've seen prices swing from under $1.10 a litre to over $1.90. At 2,300 litres a year, that swing alone is the difference between about $2,500 and $4,400 for the exact same house. That unpredictability is a real cost, even if it never shows up as a single line on a bill.

**Maintenance is steady and modest.** A yearly tune-up runs $150 to $250 and keeps your furnace burning at its rated efficiency, usually 82 to 87 percent for units installed in the last two decades. Skip the tune-up and a sooted-up burner can quietly eat an extra 10 percent of your fuel.

**Equipment is where oil shines.** If your furnace is in decent shape, keeping oil heat alive usually means replacing the tank once every 15 to 25 years. A new Granby steel or Roth double-wall tank, installed and inspected, typically runs $3,000 to $5,500 in our area. ## What a Heat Pump Costs to Run on NB Power Rates

A heat pump doesn't make heat, it moves it. Even at minus 15, there's heat energy in outdoor air, and a cold-climate unit squeezes it inside. For every kilowatt-hour of electricity it uses, it typically delivers two to three kilowatt-hours of heat over a New Brunswick winter.

Natural Resources Canada's guide to [heating and cooling with a heat pump](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/energy-star-canada/about/energy-star-announcements/publications/heating-and-cooling-heat-pump/23621) is worth a read if you want the engineering details. The short version for our climate: expect a seasonal average of roughly 2 to 2.5 units of heat per unit of electricity, not the 3 or 4 you'll see quoted for milder provinces.

Now the dollars. A typical Moncton-area home needs somewhere around 18,000 to 20,000 kilowatt-hours of heat per year. Delivered by a cold-climate heat pump at a seasonal average of 2.3, that's roughly 8,000 to 8,700 kilowatt-hours of electricity. At NB Power's residential rate of about 15 cents per kilowatt-hour, you're looking at $1,200 to $1,300 a year.

Real life adds a bit on top. Output drops on the coldest days, so backup heat kicks in, whether that's electric baseboards at full price per unit of heat or your oil furnace. Budget $1,300 to $1,800 a year in total heating electricity for a well-installed system, plus $100 to $200 for an annual cleaning.

Tanya in Riverview is a good example of how this plays out. She had two mini-split heads installed for about $9,800 and kept her oil furnace for backup. Her oil use dropped from roughly 2,400 litres a year to about 900, saving her around $2,100 in fuel at $1.45 a litre, while her power bill went up about $1,000. Net savings: about $1,100 a year, and a cooler house in July as a bonus.

## Why Most Greater Moncton Homes End Up With Both

Walk any street in Dieppe or Shediac and you'll see the pattern: a heat pump head on the wall and an oil fill pipe on the foundation. That's not indecision. That's the setup that fits our climate.

A hybrid home runs the heat pump for most of the season, when it's cheap and efficient. When temperatures dive below about minus 20 and the heat pump's output sags, the oil furnace takes over and delivers full heat no matter what the thermometer says. You burn maybe a third of the oil you used to, and you never lie awake during an ice storm wondering if the house will hold temperature.

The Boudreau family in Shediac learned this the reassuring way. During a February cold snap two winters back, their two-head mini-split system couldn't hold the house above 17 degrees on its own at minus 26 with the wind chill. When their tank hit year 22 last summer, replacing it for $3,100 was an easy call.

There's a power-outage angle too. Heat pumps and electric backup both die with the grid. An oil furnace still needs electricity for its burner and blower, but it can run on a small generator that would never keep up with electric heat.

One caution if you go hybrid: an oil tank that only gets topped up once a year needs a little more attention, not less. Condensation builds in a tank that sits half empty through the summer, and water in the tank leads to corrosion from the inside out. Keep it full in the off-season, get it looked at during your furnace tune-up, and never ignore a wet spot or an oil smell. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it cheaper to heat with oil or a heat pump in New Brunswick?

A cold-climate heat pump is cheaper to run for most NB homes, typically $1,300 to $1,800 a year in electricity versus $3,000 to $3,800 for oil. Oil is much cheaper up front, since a new tank costs $3,000 to $5,500 while a full heat pump conversion can top $20,000. Over ten years, a well-sized heat pump usually comes out ahead, especially with rebates. The gap shrinks for smaller homes or homes that need lots of backup heat.

### Should I replace my oil tank if I'm planning to switch to a heat pump?

If the switch is happening this year and your tank is sound, you can often wait. But if your tank is past 15 to 20 years old, showing rust, or flagged by your insurer, replace it rather than gambling on a leak. A leak can cost tens of thousands in cleanup and would wipe out a decade of heat pump savings overnight. A new tank also keeps the hybrid option open, which is what most Greater Moncton homes choose anyway.

### Can a heat pump handle a New Brunswick winter on its own?

A properly sized cold-climate heat pump can carry a well-insulated home through most of the winter. Below about minus 20 to minus 25, output drops and some form of backup heat has to fill the gap. That backup can be electric resistance, which is pricey to run, or an existing oil furnace. Homes with older insulation or open layouts on the coast, like Shediac, tend to lean on backup more often.

### What does it cost to fully switch from oil to a heat pump?

Plan for $16,000 to $25,000 for a ducted cold-climate system, or $8,000 to $14,000 for multiple ductless heads, before rebates. Then add proper decommissioning of the oil system, including tank removal, which typically runs several hundred to a couple thousand dollars depending on access. Rebate programs in NB can knock thousands off the total, and they change often, so check what's current before you sign anything.
