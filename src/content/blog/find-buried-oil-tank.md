## Why Buried Oil Tanks Keep Turning Up Around Moncton

Oil heat has deep roots in Westmorland, Albert, and Kent counties. For decades it was the default way to keep a house warm through a New Brunswick winter, and in the middle of the last century, burying the tank was considered the tidy option. It freed up basement space, kept the yard looking clean, and nobody worried much about what steel does after fifty years in wet clay soil.

We know better now. Buried steel tanks corrode from the outside in, which means you can't see the damage getting worse. When one finally lets go, heating oil moves into the soil and groundwater fast, and cleanup gets expensive just as fast. Corroded underground tanks are such a widespread problem that the U.S. EPA runs an entire [underground storage tank program](https://www.epa.gov/ust) dedicated to them, and the same physics applies on this side of the border.

That's why insurers, lenders, and real estate lawyers in New Brunswick treat an unknown buried tank as a serious red flag. Many insurance companies won't write or renew a policy until the tank is confirmed gone or properly dealt with.

## 8 Signs a Property Has a Buried Oil Tank

No single clue proves anything on its own. But when two or three of these show up together, take it seriously.

1. **A capped pipe sticking out of the ground.** Fill and vent pipes are usually one to two inches wide, made of steel, and sit within a metre or two of the foundation. Look behind shrubs, under decks, and along the driveway side of the house where the delivery truck would've parked.

2. **Copper lines through the foundation wall below grade.** Oil lines from an outdoor buried tank enter the basement low on the wall. Even if the lines are gone, the patched holes often aren't.

3. **Patches in the basement floor or walls.** Concrete scars near the furnace area can mark where old oil lines or a tank pit used to be.

4. **An oil-heat history with no visible tank.** If the house has a chimney thimble, an old oil furnace, or oil stains in the mechanical room but no tank anywhere on the property, ask where the oil used to come from.

5. **A dip or mound in the lawn.** Soil settles over a buried tank as it corrodes, and it also settles over a hole that was backfilled after a sloppy removal. Either way, that low spot is telling you something.

6. **Dead grass, stained soil, or a petroleum smell.** A patch of lawn that won't grow, an oily sheen in puddles after rain, or a diesel-like smell near the foundation can point to a tank that's already leaking.

7. **Old fuel company stickers and delivery records.** Check the furnace, electrical panel, and basement door frames for delivery stickers. Past fuel bills or service tags are a paper trail worth following.

8. **The age of the house.** Homes built before about 1975 that heated with oil are the prime candidates. Newer builds almost never have underground tanks.

## How to Find a Buried Oil Tank: A Step-by-Step Check

Here's the order we'd suggest for checking a property yourself before spending a dime.

**Step 1: Walk the perimeter.** Circle the house slowly and look for pipes, caps, or threaded fittings poking out of the soil near the foundation. Pay extra attention to the side facing the street or driveway, and don't skip the awkward spots under decks and behind hedges.

**Step 2: Head to the basement.** Check the walls near the furnace for capped copper lines, patched penetrations low on the wall, or an old fuel filter bracket with nothing attached to it.

**Step 3: Pull the paper trail.** Ask the seller or previous owner directly. Call local fuel suppliers to ask if the address has a delivery history. Check with the City of Moncton, Dieppe, or Riverview, or your local service district, for any old permits tied to tank installation or removal.

**Step 4: Ask the neighbours.** Houses built in the same era on the same street often got the same setup. A long-time neighbour who remembers oil trucks filling a pipe in the yard is better evidence than any gadget.

**Step 5: Resist the urge to dig or probe.** Pushing a steel rod into the ground blindly can puncture a corroded tank or hit a gas, power, or water line. Always request utility locates before any digging, and honestly, this is the point where DIY should stop.

**Step 6: Book a professional scan.** A trained technician with a magnetometer or metal detection gear can sweep a typical yard in under an hour and confirm whether a tank is present, roughly how big it is, and how deep it sits. You get a written result you can hand to your insurer or lawyer.

One more thing. If at any point you smell oil, see a sheen on standing water, or find soil that's wet and smells like diesel, stop everything. A suspected leak is an emergency, not a weekend project. ## Found a Buried Oil Tank? Here's What to Do Next

First, don't panic. Finding a tank doesn't mean it's leaking. Plenty of buried tanks come out of the ground intact. But don't ignore it either, because the risk only grows the longer it sits.

Your realistic options come down to two:

**Removal.** The tank is pumped out, excavated, and hauled away, and the soil underneath gets checked. This is what most insurers, lenders, and buyers' lawyers want to see, because it comes with proof the problem is gone. Our [buried oil tank removal service in Moncton](/buried-oil-tank-removal-moncton/) handles the excavation, disposal, and documentation in one job.

**Abandonment in place.** The tank is pumped, cleaned, and filled with an inert material like sand or foam, then left in the ground. This is generally reserved for tanks sitting under additions, garages, or anywhere digging would damage the structure. It's cheaper up front, but some insurers still aren't satisfied by it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to find a buried oil tank?

A professional scan in Greater Moncton typically runs $250 to $600, and a basic visual assessment is sometimes free. Adding lab-tested soil samples usually brings the total to somewhere between $700 and $1,500. Compared to the cost of an undiscovered leak, it's some of the cheapest insurance a homeowner can buy.

### Can I just leave a buried oil tank in the ground?

Sometimes, but it's rarely the best move. Abandonment in place, where the tank is pumped, cleaned, and filled with inert material, is normally reserved for tanks that can't be excavated without damaging a structure. Most insurers and buyers' lawyers prefer full removal with clean soil results, so get professional advice before you choose.

### Who is responsible for a buried oil tank when a house is sold?

In practice, the current owner owns the problem, which is why buyers should make a scan a condition of sale. Once you take title, the tank and any contamination that comes from it are yours to deal with. Sellers who knew about a tank and stayed quiet can face legal trouble later, so talk to your lawyer about disclosure.

### How deep are buried oil tanks usually?

Most residential tanks sit two to five feet below grade, close to the foundation on the side the delivery truck could reach. The fill and vent pipes usually sit directly above or very near the tank itself. That's shallow enough for a magnetometer scan to locate them reliably in a single visit.
