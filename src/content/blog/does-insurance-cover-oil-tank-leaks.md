## Does Insurance Cover Oil Tank Leaks? The Short Answer

Most standard home insurance policies in New Brunswick will cover an oil leak only if it's sudden and accidental, and only if you've held up your end of the policy. That's the key phrase to remember: sudden and accidental.

A tank that splits open overnight because a snowplow clipped it? That's sudden and accidental. A tank that's been quietly weeping oil through a pinhole for two years because it rusted from the inside out? Insurers call that gradual damage or wear and tear, and most policies exclude it outright.

Here's the part that catches people off guard. Even a genuinely sudden leak can be denied if the tank broke a policy condition. The most common conditions we see in the Moncton area are:

- The tank was older than the insurer's age limit, usually 15 to 25 years depending on the company
- The tank wasn't installed by a licensed technician to code
- The homeowner knew about rust, dents, or a wet spot and didn't act on it
- The tank or oil line didn't meet the current installation standard

So the real answer to "does insurance cover oil tank leaks" is this: yes, if the leak is sudden, the tank meets your policy's conditions, and you can prove it. That's a lot of ifs. The good news is that every one of those ifs is within your control before a leak ever happens.

## Why Your Insurer Keeps Asking About Your Tank's Age

Insurance companies aren't being difficult for fun. Residential oil spills are among the most expensive claims in Atlantic Canada. A single leak that reaches the soil under a home can cost more to clean up than the house is worth, because contaminated soil has to be excavated, trucked away, and replaced, and sometimes the foundation comes up with it.

Steel tanks corrode from the inside, where condensation collects at the bottom and mixes with sludge. That means a tank can look fine on the outside while it's paper thin at the base. Since insurers can't see inside your tank, they use age as a proxy for risk. Once a tank crosses their age threshold, the odds of an interior failure jump, so they either demand a replacement, add an exclusion, or decline to renew.

Take Gilles in Memramcook. His renewal arrived last October with a note saying his 26 year old steel tank had to go within 30 days or his policy wouldn't renew. He was frustrated, and fair enough, the tank had never leaked a drop.

But when we cut the old tank apart after removal, the bottom third was flaking rust you could crumble with your fingers. His new tank and installation came to $3,400, his insurer confirmed coverage the same week, and he heats his home now without that letter hanging over him.

That's the typical pattern. The letter feels like a threat, but it's usually the cheapest warning you'll ever get. ## When Home Insurance Covers Oil Tank Leaks (and When It Won't)

Let's get specific about how claims actually play out, because the difference between covered and denied often comes down to details homeowners never think about until it's too late.

**Usually covered:** a sudden rupture or puncture of a tank that meets your policy conditions, oil damage to your own building and belongings from that sudden event, and in many policies, third party liability if your oil migrates onto a neighbour's property. Liability coverage matters more than people realize. In a tight Moncton neighbourhood or along the shore in Shediac, oil doesn't respect property lines.

**Usually denied:** slow leaks from corrosion, leaks from a tank past the insurer's age limit, leaks from tanks with known defects you ignored, spills during a DIY tank move, and contamination discovered when you sell the house years after the leak started. Some policies also cap environmental cleanup at a fraction of what a real remediation costs, so even an approved claim can leave you short.

Karen in Riverview is the near miss story we tell most often. She noticed a shiny patch on the concrete pad under her outdoor tank one Saturday morning. Instead of wiping it up and hoping, she called right away. The tank had a pinhole starting at a rusted seam.

She'd lost maybe $180 worth of oil into a patch of gravel, which was contained and dealt with quickly. Her replacement tank cost $3,100. If that pinhole had opened up over winter and run into the soil beside her foundation, she'd have been looking at an excavation job starting around $40,000, and because the tank was 24 years old, past her insurer's 20 year limit, the claim almost certainly would've been denied.

## How to Protect Yourself Before Anything Leaks

You can't control your insurer's rules, but you can make yourself the easiest customer they insure. Here's the playbook we give every homeowner who calls us after getting the letter.

**Know your tank's age and put it in writing.** Find the data plate, note the manufacture date, and keep a photo. If the plate is unreadable, a technician can usually estimate age from the style and serial format.

**Read your policy's oil heat section, then phone your broker.** Ask three questions: what's your tank age limit, is gradual leakage excluded, and what's the cap on environmental cleanup. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada has a plain language overview of [how home insurance works](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/insurance/home.html) if the policy wording reads like a foreign language.

**Get eyes on the tank twice a year.** Look for rust streaks, dents, wet spots, a strong oil smell, or legs sinking into soft ground. Check the oil line where it leaves the tank and enters the house. Anything suspicious, stop there.

Don't sand rust, don't tighten fittings on a full tank, and don't test your luck. That's the point where a professional inspection earns its keep.

**Replace on your schedule, not the tank's.** A steel tank in our climate typically gives 15 to 25 years, less if it sits outside catching salt air and freeze thaw cycles. Replacing in September on your timeline beats replacing in January on the tank's timeline, with no heat, a spill, and an emergency premium.

**Keep every receipt.** Installation paperwork from a licensed installer, inspection notes, and delivery records are exactly the proof an adjuster asks for when a claim lands on their desk. A shoebox of paper has saved more than one claim.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will my insurance company really cancel me over an old oil tank?

Yes, non renewal over tank age happens regularly in New Brunswick, and it's the most common reason people call us. Most insurers set a limit somewhere between 15 and 25 years, and some now ask for photos or an inspection report at renewal. Replacing the tank before the deadline almost always resolves it, and your broker can confirm the exact requirement in writing.

### Does insurance pay for a new oil tank when the old one wears out?

No. A worn out tank is considered normal aging, like an old roof, so replacement is a maintenance cost that's on you. Insurance is there for sudden, accidental damage, not for parts of the home reaching the end of their life. Budgeting $2,800 to $4,500 once every couple of decades is the trade off for keeping coverage intact.

### What should I do the moment I find a leak or smell oil?

Shut off the tank's supply valve if you can reach it safely, keep people and pets away, and don't run fans or spread the oil around. Then call a professional right away, because containment in the first few hours is what separates a $2,000 problem from a $60,000 one. Our emergency line at (506) 802-3471 answers 24/7. Significant spills must also be reported to the province.

### Do indoor tanks face the same insurance rules as outdoor tanks?

Mostly yes, because age limits and the sudden and accidental rule apply either way. Indoor tanks are protected from weather so they often last longer, but a basement leak can be worse when it happens, since oil soaks into concrete and living space. Insurers treat both seriously, so the age letter can arrive no matter where your tank sits.
