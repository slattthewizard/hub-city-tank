## Why Ten Minutes Now Beats a Five-Figure Bill Later

Steel oil tanks almost never fail without warning. They rust from the inside out, which is sneaky, but the failure still telegraphs itself. Paint blisters. A faint diesel smell shows up near the basement stairs. A leg starts leaning after a wet fall. By the time oil actually reaches your floor or your lawn, the tank has usually been whispering about it for a season or two.

The gap between catching it early and catching it late is enormous. A weeping filter gasket is a couple hundred dollars. A planned tank swap in September is a routine half-day job. A split seam that dumps 400 litres into the soil beside your foundation is an environmental cleanup, and those regularly run $15,000 to $50,000 or more in this province, sometimes not fully covered by insurance.

There's an insurance angle too. Most insurers in New Brunswick now ask about your tank's age and condition at renewal, and some send letters demanding replacement of tanks over 15 or 20 years old. Homeowners who check their own tanks twice a year almost never get blindsided by those letters, because they already know what shape their tank is in.

## The 10-Minute Oil Tank Inspection Checklist, Step by Step

Do this in daylight if your tank is outside, or with a good light if it's in the basement. One ground rule first: don't scrape, bang on or poke the tank, especially the bottom. A badly corroded tank can be thinner than a pop can in spots, and you don't want to be the one who finds out.

**1. Start with the legs and stand (1 minute).** All four legs should sit flat and solid on a proper pad or patio stones, not on bare soil. Look for tilting, sinking on one corner, or rust eating through the legs where they meet the ground. A leaning tank strains the oil line and the seams.

**2. Scan the tank body for rust (2 minutes).** Walk around the whole tank. Surface rust on top is common and usually cosmetic. What matters is blistering paint, flaking scabs, or rust streaks running down the sides. If you want to know which rust is harmless and which isn't, we broke it down in [our guide to rust on oil tanks](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/).

**3. Check the bottom and the seams (2 minutes).** This is where steel tanks fail. Crouch down and shine your light along the bottom seam and the underside. You're looking for weeping, which shows up as a dark damp line, a drip forming, or a shiny wet patch on rusty steel. A small mirror or your phone's camera on selfie mode helps you see underneath without touching anything.

**4. Look and smell for wet spots (1 minute).** Check the floor or ground directly under the tank, the shutoff valve and the filter. Any dark staining, dampness or oil odour is a flag. A [wet spot under your oil tank](/blog/wet-spot-under-oil-tank/) is never normal, even a small one, so treat it as urgent until a pro says otherwise.

**5. Follow the oil line and filter (1 minute).** Trace the line from the tank toward the furnace as far as you can see it. Look for kinks, corrosion, damp fittings or a filter canister with oil sweat on it. Outdoor lines should be protected, not lying where ice or a snow shovel can hit them.

**6. Check the vent and whistle (1 minute).** The vent pipe should be capped, clear of snow and ice, and free of bird nests or debris. If your delivery driver has mentioned the vent whistle going quiet during fills, flag it, since a blocked vent can over-pressurize the tank during a delivery.

**7. Test the gauge (30 seconds).** The float gauge on top should move freely and roughly match your fuel use. A stuck gauge isn't dangerous by itself, but it's how people run out of oil in February and end up paying for a no-heat service call.

**8. Inspect the fill pipe and cap (30 seconds).** The cap should be snug and the fill pipe solid, with no rust holes. A loose or missing cap lets rain and snow in, and water inside the tank is the biggest driver of internal corrosion.

**9. Look up and around (30 seconds).** Is there a roofline dumping snow or dripping water straight onto the tank? Icicles forming above it? Shrubs trapping moisture against the steel? Outdoor tanks in our climate die from the outside in when they're kept constantly wet.

**10. Find the age (1 minute).** Check the nameplate or sticker for a manufacture date, usually on the end of the tank. If it's older than 15 years, or you can't find a date at all, put "confirm tank age" at the top of your to-do list. [Here's how to date your tank](/blog/how-old-is-my-oil-tank/) from the serial plate.

That's the whole checklist. If everything came up clean, you're done until spring or fall, whichever comes next.

## When to Stop DIY and Call a Professional

This checklist is a looking exercise, not a fixing exercise. There's a hard line between inspecting your tank and working on it, and here's where it sits.

Stop and call a licensed professional right away if you find any of these:

- Any visible oil, even a drop, on the tank bottom, the floor or the ground
- A strong oil smell indoors or near the tank
- Weeping, blistering or wet rust along the bottom seam
- A tank leaning badly enough that the oil line is under tension
- Any drip or dampness on the oil line or its fittings

Don't tighten fittings yourself, don't patch a weep with epoxy or a magnet kit from the hardware store, and never try to drain or move a tank on your own. Heating oil installations in New Brunswick fall under the CSA B139 code for good reason, and DIY repairs on a failing tank tend to turn small leaks into big ones. If oil has already reached soil or water, you're also legally required to report the spill to the province.

## How Often Should You Run This Oil Tank Inspection Checklist?

Twice a year is the sweet spot for most homes: once in the fall before heating season, and once in spring after the melt. The fall pass catches problems before your tank spends five months full and working hard. The spring pass catches whatever winter did, especially to outdoor tanks that spent months under snow load and freeze-thaw cycles.

Add a quick bonus check after any big event. A delivery, a windstorm, a roof avalanche onto the tank, or water in the basement are all worth a two-minute walk-around. Outdoor tanks near the coast, in places like Shediac and Cap-Pele, deserve a third look mid-winter because salt air speeds up corrosion.

If you want a sense of how seriously regulators treat tank corrosion, the U.S. EPA's [underground storage tank program](https://www.epa.gov/ust) exists almost entirely because small, slow leaks kept turning into massive groundwater problems. Massachusetts went as far as passing a [homeowner oil heating law](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/homeowner-oil-heating-system-upgrade-and-insurance-law) requiring leak-prevention upgrades on home systems. New Brunswick hasn't gone that far for residential tanks, but insurers here are quietly enforcing the same idea through their renewal letters.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I inspect a buried oil tank myself?

Not really, and please don't dig around one to try. With a buried tank you're limited to indirect signs: unexplained fuel use, oil smells outside, dead grass over the tank area, or water showing up in your fuel. If your property has or might have a buried tank, get a professional assessment, because buried tanks are the highest-risk category for expensive contamination.

### What should I do if my tank fails the checklist in the middle of winter?

Call right away rather than waiting for spring, because a failing tank with a full load of oil and freeze-thaw cycles working on it is a bad combination. Tank replacements happen year-round in New Brunswick, including January. If the tank is actively leaking, treat it as an emergency, get a container or absorbent pads under the drip, and use the 24/7 line instead of booking a regular appointment.

### Does my insurance company really care about the results?

Very much so. Most insurers in New Brunswick ask about tank age, material and location, and many will decline renewal or demand replacement once a steel tank passes 15 to 20 years. Keeping dated photos from your twice-yearly checks gives you proof of maintenance, and knowing your tank's condition turns an insurance letter into a scheduling question instead of a panic.

### My tank looks fine but it's 18 years old. Should I still worry?

Age matters even when the outside looks clean, because steel tanks corrode from the inside out where condensation collects on the tank floor. An 18-year-old tank is past the typical insurable lifespan regardless of appearance. You don't necessarily need to replace it this week, but you should get a professional opinion and start budgeting, because at that age the question is when, not if.
