## Why Insurers Put an Age Limit on Oil Tanks

Insurance companies don't care how your tank looks. They care about what they can't see, which is the inside of it.

Steel oil tanks almost always fail from the inside out. Condensation collects at the bottom of the tank, mixes with sludge, and slowly eats through the steel where nobody can inspect it. A tank can have a fresh coat of paint on the outside while the bottom is thin as a pop can. By the time oil shows up on your basement floor, the corrosion has been working for years.

Since no insurer can look inside every tank in New Brunswick, they use age as a stand-in for risk. The data backs them up. Most tanks that leak are past the 15 year mark, and a residential oil spill is one of the most expensive claims in Atlantic Canada. Cleaning contaminated soil under a home routinely costs $25,000 to $250,000, and in bad cases the remediation bill passes the value of the house.

## Oil Tank Age Insurance Limits: What NB Companies Actually Use

Here's the thing nobody tells you up front. There's no provincial law that says an oil tank must be replaced at a certain age. The limits come from each insurance company's underwriting rules, which is why your neighbour's insurer might allow 25 years while yours draws the line at 15.

That said, after years of replacing tanks across Westmorland, Albert, and Kent counties, we see the same patterns over and over. Here's roughly how tank age plays out with insurers in our area:

| Tank age | What most insurers do | Your smart move |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 10 years | Full coverage, few questions | File your installation paperwork somewhere safe |
| 10 to 15 years | May ask for photos or an inspection at renewal | Do a yearly walkaround, watch for rust and wet spots |
| 15 to 20 years | Replacement letters start showing up | Get a written replacement quote before they ask |
| 20 to 25 years | Many won't renew until the tank is replaced | Replace it on your schedule, not theirs |
| Over 25 years | Most decline coverage outright | Replace now, and call immediately if you see any leak |

A few things shift these numbers. Fibreglass tanks and double-bottom steel tanks often get more generous limits because they resist internal corrosion far better than single-wall steel. Outdoor tanks in our climate sometimes get flagged earlier because freeze-thaw cycles and snow load are hard on legs, fittings, and oil lines. And a tank with visible rust or a homemade stand can get flagged at any age.

## How to Find Out How Old Your Tank Really Is

Before you can argue with an insurer about age limits, you need to know your tank's actual age. Plenty of homeowners honestly don't, especially if the tank came with the house.

Start with the tank itself. Look for a metal nameplate or sticker on the top or side. Most tanks made for the Canadian market show a manufacture date or a serial number that encodes the year. Granby and Roth tanks both mark theirs, though 20 years of basement dust or outdoor weathering can make the label hard to read. A photo of that plate is exactly what your insurance company wants to see.

No legible plate? Try these next:

- Your home purchase documents or inspection report, which often note the tank's age
- The previous owner, if you can reach them
- Your oil delivery company, which may have equipment records going back years
- Receipts or permits from the installation, if the tank was replaced during your ownership

## Past the Limit? What to Do When the Insurance Letter Arrives

First, don't panic, and don't ignore it. Both reactions cost money. The letter usually gives you 30 to 60 days, and that's genuinely enough time if you act in the first week.

You have four realistic options:

**Replace the tank before the deadline.** This is the clean fix. You send the installation certificate to your broker, coverage continues, and you never think about it again. This is what most homeowners do, and it's almost always the cheapest path over any five year window.

**Ask for an extension with an inspection.** Some insurers will accept a professional inspection of a borderline tank, say one at 16 or 18 years, to buy you a season. It's worth a phone call to your broker, but don't count on it for a tank past 20.

**Shop for another insurer.** Possible, but the first question on every application is the tank's age, and answering it dishonestly can void your policy exactly when you need it. Companies that accept older tanks usually charge more and add leak exclusions. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada has a plain-language rundown of how [home insurance](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/insurance/home-insurance.html) policies and exclusions work if you want the background before calling around.

**Accept an oil leak exclusion.** Some homeowners keep their policy by signing away leak coverage. We'd urge you to think hard before doing this. An excluded leak means the entire cleanup lands on you, and under New Brunswick's rules a spill must be reported and remediated whether you're insured or not. The [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg/environment.html) oversees petroleum spills in the province, and their standards don't shrink because your policy has an exclusion. Our article on [whether insurance covers oil tank leaks](/blog/does-insurance-cover-oil-tank-leaks/) shows how often homeowners discover the gap after the oil is already in the ground.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### At what age do insurance companies require oil tank replacement in New Brunswick?

Most insurers operating in NB start asking questions at 15 years and set hard limits somewhere between 15 and 25 years, depending on the company and the tank type. Fibreglass and double-bottom tanks often get longer allowances than single-wall steel. There's no provincial law setting the age, so the exact number is up to each insurer's underwriting rules.

### Can I get home insurance with a 20 year old oil tank?

Often yes, but your options narrow. Some insurers will cover a 20 year old tank after an inspection, others will insist on replacement before renewal, and a few will offer coverage with an oil leak exclusion. Be honest about the age on any application, because a wrong answer can void your coverage exactly when a leak happens.

### What if I can't prove how old my oil tank is?

Insurers generally treat an unprovable age as an old tank, which usually triggers a replacement requirement. Check the nameplate, your purchase documents, your oil delivery company's records, and any old permits first. If none of those turn up a date, replacing the tank is usually faster and cheaper than fighting the underwriter, especially during a home purchase.

### Will a new oil tank lower my insurance premium?

Sometimes, though the bigger win is keeping full leak coverage and avoiding exclusions. Some companies offer modest discounts for new tanks, fibreglass construction, or double-bottom designs, and a fresh installation certificate makes renewals smoother for years. Ask your broker what your insurer specifically credits before you choose a tank type.
