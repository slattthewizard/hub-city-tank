## Why the Indoor vs Outdoor Oil Tank Question Matters More in NB

Start with the weather. An outdoor tank in Moncton takes everything winter can throw at it. Rain, ice storms, snow sliding off the roof, and dozens of freeze-thaw cycles between November and April. The tank shell, the legs, the fittings, and the pad underneath all get worked over year after year.

Then there's condensation, which is the quiet killer. When an outdoor tank swings from minus 20 at night to plus 5 in an afternoon thaw, moisture condenses on the inside walls of the tank. That water sinks below the oil, sits on the bottom, and rusts the tank from the inside out. That's why so many tanks that look fine on the outside fail anyway. An indoor tank living at a steady basement temperature barely deals with this at all.

Coastal homes have it even worse. If you're in Shediac, Pointe-du-Chene, or anywhere the sea breeze reaches, salt spray speeds up exterior rust in a way inland homeowners never see. We wrote about that in detail in our post on [salt air and oil tank corrosion](/blog/salt-air-oil-tank-corrosion/).

And finally, cold snaps. Furnace oil doesn't freeze solid, but in a deep freeze it can thicken and gel in an outdoor tank or an exposed line, starving your furnace on the coldest night of the year. Indoor tanks simply don't have that problem.

None of this means outdoor tanks are a mistake. It means location is a bigger deal here than the brochures suggest. ## The Case for an Indoor Oil Tank

If your basement has the room, indoors is usually the better long-term home for a tank in our climate.

The biggest win is lifespan. A tank that might give you 10 to 15 years outside can run 20 to 25 years in a dry basement, because it's protected from weather on the outside and condensation on the inside. Over the life of a house, that can mean buying one tank instead of two.

Indoor tanks also make winter simpler. No gelled fuel lines, no shovelling a path for the delivery driver to fight through, no chunk of ice off the roof denting your tank shell. Insurance companies tend to like them too. Many insurers allow indoor tanks to stay in service longer than outdoor ones before demanding replacement.

There are trade-offs. An indoor tank takes up real basement space, roughly the footprint of a chest freezer. Every litre of oil has to come in through a fill pipe and vent, so the plumbing has to be done right. And if an indoor tank ever does leak, the cleanup happens inside your home, which is exactly as miserable and expensive as it sounds. That's why we only install tanks with proper drip protection, and why any [oil smell in the basement](/blog/oil-smell-in-basement/) deserves attention the same week you notice it.

The tank looked fine from the driveway, but at that age outside, no insurer wanted it on the books. ## The Case for an Outdoor Oil Tank

Outdoor tanks are the right call for a lot of homes around here, and it's usually about the basement.

Some houses just don't have the space. Low ceilings, tight stairwells, finished rec rooms, mini homes with no basement at all. Others have damp foundations where an indoor steel tank would rust nearly as fast as one outside. In those cases, a good outdoor tank on a proper pad is the honest answer, not a consolation prize.

Outdoor installs are also simpler and usually a few hundred dollars cheaper. The delivery driver can reach the fill easily, and when the tank eventually needs replacing, nobody has to wrestle 250 kilograms of steel up a staircase. If a leak ever happens, the oil ends up in the ground outside instead of across your basement floor. To be clear, an outdoor spill is still a serious and costly problem for your soil and your neighbours' wells, but it won't soak your rec room and everything stored in it.

The trade-off is a shorter life and more babysitting. Outdoor tanks need snow cleared off them, ice kept away from the fittings, and stable legs that aren't slowly sinking into soft ground. ## What Insurance Companies and the Code Say

In Canada, oil tank installations follow the CSA B139 installation code, which sets the rules for clearances, venting, oil lines, and tank supports. Any licensed installer working in New Brunswick should be installing to it. That part is the same whether your tank goes inside or outside.

Where location really bites is insurance. Insurers set their own age limits for oil tanks, and many use different clocks for indoor and outdoor units because outdoor tanks fail younger. It's common to see outdoor tanks flagged for replacement years before an identical indoor tank would be. If you've received one of those letters, don't panic, but don't sit on it either. The deadline is usually real.

There's also the environmental side. In New Brunswick, oil spills must be reported, and cleanup happens under the oversight of the provincial [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg.html). Heating oil releases can contaminate soil and groundwater for years, which is why [Environment and Climate Change Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change.html) treats petroleum products as a pollution priority. A properly located, properly installed tank is cheap insurance against ever meeting either department.

This is also where we'd gently say: don't DIY any of this. Moving, draining, or hooking up an oil tank yourself can void your insurance, and one botched fitting can turn into a five-figure cleanup. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do outdoor oil tanks freeze in New Brunswick winters?

The oil itself won't freeze solid, but untreated furnace oil can gel or cloud in a hard cold snap, clogging your filter and line just when you need heat most. Suppliers blend winter fuel to help, and a sheltered tank location and an insulated line reduce the risk further. Indoor tanks avoid the issue entirely because the oil stays at basement temperature.

### Which lasts longer, an indoor or outdoor oil tank?

The same tank model typically lasts 20 to 25 years indoors versus 10 to 15 years outdoors. The difference comes from weather exposure on the outside of the tank and condensation forming on the inside as outdoor temperatures swing. That longevity gap is the single biggest argument for an indoor install when your basement allows it.

### Will my insurance company accept either tank location?

Most insurers accept both, but they usually apply stricter age limits to outdoor tanks because those fail younger. Some also require double-wall or fibreglass construction for certain setups. Check your renewal documents or ask your broker before choosing, and keep your installation paperwork, because we've seen it settle insurance questions years later.

### How much does it cost to move an oil tank from outside to inside?

In Greater Moncton, expect roughly $3,000 to $5,500 CAD depending on oil line length, how much fuel needs transferring, and disposal of the old tank. That's more than a like-for-like swap, but the longer indoor lifespan usually pays the difference back. We'll price both options in the same free written estimate so you can compare them directly.
