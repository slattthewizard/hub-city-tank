## Why Two Quotes for the Same Job Can Look So Different

Say you get three quotes for a basement tank in a 1970s Riverview bungalow. One comes back near the bottom of the $3,500 to $6,400 full-swap range, one lands in the middle, and one comes in above it. That spread isn't automatically a red flag. It's usually a sign that the three installers are pricing three different jobs, even though they walked through the same basement.

The cheap number often means fewer line items are included: no mention of disposal, no line for the leftover oil in the old tank, nothing about a new filter or valve. The higher numbers might include a double-wall or fibreglass tank instead of standard steel, a full stand-and-pad rebuild instead of reusing the old base, or a harder basement removal that needs the old tank cut apart. Our guide to [choosing an oil tank removal company](/blog/hiring-oil-tank-contractor/) covers the questions that expose which is which. This article works from the other direction: what a complete quote should list, item by item, so you can read any quote and see what's missing.

## The Tank Itself

This is the line most homeowners focus on, and it's where the biggest swings in price come from. New steel tanks installed run $3,000 to $4,500 CAD, and new fibreglass tanks run $4,500 to $5,500, per our [oil tank replacement cost](/oil-tank-replacement-cost/) breakdown. A quote should name the material and, ideally, the size.

Residential tanks are sold in a handful of standard capacities. As one example, [Granby Industries' Canadian residential range](https://www.granbyindustries.com/product-category/residential-tanks/) lists a double-bottom steel tank at 680, 910 and 1,135 litres (150, 200 and 250 imperial gallons) and fibreglass tanks from about 680 to 1,140 litres. Other makers publish their own sizes, so the quote should name the brand, the model and the capacity in litres. If you're weighing steel against fibreglass for your situation, our [steel vs fibreglass comparison](/blog/steel-vs-fiberglass-oil-tank/) goes into the tradeoffs. Either way, the quote should say which one you're paying for, not just "new tank."

## The Stand or Pad

A tank is only as good as what it sits on, and this line gets skipped more than any other. Outdoor tanks need a stand on a solid, level base such as a concrete pad, never bare soil that heaves every freeze-thaw cycle. Indoor tanks need a level floor and legs that don't rock. A quote that jumps straight from "tank" to "installation" without mentioning the base is one to ask about directly, because a tank on a shifting or sloped base puts strain on every fitting attached to it. Our guide to [oil tank stands and pads](/blog/oil-tank-stand-and-pad/) covers what a good base looks like.

## Oil Line, Filter, and Shut-Off Valve

Three smaller items belong on every quote, and together they add up:

- **The oil supply line.** Copper line running from tank to burner, ideally new rather than reused, and sleeved where it runs through a wall or floor.
- **The filter.** A canister filter near the tank outlet, with a replaceable cartridge, catches water, rust, and sediment before they reach the burner. A new tank deserves a new cartridge, not the one that spent years filtering the old tank's sludge.
- **The shut-off valve.** A fusible fire-safety valve on the oil line closes by itself and stops the flow of oil if a fire or other high temperature hits it. Beckett, which makes the Firomatic line, says its [fire safety fusible valves](https://www.beckettcorp.com/product/firomatic-fire-safety-fusible-inline-valves/) are required in residential installations covered by CSA B139 in Canada (the page cites the 2019 edition) and NFPA 31 in the US. The code is now in its [CSA B139 Series:24](https://www.csagroup.org/store/product/2702055/) edition, which updated its shut-off valve requirements. Either way, this is a fire-safety device, not an optional extra, and the quote should name it.

If a quote lumps all three into "fittings" with no detail, ask what's in that line. A missing filter or valve isn't a corner worth cutting, and adding either one later usually means a separate service call.

## Fill Pipe, Vent Pipe, and the Vent Whistle

The fill pipe (where the delivery driver connects) and the vent pipe (which lets air escape as the tank fills) both run from the tank to the outside, and both should be on the quote as named items, especially if the old ones are rusted or the tank is moving to a new spot. The vent line also carries the whistle, the device that sounds while oil is filling and goes silent as the tank nears full. It works as overfill protection, and many drivers won't keep filling if they can't hear a working whistle or can't get to the fill because of snow or ice. Our guide to [oil tank parts](/blog/oil-tank-parts-explained/) breaks down what each of these does if you want the fuller picture.

Winters here make this more than a technicality. A vent cap buried under roof snow or an outdoor tank iced over after a freeze-thaw cycle can silence a whistle just as effectively as a broken one, which is one more reason this line belongs on the quote in writing rather than assumed.

## Oil Transfer

If you're staying on oil, the quote should say what happens to the fuel already in your old tank. Clean oil is often pumped out and put back into the new tank once it's set and levelled. The sludge and water that settle at the bottom of an old tank don't get transferred; they're handled as part of proper disposal. Not everyone agrees transfer is wise: a [Nova Scotia fuel safety bulletin](https://novascotia.ca/lae/fuelsafety/docs/FSB0906.pdf) on the national code warns that transferring contaminated oil can shorten the life of a new steel tank and may affect its warranty, and notes that a Nova Scotia guide recommends putting only new fuel in a new tank. Ask the installer how they handle it. If your tank is close to empty when the crew arrives, there's less to transfer either way, which is part of why booking a swap before you're down to fumes is worth planning around.

## Removing and Disposing of the Old Tank

A full swap should include taking the old tank away, and that line item is exactly why a bundled removal-plus-replacement job (the $3,500 to $6,400 full-swap range) usually makes more sense than booking removal and installation as two separate calls: one crew, one visit, and one stretch with the heat off instead of two. Our [oil tank removal cost](/blog/oil-tank-removal-cost/) guide has the standalone removal ranges if you're only removing a tank and not replacing it, say you're switching off oil entirely. Either way, ask where the old tank goes. A legitimate quote includes proper draining and disposal, not a tank left in a driveway or a scrapyard with no paperwork trail. ## The Paperwork

The last line item is the one people forget to ask about until they need it: what you get in hand once the crew leaves. At minimum, that should include an itemized invoice, a disposal receipt for the old tank, and a dated installation record for the new one. If your insurer sent the letter that started this whole process, that paperwork is what goes back to them. If you sell the house in five or ten years, a buyer's lawyer may ask for the same file. A quote that's silent on documentation is a quote that assumes you won't need to prove anything later, which is a bad assumption for a tank job.

## When a Low Quote Is a Warning Sign

None of this means the cheapest quote is automatically the wrong one. Plenty of straightforward outdoor jobs (easy access, a standard steel tank, no complications) land near the bottom of the $3,500 to $6,400 full-swap range honestly. The problem is a quote that's cheap because it's missing lines, not because the job is genuinely simple. If a number looks low, ask directly: does this include disposal? Is there a filter and valve in this price, or are those extra? What happens to the oil in my current tank? A contractor who answers those questions without hesitation is quoting the whole job. One who gets vague is quoting around the parts that would have made the number less attractive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should a written oil tank installation quote include?

At minimum, the tank (material and size), the stand or pad, the oil line, filter, and shut-off valve, the fill and vent piping, what happens to any oil left in your old tank, removal and disposal of the old tank, and the paperwork you'll get once the job is done. ### Why do oil tank installers quote different prices for the same job?

Usually because they're including different things, not because one is overcharging. A lower number often means disposal, oil transfer, or a new filter and valve aren't in the price yet. A higher number might reflect a fibreglass tank, a full stand rebuild, or a harder basement removal. Compare what's listed, not just the total.

### Does an oil tank installation quote include removing the old tank?

It should, if you're doing a full swap rather than adding a second tank. A combined removal-and-replacement job typically runs $3,500 to $6,400 CAD and should name the old tank's removal and disposal as its own line, with a disposal receipt included.

### Is a fire-safety valve required on a new oil tank?

According to Beckett, the maker of Firomatic valves, fusible fire-safety valves are required in residential installations covered by CSA B139, the Canadian installation code. The valve closes by itself and cuts off the oil if a fire or high heat reaches it. Ask your installer where it goes on your line, and make sure the quote names it rather than treating it as optional.

### What happens to the oil left in my old tank when a new one goes in?

If it's clean, it's often pumped out and put back into the new tank once it's set and levelled. Sludge and water from the bottom of the old tank are handled as part of disposal, not transferred. Some guides advise against moving old oil into a new steel tank at all, so ask your installer how they handle it.
