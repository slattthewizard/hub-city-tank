## First, Make Sure It's Actually a Leak

Not every wet spot is a disaster. Indoor tanks in damp basements often sweat in summer, and that condensation can drip off the bottom of the tank and look alarming.

Here's the quick test. Wipe the spot with a clean paper towel and smell it. Heating oil has a sharp diesel-like odour you won't mistake for water, and it leaves a yellowish stain on the towel. Condensation smells like nothing and dries clean.

Also look at where the moisture is coming from. Condensation forms all over the tank's cold surface. A leak comes from a specific point: a rusted seam, the bottom of the tank, the filter, a fitting, or the oil line. If you see a rainbow sheen on the moisture, that's oil.

Still not sure? We wrote a whole guide on [what a wet spot under your oil tank means](/blog/wet-spot-under-oil-tank/). When in doubt, treat it as a leak until a professional tells you otherwise.

## What to Do When Your Oil Tank Is Leaking: The First 5 Steps

Do these in order. None of them require special skills or tools, and together they can save you tens of thousands of dollars.

### Step 1: Slow or stop the flow, if you can do it safely

Put a bucket, roasting pan, or any container under the drip right away. If the leak is coming from the filter, the fuel line, or a fitting rather than the tank body, close the shut-off valve at the bottom of the tank. It's usually a small wheel or lever where the line leaves the tank, and turning it clockwise stops oil from feeding the leak.

While you're at it, shut the furnace off at the emergency switch. You don't want the burner pulling fuel through a compromised line while you deal with the tank.

If the tank body itself is weeping through rust, don't try to move the tank, tip it, or tighten anything on it. A corroded steel tank can let go completely if you disturb it, and 900 litres of oil on your basement floor is a catastrophe. Catch the drip, close the valve if you safely can, and leave the rest to a licensed technician.

### Step 2: Contain what's already out

Grab whatever absorbent you have: kitty litter, floor-dry, sawdust, even old towels you're willing to sacrifice. Ring the spill so it can't spread, especially toward floor drains, sump pits, or cracks in the slab. Oil that reaches a drain or sump can end up in the ground or the municipal system, and that changes the whole situation.

Open basement windows if you have them and keep kids and pets out of the area. Heating oil isn't explosive at room temperature the way gasoline is, but the fumes cause headaches and nausea, and nobody should breathe them longer than necessary. Don't smoke or run anything with an open flame near the spill, and skip the scented candles too. The smell is information, and masking it just hides whether your cleanup is working.

Keep the soaked absorbent in a plastic-lined pail or bin. It counts as contaminated material and shouldn't just go in your regular garbage.

### Step 3: Report the spill

In New Brunswick, petroleum spills must be reported. The 24 hour environmental emergencies line for the Maritimes is 1-800-565-1633, and it connects you with the people who notify the province's [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg.html). The call is free, it takes a few minutes, and it protects you.

Homeowners sometimes hesitate here because they're afraid reporting will get them in trouble. It's the opposite. A documented, promptly reported spill with a proper cleanup is exactly what your insurance company and any future home buyer want to see. An unreported spill that surfaces later, during a home sale or a neighbour's well test, is where the real trouble lives.

For small indoor drips fully caught on concrete, the response is usually simple guidance over the phone. For anything that touched soil, a drain, a well, or a ditch, reporting is essential and follow-up is likely. Environment and Climate Change Canada's [Environmental Emergencies Program](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/environmental-emergencies-program.html) explains how spill response works across the country, but for a home tank leak, that one phone number covers you.

### Step 4: Call your insurance company and start documenting

Before anyone cleans anything beyond emergency containment, take photos and video. Get the tank from several angles, the leak point, the spread of the oil, the tank's nameplate or serial number, and the room around it. Note the date and time you found the leak, and dig out your recent oil delivery receipts, since they help establish how much oil was in the tank.

Then call your home insurer's claims line and get a claim number the same day. Coverage for oil leaks in New Brunswick varies a lot between policies, and many insurers apply age limits to tanks, so don't assume you're covered or assume you're not. ## What Not to Do With a Leaking Oil Tank

The wrong moves in the first day often cost more than the leak itself. Here's what we see go wrong in Greater Moncton basements and yards.

**Don't hose it down.** Water spreads oil, it doesn't dilute it. Hosing an outdoor spill pushes oil deeper into the soil and toward storm drains. Hosing an indoor spill sends oil to the floor drain, and now you've got a much bigger reporting situation.

**Don't trust a DIY patch.** Epoxy sticks, fibreglass wrap, even the old bar-of-soap trick might slow a pinhole for a day. They do nothing about the corrosion eating the rest of the tank floor, and a patched tank will fail again, usually worse, usually in February.

**Don't keep running the furnace off a tank that's leaking from the bottom or a seam.** Sludge and water stirred up near a failing tank floor can foul your filter and nozzle, and you want the remaining oil pumped out cleanly, not burned down over an active leak.

**Don't wait for spring.** We hear this one every fall. The tank has already told you it's done. Winter is when tanks fail hard, when the ground is frozen, and when emergency work costs the most.

**Don't throw soaked absorbent in the garbage.** Contaminated material has to be disposed of properly. Your contractor handles this, which is one more reason to get one involved early.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to report a small oil leak in New Brunswick?

The safe assumption is yes. Call the 24 hour Maritimes environmental emergencies line at 1-800-565-1633 and describe what happened. For a few cups fully caught on concrete, the response is usually simple advice, while anything that reached soil, a drain, or water gets follow-up. Reporting protects you with your insurer and in any future sale of the house.

### Will my insurance cover a leaking oil tank?

It depends on your policy, and the differences between insurers are big. Many policies cover sudden accidental spills but exclude gradual leaks from corrosion, and most insurers apply age limits, often around 15 to 20 years. Call your insurer's claims line the same day you find the leak and document everything with photos before cleanup. The paper trail is often the difference between a covered claim and a fight.

### Can a leaking oil tank be repaired instead of replaced?

If the leak is at the filter, a fitting, or the oil line, yes, that's a repair, and it's often a few hundred dollars. If the tank body itself is leaking, replacement is the only safe answer. Steel tanks corrode from the inside out, so a pinhole means the metal around it is thin too, and any patch is temporary at best.
