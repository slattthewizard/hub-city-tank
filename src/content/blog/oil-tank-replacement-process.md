## How the Oil Tank Replacement Process Starts: The Estimate

Everything begins with a proper look at what you've got. A tech visits your home, checks the tank, and works through a short list:

- Where the tank sits: basement, garage, or outside on a stand
- How the oil line runs to the furnace or boiler
- The condition of the fill pipe, vent pipe, and gauge
- How much oil is left in the tank right now
- Access issues like narrow stairs, tight doorways, or a soft, sloped yard

That last point matters more than people think. A standard 910 litre steel tank is about five feet long, and getting one out of a 1970s basement with a tight stairwell takes real planning. Sometimes the old tank has to be drained, cut open, and carried out in pieces.

You'll also talk tank options during this visit. Most homes in our area run a standard 910 litre steel tank, but double-bottom steel tanks and double-wall models are worth a serious look, especially for outdoor installs near the coast. They cost more up front and buy you a lot of peace of mind.

Then you get a written estimate. Insist on written. It should list the tank model, the stand or pad, the oil line, filter, fittings, vent whistle, labour, oil transfer, disposal of the old tank, and taxes. ## Replacement Day, Step by Step

Here's what happens once the truck pulls into your driveway. Most of these steps overlap, and a two-person crew moves through them faster than you'd guess.

### Step 1: Shut down and protect the space

The furnace or boiler gets switched off at the emergency switch, and the crew lays down drop sheets along the path from the tank to the door. For basement tanks, anything fragile nearby gets moved or covered before a wrench comes out.

### Step 2: Pump out the old oil

A transfer pump moves your remaining oil into clean holding drums. Good crews pull from a few inches above the tank bottom so the sludge and water that collect down there stay out of your fresh supply. That bottom layer goes off for proper disposal, not into your new tank.

### Step 3: Disconnect the lines

Next the oil supply line to the furnace comes off, along with the fill pipe and vent pipe. Each open connection gets capped or plugged right away so nothing drips while the tank is being moved.

### Step 4: Remove the old tank

An outdoor tank usually lifts straight off its stand and onto the truck. A basement tank is the wild card. If it fits up the stairs and through the door, out it goes in one piece. If it doesn't, the crew cuts it apart with the vapours properly managed, which is one of several reasons this is not a DIY job.

### Step 5: Prep the base

The new tank needs a solid, level base. Outside, that means a proper stand on a concrete pad or patio stones set on compacted ground, never bare soil that heaves every frost. Inside, the floor gets checked for level and the legs get set so the tank can't rock. A leaning tank stresses every fitting attached to it.

### Step 6: Set and level the new tank

The new tank is placed and levelled with a slight pitch toward the outlet, exactly as the manufacturer specifies. That pitch lets water and sediment settle away from the fuel pickup instead of feeding into your burner.

### Step 7: New line, filter, and fittings

A quality install includes a new oil line in a protective sleeve, a new filter, new fittings, and a vent whistle so your delivery driver can hear when the tank is nearly full. Reusing a 25 year old copper line on a brand new tank is false economy, and it's where corner-cutting outfits save their money. Everything is installed to the CSA B139 code that governs oil burning equipment in Canada.

### Step 8: Refill and leak test

Your clean oil goes back into the new tank through a filter. Then every joint and fitting gets checked for weeps and drips. No crew should leave before the whole system has been inspected under real operating conditions.

### Step 9: Fire the furnace and bleed the line

Air in the oil line will stop a burner cold, so the tech bleeds the line at the pump until fuel runs clear, then fires the furnace and runs it through a full heating cycle. You should have warm air or hot water moving before anyone packs up.

### Step 10: Paperwork and haul-away

The tank gets tagged with the install date, you get documentation for your insurance company, and the old tank leaves on the truck for recycling. Steel tanks are almost entirely recyclable, so very little of the old unit ends up in a landfill.

## How Long Does the Whole Job Take?

For most homes, replacement day is half a day. Your heat is typically off for three to five hours, and we schedule morning starts so everything is warm again well before supper.

A few things stretch the timeline:

- A basement tank that has to be cut apart for removal
- A nearly full tank, since 800 litres takes a while to pump twice
- Winter conditions, where snow and ice slow down outdoor work
- Rot or rust on fill and vent pipes that need replacing along the way

In winter we plan jobs so no home sits without heat overnight, full stop. And if your tank is already 15 years or older, don't wait for a failure to force an emergency timeline on you. Our guide on [how long oil tanks last](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) will help you judge how much runway you've got.

One more wrinkle worth knowing: if your property has an old buried tank from decades past, that's a different job entirely, with its own excavation and testing steps. Underground leaks can quietly contaminate soil and groundwater for years, which is why the [U.S. EPA runs an entire program](https://www.epa.gov/ust) on underground storage tanks.

## Getting Ready for Replacement Day (and What Not to DIY)

You don't need to do much, but a little prep saves the crew time and you money.

- Clear a path from the tank to the nearest door, about a metre wide
- Keep pets closed off in another room
- Note roughly how much oil is in the tank when you book
- In winter, shovel the driveway and the route to the tank
- Move vehicles so the truck can back in close

Now the other side of it. Do not drain the tank yourself, and don't loosen a single fitting. Home heating oil is a regulated contaminant, and even a few litres soaked into a basement floor or lawn can trigger a cleanup running into tens of thousands of dollars. In New Brunswick, oil spills must be reported to the [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg/environment.html), and homeowners who caused a spill while freelancing on their own tank have a hard conversation ahead with their insurer.

Same goes for moving a partly full tank, cutting up an old one, or reusing the old oil line to save a few dollars. The tools are cheap. The mistakes aren't. This is one of those jobs where paying a licensed and insured crew is genuinely the frugal choice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does the oil tank replacement process take?

Most residential swaps take three to six hours from arrival to a running furnace. Basement tanks with tight access can push toward seven hours if the old tank has to be cut apart. Booking the estimate and getting on the schedule usually adds a few days to a week.

### Will I be without heat during the swap?

Yes, but only for a few hours while the tanks are switched over. We schedule morning starts so your home is heating again the same day, and in winter we never leave a house without heat overnight. Plan for the furnace to be off from roughly mid-morning to mid-afternoon.

### Can the oil from my old tank go into the new one?

Usually, yes. Clean oil gets pumped out, held in drums, and returned to the new tank through a filter. The sludgy bottom layer stays out and goes for proper disposal. If your old oil is badly contaminated with water or sediment, we'll tell you before disposal so there are no surprise charges.

### Do I need a permit or inspection for a new oil tank in New Brunswick?

Oil tank installations must meet the CSA B139 installation code, and your insurer will want proof the work was done by a licensed installer. A good contractor handles the compliance details and gives you dated documentation for the new tank. Keep that paperwork with your insurance file, because you'll be asked for it at renewal time.
