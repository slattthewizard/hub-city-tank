It's minus 18 with a wind warning, and your furnace hums for ten seconds, shuts down, then tries again until it locks out for good. Sound familiar? A furnace not getting oil is the most common no-heat call in Greater Moncton every winter, and the cause is almost always somewhere between the tank and the burner, not inside the furnace itself.

Here's the good news. You can trace most of the fuel path yourself in about twenty minutes, without special tools, and have a solid idea of what's wrong before you pick up the phone. This guide walks the path the same way a technician would, starting at the tank and ending at the burner, in the order that finds the problem fastest.

## First, Confirm There's Actually Oil in the Tank

Before you blame the furnace, walk over to the tank. Oil tank gauges fail more often than any other part of the system, and a stuck float can read a quarter full while the tank sits bone dry.

Karen in Salisbury learned that the hard way last February. Her gauge read just under a quarter, so she figured she had two weeks of oil left. She didn't. The tank was empty, the furnace locked out on a Sunday night, and by Monday morning she'd paid about $420 for an emergency delivery plus a service call to bleed the line. A $200 gauge replacement the previous fall would have prevented the whole mess.

Here's how to check the real level. Give the gauge glass a gentle tap first. If the float drops suddenly, it was stuck. For a proper check, unscrew the fill cap and lower a clean length of wooden dowel straight down until it touches bottom, then pull it out and read the wet line like a dipstick. Anything under 10 cm of oil means the pickup line may be sucking air or sludge instead of clean fuel.

If the tank really is empty, don't just order oil and mash the reset button. Running dry pulls sediment off the tank bottom into the line, so you'll often need a filter change and a proper bleed before the burner fires again. ## Check the Simple Stuff Before You Blame the Burner

You'd be surprised how many no-heat calls end with a flipped switch. Run through these before anything else.

Start with the red emergency switch. Most homes in Moncton, Dieppe, and Riverview have one at the top of the basement stairs, and it looks exactly like a light switch. Kids flip them. So do people carrying laundry baskets. Make sure it's on.

Next, check the breaker panel for a tripped furnace circuit, then confirm your thermostat is set to heat and calling for a temperature above the current room reading. If it runs on batteries, swap them.

Now follow the oil line back to the tank and find the shut-off valve. It should be fully open. Valves get closed during renovations, chimney work, and tank service, and then everyone forgets about them.

Finally, the reset button on the burner. Press it once, and only once. If the furnace fires and keeps running, you likely had a minor airlock or a brief power hiccup. If it locks out again, stop. Every extra press sprays unburned oil into the combustion chamber, and when it finally ignites you can get a puff-back that coats your house in soot. The U.S. Department of Energy's guide to [furnaces and boilers](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/furnaces-and-boilers) has a good plain-language overview of how these systems work, but the one-press rule is the part to remember.

## Why Your Furnace Isn't Getting Oil: The Usual Suspects

If the tank has oil, the valve's open, and one reset didn't do it, something is blocking the path. Oil travels from the tank, through the shut-off valve, down the line, through a filter, into the fuel pump, and out a tiny nozzle as a fine mist. A blockage at any point starves the burner.

Here are the usual suspects, roughly in order of how often we see them:

**A clogged fuel filter.** The cartridge filter near the tank or burner catches rust, sediment, and sludge. When it's packed solid, oil can't get through. This is the number one cause of a furnace not getting oil in older systems.

**Sludge stirred up in the tank.** Deliveries churn the tank bottom, and running the level low lets the pickup line drink from the sediment layer. Either one can send a slug of black paste toward your filter.

**Water in the tank.** Condensation builds up over years, sits under the oil, and gets pulled into the line where it rusts fittings and freezes in winter.

**A plugged nozzle or failing fuel pump.** The nozzle opening is thinner than a human hair, so it doesn't take much to block it. Pumps announce their retirement with whining and sputtering.

Denise in Riverview called us two days after a fill-up because her furnace kept short-cycling. Her filter was packed with black sludge, and the fix that day was $310 to swap the filter, flush the line, and bleed the system. But sludge that thick usually points upstream. Her tank was 24 years old and shedding rust off its inside walls faster than any filter could catch it. She replaced the tank that spring for $3,400 rather than paying for filter after filter while the steel kept thinning. Our article on [oil tank sludge](/blog/oil-tank-sludge/) explains why the last few years of a tank's life produce the most gunk.

A handy homeowner can swap an outside filter cartridge with the valve closed and a drip pan underneath. Anything past that, bleeding the pump, touching the nozzle, or opening the burner assembly, is technician territory. Heating oil under pressure plus an ignition source is not a place to learn as you go.

## Frozen Lines and Gelled Fuel: The Winter Classic

Outdoor tanks are the norm across Southeast New Brunswick, and they give winter two ways to stop your oil.

First, ice plugs. Any water in the line settles at the lowest point, and when a cold snap hits, it freezes into a solid block. Second, the oil itself can gel. Straight furnace oil starts to wax up in extreme cold, especially in long, exposed line runs on the shaded side of a house.

Marc in Shediac got the full experience during a minus 25 stretch two winters back. His outdoor tank sits on the north side, catching the wind off the strait, and his oil line dipped under the deck before entering the basement. That low spot held a bit of water, the water froze, and the furnace quit at 2 a.m. A careful thaw got the heat back on that night. The real fix came later: rerouting the line to remove the low spot and adding an insulated sleeve, about $480 all in. He hasn't had a frozen line since.

One safety rule that matters more than all the others: never use a torch, heat gun on high, or any open flame to thaw an oil line or fitting. Warm wet towels or a hair dryer on low, held on the metal fitting, is as aggressive as you should ever get. If that doesn't free it, the plug is somewhere you can't reach, and it's time for a pro.

Prevention is cheap. Insulate exposed line runs, keep the tank at least half full through cold snaps so there's less room for condensation, and ask your fuel supplier about winter-blended oil if your line has ever gelled before.

## When a Furnace Not Getting Oil Points to the Tank Itself

Here's the part most troubleshooting guides skip. If you're on your second or third clogged filter in a single season, the problem isn't the filter. Steel tanks rust from the inside out, where you can't see it, and a corroding tank sheds flakes and sediment into your fuel continuously. The furnace starving for oil is the symptom. The tank is the disease.

A few signs the tank is the real problem:

- The tank is 15 years old or more, or you don't know its age
- Filters keep clogging with black paste or rust flakes
- Your oil company or insurer has flagged the tank or asked for its age
- The furnace acts up after every delivery or every cold snap

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is my furnace not getting oil right after a fill-up?

A fresh delivery churns the tank and stirs up sediment sitting on the bottom, which then rides the fuel line straight to your filter and nozzle. Usually a filter change and a line bleed gets you running again. If it happens after every delivery, the tank bottom is carrying serious sludge, and the tank itself should be inspected.

### Can I bleed the oil line myself?

Technically yes, practically we don't recommend it. Bleeding requires running the pump while cracking a fitting, and getting it wrong means air lockouts, oil sprayed around the burner, and a smell that lingers for weeks. Press the reset button once, and if the furnace doesn't stay running, have a technician do the bleed properly.

### How do I keep my outdoor oil line from freezing?

Make sure the line slopes continuously with no low spots where water can pool, wrap exposed runs in an insulated sleeve, and keep the tank topped up through the coldest months to limit condensation. If your line has gelled before, ask your supplier about winter-blended fuel. Rerouting a problem line is usually a few hundred dollars and ends the 2 a.m. wake-up calls for good.

### How do I know if sludge in my tank is what's starving the furnace?

The tell is repetition. One clogged filter is bad luck, but filters that keep filling with black paste, a furnace that stumbles after deliveries, and a tank over 15 years old all point to a sludge and rust problem inside the tank. A technician can pull a sample from the bottom drain to confirm it. If sludge keeps coming back, put the money toward a new tank instead of another cleaning.
