## Why this isn't a DIY job in New Brunswick

Start with the trade. The federal occupational profile for [oil and solid fuel heating mechanics](https://noc.esdc.gc.ca/Structure/NOCProfile?GocTemplateCulture=en-CA&code=72420&version=2021.0) says oil heat system technician trade certification is compulsory in New Brunswick. That's the trade that connects oil burners to their fuel supply, so anyone doing this work for pay here needs that certificate.

Then the code. CSA B139, the installation code for oil-burning equipment, doesn't stop at the furnace. It covers the tank, the base it sits on, the fill and vent piping, and the supply line to the burner, which is the whole system our post on [CSA B139 oil tank rules](/blog/csa-b139-oil-tank-code/) walks through. Taking a tank out means undoing that system, and the hazards on the way out are the same ones the code is written around on the way in.

Here's what we couldn't find: a New Brunswick rule that spells out, one way or the other, whether a homeowner may take out their own above-ground tank. The province's [Petroleum Product Storage and Handling Regulation](https://laws.gnb.ca/en/showdoc/cr/87-97) requires approvals and a licensed installer to dismantle or dispose of a storage system of 2,000 litres or more, which is bigger than a normal household tank. The same regulation does require anyone who suspects or finds a petroleum leak from a system to report it right away, and that part has no size limit. So the leak you cause while draining a tank is your problem to report, whoever owns the wrench.

There's also a plainer reason. Your insurer, your oil supplier and any buyer's lawyer down the road will all want to know the tank came out properly, with a receipt from a company that does this work. "I did it myself on a Saturday" doesn't answer any of them.

## Why "empty" doesn't mean safe

This is the part most homeowners underestimate. A tank that reads empty on the gauge isn't empty. Whatever sits below the outlet stays behind: oil, water and sludge. And the inside walls carry a film of oil you can't see or measure.

Furnace oil isn't as volatile as gasoline, which is why a full tank in a basement is safe to live with. The trouble starts when you add heat or a spark. A grinder, a reciprocating saw or a torch heats the steel, the film and sludge inside give off vapour, and now you have a fuel-air mix inside a closed steel shell.

Workplace safety bodies treat that shell as a confined space. The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety lists tanks among its examples of [confined spaces](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/hsprograms/confinedspace/confinedspace_intro.html), and names a flammable atmosphere, residue from what the space held, and low oxygen among the hazards. New Brunswick's own [WorkSafeNB hazard alert on confined spaces](https://www.worksafenb.ca/hazard-alerts/en/confined-spaces-recognizing-the-danger.html) lists fire or explosion as one of the four main dangers and says NB law requires a competent person to test the air for oxygen, flammable and toxic contaminants before anyone enters one. Those rules are written for workers, but the physics don't care whose tank it is.

That's why we won't walk you through cutting up an old tank, and no honest source should. Professional crews pump out, clean and ventilate a tank before anyone opens it up, and the cutting, when a tank has to come apart to clear a stairwell, happens only after that. None of that is visible from the driveway, which is part of why it's easy to underestimate.

## What a professional removal involves

It helps to know what you're paying for when you hire it out, because most of the work happens in an order that isn't obvious.

The oil gets pumped out first, into sealed containers, not a shop-vac or a bucket. Usable oil can often go into a new tank; oil and sludge that can't be reused go to a licensed handler. The fill pipe, vent pipe and supply line get disconnected and capped so nothing weeps into your basement or onto the lawn while the tank moves. Only then does the tank come out, sometimes whole, sometimes cut down on site if a basement tank won't clear a doorway or a stairwell. From there it's cleaned and taken for scrap.

## Where DIY attempts go wrong

Say you decide to save the labour and handle it yourself. These are the ways it tends to go sideways, without any worst-case scare stories.

**The tank isn't as empty as the gauge says.** Tipping a basement tank that still holds sludge and old oil below the outlet sends that liquid to the floor, not a container. A soaked concrete slab is now a cleanup, not a removal.

**An outdoor tank on a trailer isn't drained or secured the way it needs to be.** A tank that shifts or weeps on the road or the driveway is a spill, and petroleum leaks in New Brunswick have to be reported. Our post on [reporting an oil spill in New Brunswick](/blog/report-oil-spill-nb/) covers what that looks like.

**The scrap yard won't take it.** Yards often want a tank cleaned and opened before they'll accept it, because a tank with liquid inside is a hazard for them too. Call first. Otherwise you can end up holding a heavy, half-drained tank with nowhere to take it.

**A buried tank gets treated like an above-ground one.** Digging up a buried tank without knowing its condition or checking the soil underneath is a much bigger job than it looks, and you can't undo it if something's wrong below. If you suspect a tank under your yard, read [abandoning an oil tank in place](/blog/abandon-oil-tank-in-place/) before anyone picks up a shovel. Our [buried oil tank removal service](/buried-oil-tank-removal-moncton/) starts with locating and assessing the tank before any digging happens.

**The weight catches people off guard.** An empty 900 litre steel tank weighs well over 100 kilograms on its own, before any sludge. Carry that down or up a narrow basement stair in a Riverview or Dieppe bungalow and the injury risk is as real as the fire risk.

These are the reasons a removal that looks like an afternoon with a friend and a pickup often ends up costing more than hiring it out would have: in cleanup, in a wasted trip to the scrap yard, or in a strained back.

## What you can safely do yourself

None of this locks you out of the process. Before a crew shows up, a homeowner can help and save money on the job.

- **Let the oil level run down** before removal day if you're not switching fuels right away. Less oil in the tank means less pumping and handling. (If the tank will sit nearly empty through a humid summer, talk to us first; condensation is the trade-off.)
- **Clear a path.** Move bikes, boxes and stored furniture away from the tank and the route it has to travel, especially if it's coming up a basement stair.
- **Photograph the tag or label** before the tank goes, along with the gauge reading. If you ever need to prove the tank's age or condition, that photo beats memory.
- **Note anything unusual** now: a wet spot, a rust bloom, a smell, a lean. Mention it when you call so the crew arrives prepared.
- **Leave the wrenches alone.** Disconnecting lines, tipping the tank, and anything involving a grinder or a torch is where this stops being a homeowner job.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I remove my own oil tank in New Brunswick?

You can do the prep: run the oil down, clear access, and photograph the tank and its label. The draining, disconnecting, cutting and hauling belong with a crew equipped for it. Oil heat system technician is a compulsory certification trade in New Brunswick, and insurers, buyers and scrap yards all expect proof the tank came out properly. Any leak you find or cause has to be reported.

### How do I safely empty an oil tank myself?

You don't, not fully. A tank that reads empty still holds oil, water and sludge below the outlet, and a proper pump-out uses sealed transfer equipment, not a bucket or a shop-vac. Running the fuel level down before removal day helps. Pumping out the last of it is a job for the crew that can handle and dispose of what comes out.

### Why can't I just cut up my old oil tank myself?

Because the oil film and sludge inside a tank give off vapour when the steel heats up, and a grinder or torch supplies both the heat and the spark. Occupational safety bodies treat tanks as hazardous confined spaces for exactly this reason. Professional crews pump out, clean and ventilate a tank before anyone opens it, a step that isn't visible and isn't skippable.

### What happens if I try to haul my old tank to the scrap yard myself?

Scrap yards often want a tank cleaned and opened before they'll take it, because a tank with liquid inside is a hazard for them too. You can end up with a heavy, half-drained tank on a borrowed trailer and nowhere to take it, plus a spill to deal with if it weeps on the way. Having disposal handled as part of the removal avoids that trip.
