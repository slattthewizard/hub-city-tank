## The Short Answer: Nobody Reputable Will Patch a Leaking Tank Shell

If oil is seeping through the steel of your tank, whether it's a pinhole on the bottom, a weeping seam, or a rusty spot that never dries, no licensed heating contractor in New Brunswick will repair it. That's not laziness, and it's not an upsell. It comes down to three hard facts.

First, the steel is failing from the inside out. The leak you can see is just the first place the corrosion broke through, not the only place it's working.

Second, there's no approved way to do it. CSA B139, the Canadian installation code for oil-burning equipment, has no provision for patching a corroded tank shell, and insurance companies treat a patched tank as an uninsurable tank. A repair that costs you your coverage isn't a repair.

Third, the stakes are lopsided. A typical residential tank holds around 910 litres of furnace oil. A patch that fails at 2 a.m. in January can turn a $60 fix into a cleanup bill bigger than a new car.

## Why a Leaking Oil Tank Usually Can't Be Repaired

To understand why the answer is no, you need to know how oil tanks die. It's almost never from the outside. It's condensation.

Every time the temperature swings, and around Moncton it swings hard, moist air inside the tank condenses into water droplets on the cold steel walls. Water is heavier than oil, so it sinks to the bottom and sits there. Over the years it builds a layer of water and sludge where bacteria live, and that mix quietly eats the floor of the tank from the inside. That's why tanks almost always fail at the bottom, right where you can't see the damage coming.

If you've noticed scaly rust or blistered paint on your tank, our post on [rust on an oil tank](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) explains which spots matter and which are just cosmetic.

Now picture what a patch actually does. That pinhole is simply the thinnest point of a much larger area of pitted, weakened steel. Glue an epoxy pad over it and the corrosion keeps chewing away at everything around the patch. The next hole opens an inch away, often within weeks. Heating oil is also a mild solvent, so it works at the edges of the patch around the clock.

Welding is off the table too. A used oil tank is full of flammable vapour, and putting a torch to it is genuinely dangerous even for professionals. No sane welder in Westmorland County will touch a residential tank that has held furnace oil.

Marc in Riverview learned the arithmetic the hard way. Two winters back he found a damp spot on the bottom of his 15-year-old tank, picked up a $28 epoxy stick at the hardware store, and pressed it over the weep. It held for about six weeks. Then a February cold snap came through, the patch let go overnight, and roughly 250 litres of furnace oil spread across his basement floor and soaked into the concrete.

His cleanup bill passed $19,000, and his insurer denied the claim because the tank had a visible DIY repair on it. A new tank, installed, would have cost him about $3,400. That's the real answer to the repair question. It's not that a patch can't slow a drip for a while. It's that the downside runs 5 to 30 times the cost of doing it right.

## The Leaks That Actually Can Be Fixed

Here's the good news, and it surprises a lot of people: a big share of the "leaking tanks" we get called about aren't leaking tanks at all.

Oil is sneaky. It creeps along the underside of the tank and drips off the lowest point, so the puddle often forms under the middle of the tank even when the leak started somewhere else entirely. Before you assume the worst, figure out where the oil is actually coming from. These parts fail far more often than the tank shell, and every one of them can be repaired or replaced legitimately:

- The fuel filter housing and its gasket, usually mounted just below the tank outlet
- The shutoff valve and the flare fittings where the fuel line connects
- The fuel line itself, especially older bare copper lines run along or under concrete
- The gauge and its seal on top of the tank
- The fill and vent piping connections

Repairs like these typically run $150 to $600 depending on parts and access, and most are an afternoon's work. The tank stays, your insurance stays, and you're done.

Diane in Shediac is a good example. She got one of those insurance letters last fall, then spotted oil under her outdoor tank a week later and assumed she was looking at a full replacement. Our tech wiped everything dry, waited a few minutes, and watched the drip form at the filter cover gasket, not the tank. New filter, new gasket, one fitting snugged up: $260 all in. The tank passed inspection and her policy renewed without a fuss. Salt air along the Shediac coast is harder on fittings and filters than on tank shells, so coastal homes see this pattern a lot.

The test is simple. Wipe the whole underside of the tank and every fitting completely dry with a rag, then check back every 15 minutes. If the first fresh bead of oil forms on the steel shell, a seam, or a weld, the tank is done. If it forms at the filter, valve, or a fitting, you likely have a cheap fix. ## Your Tank Is Leaking Right Now? Do This First

If you're reading this with a drip in progress, here's the order of operations. Stay calm. A drip caught today is a manageable problem.

1. **Contain it.** Put a metal pan, bucket, or even a roasting tray under the drip. Spread kitty litter or floor-dry absorbent if oil has already reached the floor.
2. **Check where the leak sits.** If it's at or after the shutoff valve, close the valve. If oil is coming through the tank shell itself, the valve won't help, so focus on containment.
3. **Ventilate.** Crack a window or run a fan pushing air out. Furnace oil fumes are unpleasant and hard on some people, though the oil itself is not explosive like gasoline.
4. **Don't drain the tank yourself.** Home transfer attempts with pails and hand pumps are how small leaks become big spills.
5. **Report it if oil has reached a floor drain, sump, or soil.** Spills in New Brunswick need to be reported to the [Department of Environment and Local Government](https://www2.gnb.ca/content/gnb/en/departments/elg.html), and reporting early works in your favour, not against you.
6. **Call for help.** Our [emergency oil tank service in Moncton](/emergency-oil-tank-service-moncton/) runs 24/7 for exactly this situation, and we can pump a failing tank down before it lets go.

For the full walkthrough, including what to photograph for insurance before anything gets cleaned up, see our guide on [what to do when your oil tank is leaking](/blog/oil-tank-leaking-what-to-do/).

One note on buried tanks. If your leaking tank is underground, none of the DIY steps above apply, and the stakes are higher because leaked oil moves through soil toward groundwater. The U.S. EPA's [underground storage tank program](https://www.epa.gov/ust) has good plain-language background on how those leaks behave. In practice, a suspected buried tank leak around here is a call-a-professional-today situation, full stop.

## The Insurance Trap That Catches Patched Tanks

There's one more reason the repair question matters so much in New Brunswick, and it has nothing to do with steel.

Home insurers here pay close attention to oil tanks. Most ask the tank's age at renewal, many set age limits somewhere between 15 and 25 years, and some request photos or an inspection. A visible patch, epoxy pad, or weld on a tank shell is an automatic red flag. Best case, they demand replacement before renewal. Worst case, they decline to renew at all, and shopping for coverage with a patched tank on record is not a fun project.

The bigger trap is what happens after a failure. Many policies exclude damage from gradual seepage, and a claim tied to a tank the homeowner knowingly patched is an easy denial. That's how people end up paying five-figure cleanup bills out of pocket. Our post on [whether insurance covers oil tank leaks](/blog/does-insurance-cover-oil-tank-leaks/) goes through the fine print, and it's worth ten minutes of your time if you heat with oil.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I patch a leaking oil tank with epoxy or a repair kit?

You can buy the kit, but you shouldn't use it on a tank shell. Corrosion attacks the tank from the inside, so a surface patch covers one hole while the steel around it keeps thinning. Insurers also treat patched tanks as uninsurable, which means a failed patch can leave you paying the whole cleanup yourself. Use a pan and absorbent to contain the drip, then call a professional.

### How much does it cost to replace a leaking oil tank in Moncton?

Most residential replacements in Greater Moncton land between $2,800 and $4,500 installed, depending on tank size, location, and how tricky the access is. That usually includes transferring your remaining oil, connecting the new tank, and hauling the old one away. An actively leaking tank may add emergency pump-down costs, which is one more reason to act while it's still a drip.

### Will my insurance cover a leaking oil tank?

Often not, and that surprises people. Many New Brunswick policies exclude gradual seepage, and coverage gets shakier still if the tank was old, patched, or flagged in a previous renewal letter. Sudden accidental spills are sometimes covered, but you need to read your specific policy wording. Replacing an aging tank before it fails is the only sure way to stay onside.

### What if the drip is coming from the filter or fuel line instead of the tank?

Then you're likely in luck, because those repairs are legitimate and affordable. Filters, gaskets, valves, and fittings fail far more often than tank shells and typically cost $150 to $600 to put right. The key is confirming the tank itself is sound while the tech is already there. Wipe everything dry, watch where the first bead of oil forms, and have a professional verify it before you relax.
