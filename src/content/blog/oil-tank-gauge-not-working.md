## Why Your Oil Tank Gauge Stops Working

The gauge on a residential oil tank is a simple mechanical device, and that's both its strength and its weakness. Inside the tank, a float sits on top of the oil, attached to a hinged arm. As the oil level drops, the float drops with it, and the arm pulls a marked disc or rod down inside the clear plastic dome on top of the tank. No wires, no batteries, no electronics.

Simple things fail in simple ways. Here's what usually goes wrong:

- **The float sticks.** Sludge builds up in the bottom third of older tanks, and that sticky film can grab the float or gum up the hinge. The gauge freezes at whatever reading it had when the float stopped moving.
- **The float sinks.** Cork and foam floats can soak up oil over the years, or crack outright. A sunken float drags the reading toward empty, or leaves it stuck wherever the arm comes to rest.
- **The arm jams.** The hinge corrodes, or the arm catches on the tank wall or an internal fitting. This is common in tanks that have been moved or refilled hard and fast.
- **The dome fogs or cracks.** Outdoor tanks in our region take a beating from salt air and freeze-thaw cycles. A fogged dome can make a perfectly good gauge unreadable, which happens to be the cheapest problem on this list.

## Quick Checks for a Stuck Oil Tank Gauge

Before you spend a dime, run through these five checks. They're safe for any homeowner, and they'll tell you whether the gauge is lying, lazy, or just dirty.

**1. Clean the dome.** Wipe the plastic dome with a damp cloth. Condensation, road dust, and pollen can hide the disc completely. If the dome is yellowed or cracked, the gauge underneath may still work fine.

**2. Try the tap test.** Give the side of the gauge a few gentle taps with your knuckles. Floats stick against the guide all the time, especially in cold weather, and a light tap often frees them. If the disc drops or bobs, it was stuck, and you just got your reading back for free. Don't hammer on it, and don't reach for a wrench.

**3. Check the math.** A typical Moncton area home burns roughly 8 to 12 litres of oil per day in deep winter. If your last fill was six weeks ago and the gauge still reads full, the gauge is almost certainly wrong. Pull out your delivery receipts and do the rough arithmetic.

**4. Watch it for a week.** Mark the gauge position with a bit of masking tape on the dome. If the furnace runs daily and the reading never moves, you've confirmed the problem.

**5. Stick the tank.** This is the gold standard, and it deserves its own section below.

Two safety notes before you go further. Never unscrew the gauge or any other fitting, plug, or valve on a tank that has oil in it. You can release oil vapour, break a seal that won't reseat, or strip threads that had one good turn left in them. And if you smell oil or spot any wet staining while you're out there, stop the DIY entirely and call a professional right away. A leak outranks a gauge problem every time.

## The Stick Test: Your Backup When the Gauge Reads Wrong

Oil delivery drivers have measured tanks the same way for decades, and it still beats any gadget for reliability. You'll need a clean, dry wooden dowel or hardwood stick about four feet long.

Here's the process. Find the fill pipe, which is the capped pipe the delivery driver uses, and unscrew the cap. Lower the stick slowly and straight down until it touches the bottom of the tank. Don't drop it or stab it down, because a hard hit can stir up sludge or stress an old tank floor. Pull the stick out and read the wet line.

On a standard 910 litre vertical oval tank, the inside height is roughly 44 inches. A wet line around 22 inches means you have about half a tank, somewhere near 450 litres. A wet line at 8 inches or less means you're into the last 130 litres or so, and it's time to order oil now, not next week. Below 4 inches, treat it as an emergency, because sludge and water sit at the very bottom and your furnace will start drinking them soon.

## When a Bad Gauge Points to a Bigger Tank Problem

Here's the part most gauge articles skip. Sometimes the gauge isn't the patient. It's the thermometer.

A float that sticks because of heavy sludge means the tank bottom is loaded with sediment, and sediment holds moisture against the steel floor. That's exactly where tanks corrode from the inside out. A hinge that seized because of rust, on a tank that also shows weeping seams or flaky scale outside, is telling you the whole unit is nearing the end of its run. Most steel tanks in our climate give you 15 to 25 years, less if they live outdoors near the coast where salt air speeds everything up.

Diane in Shediac called us about a gauge that read a quarter full no matter what. The tank turned out to be 26 years old, and the stick test came up coated in rust flakes and sludge. Fixing the gauge would have cost her about $150 and solved nothing, because the tank itself was on borrowed time and her insurer's renewal letter was already asking for its age. Insurers lean on those standards hard, and many now refuse to renew policies on tanks past a certain age regardless of how they look.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I check my oil level if the gauge is broken?

Use the stick test. Unscrew the cap on the fill pipe, lower a clean dry stick straight down until it touches bottom, then read the wet line. On a standard 910 litre tank, about 22 inches of oil means roughly half full. It takes five minutes and it's more accurate than most gauges.

### Can I replace an oil tank float gauge myself?

We don't recommend it. The gauge threads into an opening on top of the tank, and swapping it means working over an open fuel port, sealing the threads properly, and sometimes fishing a broken float out of the tank. A heating technician can usually do it in under an hour for $100 to $250 installed, and you get it done without spills or vapour in your basement.

### Why does my oil tank gauge read full all the time?

Nine times out of ten the float has failed. It has either split and sunk, or the arm has jammed at the top of its travel, so the gauge stays pinned at full no matter how much oil burns. Confirm it with a stick test, then have the gauge assembly replaced before you run the tank dry by accident.

### Does a broken gauge mean I need a new oil tank?

Not by itself. On a younger tank in good shape, a gauge swap for around $150 solves it completely. But if the gauge failed because of heavy sludge or internal rust, and the tank is 20 years old or more, the gauge is usually the first domino. Have the whole tank assessed before you spend money on a small fix.
