Plenty of people in Southeast New Brunswick head south for part of the winter, or just spend a few weeks away at Christmas, and leave the house heated. If the house runs on oil, the oil tank becomes the thing that keeps the furnace alive while you're gone. It has to hold enough fuel, stay sound for weeks without anyone looking at it, and not give the house a reason to drop below freezing.

This is different from closing a cottage for the season, which we cover in our guide to [your cottage oil tank over an empty winter](/blog/seasonal-home-oil-tank/). Here the heat stays on and the house is only temporarily empty. The checklist is short: look at the tank before you go, plan the fill, tell your insurer, arrange a second pair of eyes. ## The two ways this goes wrong

Two things end an unattended oil-heated house in winter, and they have little to do with each other.

**The oil runs out.** The furnace shuts off, the house cools, and with nobody home, nothing happens until the pipes freeze. We cover how to get heat back after a run-out in [ran out of heating oil](/blog/ran-out-of-heating-oil/), but that guide assumes you're standing in the house. Away from home, the problem grows quietly.

**The tank leaks.** A weep that would be noticed on a normal week can run for a long time in an empty house. A tank that is full at departure puts the most oil at risk if it fails. A tank that is low risks the first problem.

Neither is a reason to panic. Both are reasons to treat the tank as part of the trip plan, not an afterthought.

## Before you go: look at the tank

Spend ten minutes at the tank, outside or in the basement, before you lock up. Our [10-minute oil tank inspection checklist](/blog/oil-tank-inspection-checklist/) walks through the full list. The short version:

- **The legs and base.** A tank that leans, or a stand with a sunk corner, will not improve while you're gone. Snow and ice sliding off the roof can make an outdoor stand worse in a week.
- **Wet spots or stains on or under the tank.** Anything fresh, shiny or smelling of oil is a reason to call before you leave, not after.
- **Rust blisters, weeping seams, a bulge or a dent.** These are the signs of a tank on its way out.
- **The vent and fill pipes.** Make sure the vent cap is clear. A vent plugged with ice is a problem at the next delivery. Brush snow off it by hand; don't chip.
- **The gauge.** Read it and write the number down with the date. That's your baseline when you or your helper looks again.
- **The filter and valves.** Look for drips at the filter housing and at the shut-off valve.

If anything on that list is wrong, don't ignore it and hope. A tank with a visible problem is the one that fails when nobody's looking.

Granby Industries, a tank maker, says on its [questions and answers page](https://www.granbyindustries.com/en-us/petroleum-tanks/questions-and-answers/) that "all tanks should be inspected and serviced annually." If yours hasn't been looked at in the past year, a service visit before departure is the natural time. The same page says that if a tank has lost its rating label and shows any signs of ageing, replace it as soon as possible. The date is usually on the tank's label.

## Planning the fill: how full, and when

A full tank is the safest answer to the first risk and a bigger stake on the second. So the plan is to fill it, but only after you've looked at it. In winter, running out is the bigger worry.

A few practical points:

1. **Check the tank first, fill second.** Don't put a full load into a tank you've just noticed is weeping.
2. **Know your level on departure.** Use the gauge. A stuck gauge can mislead, so a stick measurement is a good cross-check.
3. **Think about the length of the trip.** A cold stretch in February burns more than a mild fall week. If you don't know your winter use, ask your oil company for your delivery history, and plan with the cold months in mind, not an average.
4. **Make sure automatic delivery is active and has your correct dates.** Don't assume. Ask the company to confirm in writing that it knows you're away and will keep delivering. Leave the driver a clear path: snow cleared to the fill pipe, and a note with your helper's name.
5. **Don't put the fill off to "when I get back."** If you're leaving in the cold months, the fill happens before you go.

## A second pair of eyes: monitor and a person

You can't see the tank from Florida. You can have someone look, and you can have a sensor look.

A monitor that reports the oil level to your phone is the obvious gadget. Our guide to [oil tank monitors](/blog/oil-tank-level-monitor/) explains what they catch and what they don't. In this case the useful alert is a low-level warning, which gives you time to order oil from a distance. The limit is the one that guide spells out: a level reading says nothing about the condition of the tank.

A person is better at the other jobs. Someone who walks the house and the tank on a regular schedule can see a wet spot, smell oil, notice a cold house, and find a vent packed with ice. Pick someone who knows where the tank is and what a normal gauge reading looks like. Give them a written note: the gauge reading on departure, the oil company's phone number, and ours.

Whoever you pick, the insurer may have a say in how often they go, which is the next item.

## Tell your insurer you're away

The Insurance Bureau of Canada's page, [Winter Storm Warning: What Homeowners Should Do Now to Protect Their Property](https://www.ibc.ca/news-insights/news/winter-storm-warning-what-homeowners-should-do-now-to-protect-their-property), says most policies specify how often a competent person must check the home to confirm the heat is being maintained. It doesn't give one number, because it varies by policy. It also says most insurance policies exclude frozen pipes and water damage if your home is vacant, even if you have permission for vacancy, and that if a home is vacant, the water should be shut off and the lines drained.

For you, that means three questions to put to your insurer before you leave, in writing:

- How long can the house be left before it counts as unoccupied or vacant?
- How often must someone check the house, and does that person have to be named?
- Does the policy say anything about oil tanks, oil heat or the tank's age while the house is empty?

We can't tell you what your policy says. We can tell you that a tank an insurer would question at renewal, because of its age or condition, is exactly the tank you don't want to leave alone. Our guide to [how old is too old for an oil tank, insurance limits explained](/blog/oil-tank-age-insurance/) covers the usual age thresholds insurers ask about.

## The decision: a tank you wouldn't trust alone

Here is the simple test. If you were leaving for three months and could not get a message to anyone, would you trust this tank to sit full, in the snow, for the whole stretch?

If the answer is yes, you have a plan: fill, look, tell your insurer, arrange the check, go.

If the answer is "I'm not sure," you've got information. A tank you're not sure about is a tank to replace now, while you're home, can supervise the work, and have time to choose. That's a cheaper conversation than dealing with a failed tank from a long way away.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I fill my oil tank before leaving for winter?

In general, yes, after checking the tank for leaks, a leaning stand and rust. A full tank is the best protection against running out while you're away. Fill only a tank you trust. If it shows a wet spot or a weeping seam, sort that out first.

### Will my home insurance cover frozen pipes if the furnace stops while I'm away?

It depends on your policy. The Insurance Bureau of Canada says most policies set how often a competent person must check the home to confirm the heat is on, and most exclude frozen pipes if the home is vacant. Ask your insurer before you go, and get the answer in writing.

### Is a wireless oil tank monitor enough while I'm away?

It helps with the run-out risk, because it can send a low-level alert. It doesn't see rust, a weeping seam or a leaning stand. Use it with a person who checks the tank and the house.

### How often should someone check the house?

Your policy decides. Many state an interval, and it varies. Ask your insurer for the number and the wording, and don't rely on a figure from a friend or a website.

### What if my tank is old and I'm leaving soon?

Have it looked at before you go. If you can't say how old it is, or it shows rust, a bulge, a lean or a wet spot, replace it before the trip.
