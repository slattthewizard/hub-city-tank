A copper tube about the width of a pencil is the only thing carrying furnace oil from your tank to your burner. If that line runs under your basement floor, a pinhole the size of a grain of sand can quietly put hundreds of litres of oil into the ground before you ever smell it.

That's why oil line replacement shows up on so many insurance letters and home inspection reports around Greater Moncton. The tank gets all the attention, but the line is often the weakest link in the whole system. The good news is that fixing it is one of the cheapest jobs in oil heat. The trick is knowing whether your line needs a protective sleeve, a coating, or a full replacement.

Here's how we walk homeowners through that decision every week, from Salisbury out to Sackville.

## Why Copper Oil Lines Corrode in Greater Moncton Homes

Copper doesn't rust the way a steel tank does, but it corrodes, and it corrodes fastest exactly where you can't see it.

The worst offender is direct contact with concrete. A lot of homes built from the 1960s through the 1990s have a bare copper line buried in or under the basement slab, running from the tank in the corner to the furnace across the room. Concrete is alkaline, it holds moisture, and over decades it eats away at bare copper. The result is pitting and pinholes on the underside of the line, completely out of sight.

A few other things speed up the damage:

- **Moisture trapped against the line.** A line pinched under carpet, subfloor, or a workbench leg sits in condensation for years.
- **Kinks and dents.** Copper work-hardens. Every time a line gets stepped on or bent, it gets more brittle at that spot.
- **Salt air.** Homes near the water in Shediac, Pointe-du-Chene, and Cap-Pele see faster corrosion on any exposed metal, oil lines included.
- **Vibration.** A line resting directly on the burner or the floor picks up vibration from the furnace and slowly wears at contact points.

None of this happens overnight. A copper line can run 30 or 40 years without trouble. But when one fails, it fails silently, and cleanup costs make a new line look like pocket change.

## What Sleeving Actually Does (And What It Doesn't)

Sleeving means running the copper line inside a continuous protective jacket, usually a plastic sheath, so the copper never touches concrete, earth, or standing moisture. Modern oil lines come pre-coated from the factory with a bonded plastic layer that does the same job.

The current CSA B139 installation code, which New Brunswick follows for oil-burning equipment, requires that oil lines in contact with concrete or soil be protected. That's why every insurer and every inspector flags bare copper the moment they see it disappear into a floor.

Here's the part that surprises people. Sleeving is a great way to protect a line, but it's mostly a day-one decision. You sleeve a line when it's installed.

Retrofitting a sleeve onto an existing line only works where you can actually get at the copper. You can slide protection over an exposed run along a wall. You cannot sleeve the 20 feet that's already buried in your slab without breaking up the floor, and at that point you're better off abandoning that run entirely.

So the honest version of "sleeve or replace" is really this: exposed line in good condition, protect it. Buried line, or damaged line, replace it, and route the new one where you can see it.

## When Sleeving or Coating Is Enough

Plenty of oil lines don't need replacing. If your line checks all of these boxes, protection is usually the right call:

- The full run is visible, from tank fitting to burner, with nothing buried or hidden.
- The copper is smooth and uniform in colour, with no green or white crust, no pitting, and no flattened or kinked sections.
- The line is off the floor, supported along the run, and not resting against concrete anywhere.
- There's no history of oil odour, wet spots, or unexplained oil loss.

In that case, adding a sleeve where the line passes near concrete, securing it properly, and fitting an oil safety valve is cheap insurance.

That's what we did for Joanne in Shediac last fall. Her cottage had an outdoor tank with an exposed line running along the foundation, and the salt air had her worried. The copper itself tested sound, so instead of a full replacement she paid $380 to have the run sleeved, re-secured on proper clips, and fitted with a new filter and oil safety valve. Her insurer accepted it without a fuss.

One caution: sleeving a line that's already corroded is like painting over rust. If the copper shows pitting, the protection went on about 20 years too late. At that point the sleeve just hides the problem.

## When Oil Line Replacement Is the Only Safe Call

Some situations take sleeving off the table completely. Replace the line when:

- **Any part of the run is under or inside the concrete slab.** You can't inspect it, you can't protect it, and it's the single most common leak point we see. The fix is to abandon the buried run and reroute a new coated line overhead, along the joists, where it stays visible for life.
- **You see green or white corrosion, pitting, or weeping at any point.** Corrosion on the outside means the wall of the tube is already thinner than it should be.
- **The line is kinked, flattened, or spliced.** Old flare joints and compression fittings in the middle of a run are leaks waiting for a cold snap.
- **Your furnace is acting starved.** Sputtering, lockouts, or a burner that needs constant bleeding can mean the line is drawing air through a pinhole. Our post on a [furnace not getting oil](/blog/furnace-not-getting-oil/) covers how to tell the difference.
- **Your insurer or home inspector flagged it.** In our experience insurers around Moncton don't negotiate on buried bare copper. Sleeving what you can reach won't satisfy a letter that names the buried section.

## How the Job Gets Done (And Where DIY Should Stop)

A typical oil line replacement takes two to four hours. Here's the shape of it:

1. Shut the tank valve and protect the work area.
2. Disconnect and cap the old line at both ends. A buried run gets capped and abandoned in place, clearly marked.
3. Run new factory-coated copper from the tank, up and along the joists, down to the burner. No joints mid-run, proper clips every few feet.
4. Install an oil safety valve at the tank and a new filter, then flare the connections.
5. Bleed the line, fire the furnace, and leak-test every fitting under vacuum and under load.

There's real DIY territory here. Checking your visible line for green crust, keeping stored items from resting on it, and watching for wet spots costs nothing and catches most problems early. If you want to know what you're looking at down there, take five minutes with a flashlight and follow the line end to end.

But cutting, flaring, and bleeding a fuel line is licensed work, and for good reason. A bad flare weeps oil onto your floor. A line bled wrong can airlock the pump or spray oil under pressure.

There's a bigger pattern behind that caution. In the US, the EPA's [underground storage tank program](https://www.epa.gov/ust) exists partly because piping failures, not tanks, cause a huge share of fuel releases. The same lesson applies to the little residential line in your basement: piping is where the leaks happen, so it's not the place to guess.

The line is also the natural time to look hard at the tank it's attached to. If your tank is 20-plus years old, it may make more sense to do [tank and line together](/oil-tank-replacement-moncton/) in one visit than to bolt a new line onto a tank that's next in line to fail.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do copper oil lines last?

An exposed, properly supported copper line can easily last 30 to 40 years. A bare line in contact with concrete or soil is a different story, and can pinhole in 15 to 25 years depending on moisture. Age matters less than routing: visible and protected lasts, buried and bare fails.

### Can I sleeve an oil line that runs under my concrete floor?

Not in any meaningful way. You can't get protection onto copper that's already encased in or under a slab without breaking up the floor. The standard fix is to cap and abandon the buried run, then route a new factory-coated line overhead along the joists where it stays visible and inspectable.

### Will my insurance company require oil line replacement?

Often, yes. Insurers in New Brunswick routinely flag bare copper lines, especially buried ones, during renewals and new policies. Most accept an overhead reroute with coated line and an oil safety valve as a complete fix, and we provide photos and a written description your broker can file.
